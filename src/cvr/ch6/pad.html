1Definition

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is narrowing or blockage of the arteries supplying the limbs, usually the legs, so the muscles and skin get too little blood. The cause is almost always atherosclerosis.

Intermittent claudication is cramping pain in a muscle group, usually the calf, that comes on with walking a fairly fixed distance and goes away within a few minutes of rest. It is the main symptom of PAD.

Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans) is an inflammatory, non-atherosclerotic disease of the small and medium arteries and veins of the hands and feet. It affects young smokers.

2Classification and grading

Clinical presentation

  • Asymptomatic PAD: found only on testing.
  • Chronic symptomatic PAD: intermittent claudication.
  • Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI): rest pain, non-healing ulcer or gangrene.
  • Acute limb ischaemia: sudden loss of blood supply from an embolus or thrombosis. This is a surgical emergency.

Other arterial disorders in the syllabus

  • Buerger's disease (sections 3–5 below).
  • Raynaud's phenomenon: fingers turn white, then blue, then red on exposure to cold or stress, because small arteries spasm. Primary (no cause) or secondary (for example to scleroderma).
  • Acute limb ischaemia: embolus (often from atrial fibrillation) or thrombosis on an existing plaque.
  • Aneurysm: a balloon-like widening of an artery, most often the abdominal aorta or popliteal artery. It can rupture or throw clots.

3Causes and risk factors

Atherosclerotic PAD

  • Smoking (cigarettes and bidi): the strongest risk factor.
  • Diabetes: disease is more distal (below the knee) and often combined with neuropathy.
  • Age over 50–65, hypertension, abnormal lipids, chronic kidney disease, family history.

Buerger's disease: diagnostic criteria

Shionoya (1998)Olin (2000)
  • Smoking history
  • Onset before age 50
  • Blocks in the arteries below the knee
  • Arm involvement or migrating superficial phlebitis
  • No atherosclerotic risk factors other than smoking
  • Age under 45
  • Current or recent tobacco use
  • Distal limb ischaemia (claudication, rest pain, ulcers or gangrene) confirmed by tests
  • Autoimmune disease, clotting disorders and diabetes ruled out
  • No source of emboli; typical angiogram

4Pathophysiology to impairments

For "impairments with clinical reasoning" (W22), explain why walking hurts and why exercise helps.

  1. Atherosclerotic plaque narrows the artery. At rest, blood flow through the narrowing is still enough.
  2. Walking raises the calf muscles' oxygen demand, but the narrowed artery cannot raise its supply.
  3. The muscle becomes ischaemic, produces lactic acid and other metabolites, and pain starts: claudication. Resting lowers the demand, so the pain goes.
  4. The patient walks less, so muscles weaken and fitness falls. The distance he can walk shrinks further.
  5. When the narrowing becomes severe, even resting flow is not enough: rest pain, then skin breakdown, ulcers and gangrene.

Where the block is decides where the pain is: aorto-iliac disease gives buttock and thigh pain; femoro-popliteal disease (the commonest site, and the S19 popliteal case) gives calf pain; tibial disease gives foot pain.

Why does walking exercise help if it causes pain?

Repeated walking to moderate pain improves the muscle's ability to use oxygen, opens up collateral vessels around the block, improves the lining of the arteries, and makes walking more efficient. The block itself does not go away, but the patient walks much further before pain starts.

Why is rest pain worse at night and better with the leg hanging down?

Lying flat removes the help of gravity, and the heart pumps a little less during sleep. Hanging the leg down uses gravity to push more blood into the foot.

5Clinical features

Symptoms

  • Calf, thigh or buttock cramping pain on walking, relieved by standing still within a few minutes. The W22 head clerk gets pain after 5 minutes and stops at 10 minutes.
  • Rest pain in the toes and forefoot at night, relieved by hanging the leg over the bed.
  • Cold feet, numbness, slow-healing wounds.

Signs

  • Cool, pale skin; thin, shiny, hairless skin; thick, brittle nails; wasted calf (W22: "legs appear thin, skin dry").
  • Weak or absent pulses below the block.
  • Pallor when the leg is raised and dusky red colour (dependent rubor) when it hangs down.
  • Delayed capillary refill; ulcers on the toes, heel or pressure points; gangrene.

Buerger's disease: extra features

  • Young male smoker; starts in the feet and often the hands too.
  • Foot (instep) claudication, cold sensitive fingers and toes, Raynaud's-like colour changes.
  • Migrating red tender lines along superficial veins (superficial thrombophlebitis).
  • Painful ulcers and gangrene of the fingertips and toes.
Acute limb ischaemia: the 6 P's (emergency)

Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paraesthesia, Paralysis, Perishing cold. Stop and call the doctor at once.

6Investigations and special tests

"Three special tests to assess arterial insufficiency" was asked three times (W16, S18, W20). ABI was asked three more times.

Ankle–brachial index: how to do it

  1. Patient lies flat and rests for 5–10 minutes.
  2. Measure systolic pressure in both arms with a BP cuff and hand-held Doppler. Take the higher one.
  3. Measure systolic pressure at the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries of each ankle, cuff just above the ankle. Take the higher of the two for each leg.
  4. ABI = ankle systolic pressure ÷ higher arm systolic pressure, for each leg.
  5. Interpret with the table in section 2.

Other investigations

  • Doppler ultrasound (duplex): shows the site and degree of the block.
  • Toe–brachial index when the ABI is over 1.40.
  • CT or MR angiography and conventional angiography before angioplasty or bypass.
  • Blood tests: blood sugar and HbA1c, lipids, kidney function.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Stop smoking completely. For Buerger's disease this is the only treatment proven to halt the disease; even a few cigarettes or bidis a day keep it active.
  • Antiplatelet drug, statin, BP and diabetes control to cut the high risk of heart attack and stroke.
  • Cilostazol can reduce claudication symptoms.
  • Supervised exercise therapy is first-line treatment for claudication (section 12).
  • Revascularisation when claudication still limits life after exercise therapy, or for limb-threatening ischaemia: angioplasty with or without a stent, or a bypass graft (for example femoro-popliteal bypass).
  • Wound care, infection control, and amputation if the limb cannot be saved (see the amputation page).
  • Acute limb ischaemia: urgent anticoagulation and removal of the clot (embolectomy) or bypass.

9PT assessment

S21 asked to "enumerate evaluation parameters for peripheral arterial disorders". This list is the answer.

  • History: claudication site, onset distance or time, time to stop, recovery time; rest pain; smoking (pack-years, bidi); diabetes, HTN, heart disease; occupation, walking demands, stairs at home.
  • Inspection: colour, hair loss, nails, muscle wasting, ulcers and gangrene (site, size, base, edge), oedema.
  • Palpation: skin temperature (compare sides), pulses with grades.
  • Special tests: ABI, Buerger's test, capillary refill, venous filling time.
  • Sensation: 10 g monofilament if diabetic.
  • ROM and strength: ankle dorsiflexion and calf strength; joint stiffness from inactivity.
  • Walking capacity: treadmill test or 6-minute walk test with pain-free and maximum distance; claudication pain scale.
  • Questionnaires: Walking Impairment Questionnaire; quality of life (VascuQoL).

12PT management

1. Supervised exercise therapy (the core treatment)

Memory hook: the walking prescription

30 / 3 / 12

  • 30–45 minutes of walking per session (build up to it)
  • 3 sessions or more a week
  • 12 weeks at least

Checked against the AHA exercise recommendations and the 2023 ESC-led consensus. The 2024 ACC/AHA guideline gives supervised exercise Class 1, Level A.

Interval walking programme
PartDose
Warm-up5–10 min slow walking and calf stretches
Walking boutsTreadmill or level ground. Walk until claudication pain reaches moderate to strong (about 2–3 on the 4-point claudication scale), then stop.
RestStand or sit until the pain has fully gone, then walk again
TotalRepeat walk–rest cycles to reach 30–45 min of exercise time, ≥3 times a week, for ≥12 weeks
ProgressionWhen he can walk about 8–10 minutes before moderate pain, raise the treadmill speed or slope

Claudication pain scale: 1 = pain starts, mild; 2 = moderate; 3 = intense; 4 = unbearable. Write "walk to moderate pain (grade 2–3), rest until it goes, repeat" in the exam.

  • If walking is not possible (foot ulcer, arthritis): arm cranking or cycling also improve walking ability (2024 guideline, Class 2a).
  • If supervision is not available: a structured home walking programme with a log book, at least 30 minutes, 3–5 times a week.
  • Add: calf and thigh strengthening (heel raises, sit-to-stand), and stretching of the calf muscles.

2. Buerger's disease (7 marks in S17 and S22)

  • Absolute stop of all tobacco, including bidi, chewing tobacco and passive smoke.
  • Walking programme as above, within tolerance; foot (instep) muscle and calf exercises.
  • Buerger–Allen exercises (classic, below) and position changes to aid circulation.
  • Keep hands and feet warm with socks and gloves; avoid cold exposure, but never apply direct heat.
  • Meticulous foot and hand care; protect ulcers; manage pain with positioning (leg slightly down at night).
  • After amputation of digits or limb: stump care and prosthetic training.

Classic answer vs current evidence: Buerger–Allen exercises

Classic

  1. Lying, raise the leg to about 45–90° until the skin blanches (about 1–2 minutes).
  2. Sit with the leg hanging down until it turns red (about 2–5 minutes), with ankle movements.
  3. Lie flat with the leg level for about 5 minutes.
  4. Repeat the cycle several times, a few times a day.

Current

There is little good evidence for Buerger–Allen exercises. Supervised walking to moderate claudication pain has the strongest evidence (Class 1, Level A) and should be the main treatment whenever the patient can walk.

Write in the exam

"Supervised interval walking 30–45 min, ≥3 times a week, ≥12 weeks, is the main treatment; Buerger–Allen exercises may be added."

3. Rest pain, ulcers and limb-threatening ischaemia

  • Sleep with the head of the bed slightly raised or the leg slightly lower; do not elevate an ischaemic leg.
  • Protect heels with padding; turn regularly.
  • Gentle active ankle and toe exercises within comfort; no walking on an open ulcer without offloading footwear.
  • After angioplasty or bypass: early walking as the surgeon allows; protect groin wounds; then a walking programme.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • No heat (hot packs, shortwave, hot water) on an ischaemic limb: blood flow cannot carry the heat away and the skin burns.
  • No compression bandaging if the ABI is low; check ABI before any compression.
  • Do not elevate the leg in rest pain.
  • Most patients have heart disease too: check HR and BP, and watch for chest pain during walking.
  • Diabetics: check blood sugar and feet before and after exercise.
  • Do not walk on an open foot ulcer without offloading.
Stop and refer urgently if
  • Any of the 6 P's appear suddenly (acute limb ischaemia)
  • Chest pain, breathlessness or palpitations during exercise
  • New rest pain, a new ulcer, or the foot turns black

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

Foot care

  • Look at your feet every day, including the soles and between the toes. Use a mirror or ask a family member.
  • Wash in lukewarm water, test the temperature with your elbow, dry well between the toes.
  • Cut nails straight across. Do not cut corns yourself.
  • Always wear soft, well-fitting footwear and socks. Never walk barefoot, even at home or in temples.
  • No hot-water bottles, heating pads or fire near the feet.
  • Report any colour change, wound or pain at rest at once.

Lifestyle

  • Stop all tobacco: this is the most important single step.
  • Keep walking every day using the walk–rest method.
  • Heart-healthy diet: less fried and fatty food, less salt; control weight.
  • Take medicines regularly: antiplatelet, statin, BP and diabetes drugs.
  • Avoid cold; keep hands and feet warm with socks and gloves.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

Prognosis: most people with claudication do not lose their leg, but they have a high risk of heart attack and stroke. Walking distance improves greatly with supervised exercise. Continued smoking is the strongest predictor of worsening disease and, in Buerger's disease, of amputation.

Discharge from the supervised programme: walks independently with the walk–rest method; can measure progress with a walking log; does daily foot care; has stopped or is actively stopping smoking; knows the warning signs.

  1. ABI: ankle systolic ÷ higher arm systolic; ≤ 0.90 indicates PAD.
  2. Buerger's test: pallor on raising the leg at a low angle, then dependent rubor on hanging it down.

3 mAnkle–brachial index.

Ratio of ankle to arm systolic pressure, measured with a Doppler after 5–10 minutes' rest. Normal 1.00–1.40; borderline 0.91–0.99; PAD ≤ 0.90 (severe below 0.40); over 1.40 means calcified arteries, so use the toe–brachial index.

Define (inflammatory disease of small and medium vessels, young smokers). Aims. MCQA 55-year-old man's ABI is 1.6. What is the best next step?A. Reassure: normal arteriesB. Measure the toe–brachial indexC. Start compression bandagingD. Diagnose severe PAD

B. An ABI above 1.40 means the ankle arteries are stiff and cannot be compressed, so the result is unreliable. The toe–brachial index (0.70 or less = PAD) is used instead.

MCQDuring supervised walking for claudication, the patient should:A. Stop at the first hint of painB. Walk until moderate-to-strong pain, rest until it goes, then repeatC. Walk through unbearable painD. Avoid walking and use only Buerger–Allen exercises

B. Training to moderate claudication pain gives the best gains. Unbearable pain is unnecessary, and stopping at the first hint gives too little stimulus.

MCQWhich is a contraindication in a patient with severe PAD?A. Calf strengtheningB. A hot pack on the calfC. Daily foot inspectionD. Arm ergometry

B. Ischaemic tissue cannot get rid of heat, so it burns easily.
