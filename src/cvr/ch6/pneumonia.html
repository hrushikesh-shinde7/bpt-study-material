1Pneumonia

Definition and types

Infection of the lung tissue in which the alveoli fill with inflammatory fluid and cells (consolidation).

  • By setting: community-acquired; hospital-acquired (starting 48 hours or more after admission); ventilator-associated; aspiration pneumonia.
  • By pattern: lobar (one lobe), bronchopneumonia (patchy, both lungs), interstitial (viral, atypical).
  • By organism: bacterial (pneumococcus commonest), viral, atypical, fungal, TB.

Key features

  • Fever, cough, sputum (rusty in pneumococcal), pleuritic chest pain, breathlessness, fast breathing; confusion in the elderly.
  • Signs over the consolidation: dull percussion, bronchial breathing, crackles, increased vocal resonance.
  • X-ray: white consolidation with air bronchograms (dark airways inside white lung) and no loss of volume.
Severity score: CURB-65 (one point each)

C U R B 65

  • Confusion (new)
  • Urea above 7 mmol/L (about 42 mg/dL as reported in Indian labs)
  • Respiratory rate 30 or more
  • Blood pressure: systolic below 90 or diastolic 60 or less
  • Age 65 or more

0–1 low risk (often treated at home); 2 moderate (hospital); 3–5 severe (consider ICU).

Medical point that matters for physio

Antibiotics, oxygen (target SpO₂ 94–98%, or 88–92% if at risk of CO₂ retention), fluids. Early consolidation is solid exudate, not loose sputum, so percussion and drainage do not clear it.

Classic answer vs current guideline: chest physiotherapy in pneumonia

Classic

Postural drainage, percussion and vibrations for every pneumonia patient.

Current (BTS/ACPRC 2009; Cochrane 2022)

Airway clearance is not routine in uncomplicated community-acquired pneumonia; evidence for benefit is of very low certainty. Patients should sit out of bed for at least 20 minutes within the first 24 hours and mobilise progressively. Treat sputum retention, weakness and underlying lung disease when present.

Write in the exam

"Early mobilisation and positioning for all; airway clearance (ACBT) only when sputum is retained or in pre-existing lung disease."

ICF summary

Impairments: fever, breathlessness, low oxygen, pleuritic pain, sputum (later), weakness. Activity: walking, self-care. Participation: work and family roles during illness. Environment: hospital or home care.

PT management by problem

  • Low oxygen: positioning; in one-sided pneumonia, lie with the good lung down to improve oxygenation.
  • Deconditioning: sit out within 24 hours (20 minutes or more), then progressive walking.
  • Sputum retention (resolving stage): ACBT with huffing, 1–2 times a day; affected lung uppermost for drainage when tolerated; humidification.
  • Pleuritic pain: supported breathing and coughing; pain relief before treatment.
  • On a ventilator (S20, W24): see the worked plan below.

Ventilated pneumonia (W24: 35-year-old, both lungs, CMV, FiO₂ 0.6, PEEP 10 for 3 days)

Read the settings first. FiO₂ 0.6 and PEEP 10 cmH₂O are high: his lungs are stiff and poorly oxygenated, close to ARDS. Losing PEEP even briefly (disconnecting the circuit) can collapse alveoli and drop his SpO₂.

  • Safety check every session: SpO₂, FiO₂ and PEEP trend, HR, BP, sedation level, and the doctor's plan. At FiO₂ 0.6 and PEEP 10 he is at the upper limit for active mobilisation, so start with in-bed treatment.
  • Positioning: head of bed up 30–45° (prevents aspiration and ventilator pneumonia); regular side-lying turns to change ventilation and drain secretions; prone positioning for severe hypoxaemia is decided by the doctors, and physiotherapists help to turn the patient safely.
  • Secretions: atypical pneumonia often produces little sputum, so suction only when needed, using a closed (in-line) suction system to keep PEEP. Avoid bagging off the ventilator at this PEEP; if hyperinflation is needed, the ventilator can deliver it (ventilator hyperinflation).
  • Limbs: passive then active-assisted movement of all joints, ankle splints or positioning against foot drop, pressure care with regular turns.
  • Progress as oxygen needs fall and sedation lightens: active exercise, sitting over the edge of the bed, in-bed cycling, then standing and walking with the ventilator.
  • After extubation: deep breathing, ACBT, incentive spirometry as an add-on, early walking, strengthening and inspiratory muscle training if weak.

Ventilator modes, weaning criteria, suction and positioning are covered in more detail on the ICU technique cards.

Precautions

  • Watch for sepsis (low BP, confusion), worsening hypoxia and fatigue.
  • Infection control: hand hygiene, masks for viral pneumonia.

Exam points

  • Consolidation vs collapse: consolidation keeps lung volume; collapse loses it.
  • Physio is not routine in uncomplicated pneumonia.

2Lung abscess

Definition and types

A localised area of lung necrosis forming a cavity filled with pus.

Causes and key features

  • Aspiration of mouth contents (alcohol, seizures, reduced consciousness, poor teeth, swallowing problems). Commonest sites: posterior segment of the right upper lobe and superior segment of the right lower lobe (the lowest parts when lying on the back).
  • Other causes: severe pneumonia (Staphylococcus, Klebsiella), blocked bronchus (tumour, foreign body).
  • Swinging fever, large amounts of foul-smelling sputum, haemoptysis, weight loss, clubbing.
  • X-ray: a thick-walled cavity with an air–fluid level.

Medical point that matters for physio

Several weeks of antibiotics; drainage or surgery if it does not resolve. Once the abscess drains into a bronchus, sputum is copious.

ICF summary

Impairments: copious foul sputum, fever, fatigue, weight loss. Activity and participation: reduced work and social life (embarrassment from sputum smell).

PT management by problem

  • Copious sputum: gravity-assisted drainage of the affected segment with ACBT, 2–3 times a day, once the abscess communicates with a bronchus (the patient is coughing it up).
  • Encourage strong huffs and coughs during drainage so pus is cleared, not spilled into other lobes.
  • Breathing exercises, mobilisation, nutrition and mouth hygiene advice.

Precautions

  • Haemoptysis: stop drainage and percussion.
  • Weak or drowsy patients may not clear a sudden large release of pus: have suction ready.

Exam points

  • Cavity with an air–fluid level; foul sputum; aspiration risk factors; drain the affected segment.

3Atelectasis (collapse)

Definition and types

Incomplete expansion or collapse of part or all of a lung.

  • Obstructive (resorption): mucus plug, tumour or foreign body; air beyond is absorbed.
  • Compressive: effusion, pneumothorax, tumour pressing on the lung.
  • Passive (micro-atelectasis): shallow breathing after surgery or with pain.
  • Adhesive: surfactant loss (neonatal RDS, ARDS). Cicatrisation: scarring (TB, fibrosis).

Key features

  • May be silent when small; larger collapse: breathlessness, low SpO₂, reduced breath sounds.
  • X-ray: increased opacity with loss of volume: trachea and mediastinum pulled towards the collapse, raised hemidiaphragm, displaced fissures, crowded ribs.

Medical point that matters for physio

Post-operative and mucus-plug collapse respond well to physiotherapy; bronchoscopy for a plug that will not clear.

ICF summary

Impairments: reduced lung volume, low oxygen, retained secretions. Activity: mobility limited by breathlessness and the underlying cause.

PT management by problem

  • Reduced lung volume: deep breathing with a 3-second inspiratory hold (sustained maximal inspiration), thoracic expansion to the affected area, 5–10 breaths every hour while awake.
  • Positioning: affected area uppermost to ventilate and drain it.
  • Mucus plug: humidification, ACBT with huffs, suction if needed.
  • Early mobilisation: the best "deep breathing" exercise.
  • In ventilated or very weak patients: positive pressure (CPAP, manual hyperinflation, IPPB) as prescribed; incentive spirometry only as an add-on.

Precautions

  • Positive pressure is contraindicated with an undrained pneumothorax.
  • Compressive collapse needs the fluid or air drained first.

Exam points

  • Collapse pulls structures towards; effusion and pneumothorax push away; consolidation keeps volume.

MCQDay 1 of uncomplicated community-acquired pneumonia, no sputum. Best physiotherapy:A. Percussion and postural drainageB. Sit out of bed for at least 20 minutes and progress mobilityC. Bed rest for 3 daysD. IPPB

B. Early mobilisation is recommended; routine airway clearance is not.

MCQAn X-ray shows a white right lower zone, raised right hemidiaphragm and trachea shifted right. This is most likely:A. Right pleural effusionB. Right lower lobe collapseC. Right tension pneumothoraxD. Normal

B. Volume loss pulls the trachea towards the collapse.
