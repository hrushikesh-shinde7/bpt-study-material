1Definition

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders with a raised blood glucose, caused by lack of insulin, poor response to insulin, or both.

Diabetic foot is infection, ulceration or destruction of tissues of the foot in a person with diabetes, usually because of nerve damage (neuropathy) and/or peripheral arterial disease.

Diabetes insipidus (now called arginine vasopressin (AVP) deficiency or AVP resistance) is a different disease: the body cannot concentrate urine, so the person passes large volumes of dilute urine and is always thirsty. Blood sugar is normal.

2Classification and grading

Types of diabetes mellitus

  • Type 1: autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing cells; usually young; needs insulin; risk of ketoacidosis.
  • Type 2: insulin resistance with failing insulin secretion; linked to obesity, inactivity and family history; the commonest type, and common in younger Indians.
  • Gestational diabetes in pregnancy; other specific types (pancreatic disease, steroids).

Foot risk categories (IWGDF)

  • 0, very low: no loss of protective sensation and no PAD. Screen yearly.
  • 1, low: loss of protective sensation or PAD. Screen every 6–12 months.
  • 2, moderate: two of: loss of sensation, PAD, foot deformity. Screen every 3–6 months.
  • 3, high: loss of sensation or PAD plus a past ulcer, past amputation or end-stage kidney disease. Screen every 1–3 months.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Type 2: obesity, especially abdominal; physical inactivity; family history; South Asian ethnicity (diabetes at lower BMI and younger age); unhealthy diet; age; gestational diabetes in the past.
  • Diabetic foot: long duration, poor sugar control, neuropathy, PAD, foot deformity, smoking, kidney disease, walking barefoot, poor footwear, previous ulcer.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

Why the diabetic foot ulcerates: three causes

  • Sensory neuropathy: loss of protective feeling, so injuries, tight shoes and burns are not noticed.
  • Motor neuropathy: weak small foot muscles cause claw toes and prominent metatarsal heads, so pressure under the forefoot rises and callus forms.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: no sweating, so dry, cracked skin lets bacteria in.
  • Ischaemia (PAD) slows healing; high sugar weakens immunity, so infection spreads fast.

Repeated pressure under a callus causes bleeding and breakdown beneath it: a neuropathic ulcer. Add infection and ischaemia, and the result can be gangrene and amputation.

Other complications that affect physiotherapy

  • Heart and vessels: heart attack (often silent), stroke, PAD.
  • Eyes: retinopathy (bleeding risk with straining and head-down positions).
  • Kidneys: nephropathy.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: postural hypotension, fixed heart rate, poor heat tolerance.
  • Musculoskeletal: frozen shoulder, stiff hands, Charcot foot (collapse of foot joints).

Stages of wound healing (3 marks in S19)

Haemostasis (minutes to hours) → inflammation (about days 1–5) → proliferation (about day 4 to week 3) → remodelling (week 3 to 1–2 years). See the full table on the burns page. Diabetes slows every stage, especially inflammation and proliferation.

Factors affecting wound healing (7 marks in S25)

  • Local: infection; poor blood supply (arterial disease, pressure, tight dressings); oedema; dead tissue or foreign bodies; repeated shear, friction or pressure; a wound that is too dry or too wet; a large or deep wound; previous radiotherapy.
  • General: older age; poor nutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc); diabetes with high sugar; smoking; anaemia and low oxygen; obesity; drugs (steroids, chemotherapy); kidney or liver failure; reduced immunity; stress and poor sleep.

In a diabetic foot the main ones are high sugar, ischaemia, unnoticed pressure from neuropathy, infection and smoking. Answer S25 with this list plus the modalities in section 12.

5Clinical features

  • Diabetes: thirst, frequent urination, tiredness, weight loss, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds, repeated infections; many type 2 patients have no symptoms.
  • Neuropathic foot: numbness, tingling or burning, warm dry skin, callus, claw toes, good pulses (unless PAD), painless ulcer under the forefoot or heel.
  • Ischaemic foot: cold, pale, absent pulses, painful ulcers at toes and heel.
  • Diabetes insipidus: passing large amounts of pale urine (more than about 3 litres a day in adults), waking at night to pass urine, strong thirst, dehydration if water is not available.
Hypoglycaemia (blood sugar below 70 mg/dL)

Sweating, shaking, hunger, fast heart rate, confusion, slurred speech, unusual behaviour, seizures. Can happen during or many hours after exercise, especially with insulin or sulfonylureas.

7Medical management

  • Lifestyle: diet, physical activity, weight loss, stopping smoking.
  • Medicines: metformin; sulfonylureas (cause hypoglycaemia); DPP-4 inhibitors; SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists (also protect the heart and kidneys); insulin.
  • Usual target HbA1c around 7% for many adults, set individually.
  • Control BP and lipids; yearly eye, kidney and foot checks.
  • Diabetic foot ulcer: off-loading (for example total contact cast), debridement, infection control, revascularisation if ischaemic, good sugar control.
  • Diabetes insipidus: treat the cause; desmopressin for AVP deficiency.

9PT assessment

  • Type and duration of diabetes, medicines (insulin, sulfonylureas), recent HbA1c and readings, hypoglycaemia episodes, complications.
  • Activity level, occupation, diet habits, smoking, weight, BMI and waist.
  • Feet: skin, nails, callus, deformity, footwear, monofilament sensation, vibration, pulses, ulcer description (site, size, depth, Wagner grade).
  • BP lying and standing; resting HR; fitness (6MWT); strength; balance (single-leg stance, Timed Up and Go).
  • Outcome measures: HbA1c, weight and waist, 6MWT, ulcer area, balance tests, quality of life.

11Problem list and goals

  • Short-term (4 weeks): daily foot inspection habit; protective footwear always; walking 20 minutes on most days with safe sugar readings; knows hypoglycaemia signs and treatment.
  • Long-term (3–6 months): 150 minutes a week of aerobic activity plus resistance training twice a week; HbA1c and weight reduced; no ulcers; better balance.

12PT management

Exercise for glycaemic control (7 marks in S16 and W18)

Why exercise works: contracting muscle takes up glucose even without insulin; after training, insulin sensitivity improves for a day or more; exercise also reduces weight, BP, lipids and heart risk.

ADA prescription for adults with type 2 diabetes
TypeDose
AerobicAt least 150 min a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity, spread over 3–7 days, no more than 2 days in a row without exercise
Resistance2–3 sessions a week on non-consecutive days; 1–3 sets of 10–15 repetitions to near fatigue at first

Memory hook: hypoglycaemia "rule of 15"

15 g, then 15 minutes

If sugar is below 70 mg/dL or symptoms appear: give 15 g of fast sugar (glucose tablets, 3 teaspoons of sugar in water, half a glass of juice), recheck after 15 minutes, repeat if still low, then a snack.

Exact parameters differ between electrotherapy books and trials. Write a range with the effect, and never use heat or electrotherapy over an ischaemic or numb area without checking circulation.

Pressure sores (pressure injuries) (7 marks in W19)

Damage to skin and the tissue under it from pressure, or pressure with shear, usually over a bony point: sacrum, heels, hips (greater trochanter), ischial tuberosities, back of the head, elbows. Patients at risk: bed- or chair-bound, numb skin (diabetes, spinal cord injury), ICU patients, poor nutrition, incontinence, older age.

Stages (NPIAP, 2016)
StageWhat you see
Stage 1Intact skin with redness that does not fade (blanch) when pressed
Stage 2Partial-thickness skin loss: shallow pink wound or blister; fat not visible
Stage 3Full-thickness skin loss: fat visible; no bone, tendon or muscle seen
Stage 4Full-thickness skin and tissue loss: muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage or bone exposed or felt
UnstageableBase covered by slough or eschar, so depth cannot be seen
Deep tissue pressure injuryIntact or broken skin with a persistent deep red, maroon or purple area

Risk assessment: Braden scale (sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, friction and shear; total 6–23). A score of 18 or less means the patient is at risk; the lower the score, the higher the risk.

Physiotherapy

  • Relieve pressure: reposition on a schedule set for that patient; 30° tilted side-lying rather than lying straight on the hip; head of bed no higher than 30° unless breathing needs more; heels floated off the bed with a pillow under the calves.
  • Surfaces: high-specification foam or alternating-pressure air mattress; pressure-relieving cushion in the chair; teach chair users to shift weight (push-ups or leaning) regularly.
  • Move: bed mobility, sitting out and walking as early as possible; active and passive exercise to keep circulation and joint range.
  • Avoid shear: lift, do not drag; use slide sheets.
  • Help the wound heal: electrical stimulation has the best evidence of the physical modalities for stage 2 sores that are slow to heal and for stage 3–4 sores (parameters as in the table above); wound cleaning and dressing by the team; nutrition and continence care.
  • Teach: daily skin checks, weight shifts, never sit or lie on a red area.

Classic answer vs current guideline: turning

Classic

Turn every patient every 2 hours.

Current (international guideline, 2019)

Set the turning frequency for each patient from their risk, skin checks, comfort and the mattress they are on.

Write in the exam

"Reposition at least every 2 hours, or as the patient's skin and support surface require."

Diabetes insipidus

  • Allow free access to water and toilets during sessions; watch for dehydration (dizziness, dry mouth, fast pulse).
  • Night-time urination raises the fall risk in older patients: night light, clear path.
  • Seen in ICU after head injury or pituitary surgery: monitor fluid balance before mobilising.

13Precautions

Exercise precautions in diabetes
  • Check sugar before and after exercise if on insulin or sulfonylureas; carry fast sugar; avoid exercising at the peak action of insulin.
  • Treat a low sugar first and do not start until it is back to normal.
  • Avoid exercise when sugar is very high with ketones (mainly type 1).
  • Advanced retinopathy: avoid vigorous exercise, heavy lifting, jumping, head-down positions and breath-holding.
  • Neuropathy: proper footwear; check feet after each session; balance training.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: change position slowly; use RPE instead of heart rate; avoid hot, humid days; drink water.
  • Heart disease is often silent: report unusual breathlessness or fatigue.
  • No heat or ice on numb skin; check circulation before any electrotherapy.

14Education and lifestyle

  • Be active every day; break up sitting every 30 minutes.
  • Eat regular meals with fewer refined carbohydrates and sweets; more vegetables, pulses and fibre.
  • Reach a healthy weight and waist (Indian targets: BMI below 23; waist below 90 cm in men, 80 cm in women).
  • Take medicines regularly; know your hypoglycaemia plan.
  • Daily foot care for life; yearly eye, kidney and foot checks.
  • Stop smoking and tobacco.

15Prognosis

Good control of sugar, BP and lipids, regular exercise and daily foot care greatly reduce complications. Most diabetic amputations start with a foot ulcer that could have been prevented.
