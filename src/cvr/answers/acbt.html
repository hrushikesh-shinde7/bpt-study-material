Huffing and coughing: techniques and differences

Huff: a forced expiration with an open glottis. Take a medium breath in, then breathe out fast but not violently through an "O" mouth, as if steaming up a mirror.

Cough: a deep breath in, the glottis closes while the expiratory muscles build pressure, then it opens suddenly and air bursts out.

Forced expiration technique

FET is one or two huffs followed by a period of breathing control. It is the clearing part of the active cycle of breathing technique.

Technique: take a medium breath in, then huff out through an open mouth and glottis, tightening the abdominal muscles. Do 1–2 huffs from mid to low lung volume, then breathing control. When mucus reaches the large airways, clear it with a high-volume huff or one cough.

Principle (equal pressure point): in a forced expiration, airway pressure falls from the alveoli to the mouth. Where it equals pleural pressure, the airway beyond is squeezed and air speeds up, shearing mucus towards the mouth. The lower the lung volume, the further out this point moves. So a longer mid-to-low-volume huff clears more peripheral airways than a cough.

Advantages over repeated coughing: less fatigue, less bronchospasm and less airway collapse.

Components of the active cycle of breathing technique

ACBT is a flexible cycle of breathing exercises the patient does independently to clear secretions. It has three parts:

  1. Breathing control (BC): gentle, relaxed tidal breathing with the lower chest and relaxed shoulders, for as long as needed. It is the rest between the active parts and prevents bronchospasm, fatigue and desaturation.
  2. Thoracic expansion exercises (TEE): 3–4 deep breaths with a 3-second hold and a relaxed breath out. Air reaches behind the mucus through collateral channels. Percussion or shaking can be added.
  3. Forced expiration technique (FET): 1–2 huffs from mid to low lung volume, then BC; a cough when the mucus is central.

Sequence: BC → TEE → BC → FET → BC, repeated until the huffs are dry in two cycles (10–30 min).

Phases of autogenic drainage

Autogenic drainage (Chevaillier) means controlled breathing at different lung volumes. It gives the fastest airflow possible without the airways collapsing, which moves mucus from small to large airways.

  1. Unstick: small breaths at low lung volume (in the expiratory reserve) loosen mucus in the peripheral airways.
  2. Collect: breaths at mid lung volume (tidal range) gather the mucus in the middle airways.
  3. Evacuate: deeper breaths at high lung volume, then a gentle huff or cough to clear the central airways.

Each breath: sit upright; breathe in slowly through the nose; hold for 2–3 s; sigh out through an open mouth and throat, fast but without wheeze. Change phase when crackles are felt or heard. Hold back the cough until the mucus is central. A session takes 30–45 min.

Active cycle of breathing technique: principle, indications and contraindications

ACBT is a flexible cycle of breathing control, thoracic expansion exercises and the forced expiration technique. It loosens and clears excess bronchial secretions. Physiotherapists at the Brompton Hospital, London (Pryor and Webber), developed it from the forced expiration technique. The patient does it independently, in sitting or a drainage position, with or without a therapist.

Procedure and dose

Bronchodilator first if prescribed. Then: BC until relaxed → 3–4 TEE → BC → 1–2 huffs → BC, and repeat. Stop when the huffs are dry and non-productive in two consecutive cycles, or when the patient tires. Dose: 10–30 min, once or twice a day; more often in infection; after surgery, a few cycles every 1–2 hours.

Indications

  • Excess or retained secretions: bronchiectasis, CF, COPD, chest infection, asthma with mucus plugs.
  • Atelectasis; prevention of chest complications after surgery.
  • Making coughing easier and less tiring; an independent home routine.

Contraindications and precautions

  • Few absolute ones. It cannot be done by patients who are unconscious, confused or very young.
  • Bronchospasm: longer breathing control; stop if the wheeze increases.
  • Haemoptysis: no vigorous huffs or percussion.
  • Untreated pneumothorax, unstable heart, severe pain after surgery: modify (supported huff) or avoid the forced parts.
  • Fatigue or desaturation: shorter cycles; oxygen.

Advantages: no equipment, any position, gentle, effective, independent. Disadvantage: it needs co-operation and learning.

Indications and phases of autogenic drainage

Autogenic drainage (AD), meaning "self-drainage", was developed by Chevaillier in Belgium. The patient breathes at different lung volumes to get the highest expiratory airflow without the airways collapsing, moving mucus from the small to the large airways.

Principle

  • A slow breath in with a 2–3 s hold fills the alveoli evenly, including those behind the mucus (collateral ventilation).
  • An active but unforced breath out through an open glottis creates shear forces that move the mucus. The patient avoids forced expiration, which would squeeze unstable airways shut.
  • Breathing at low volume reaches the peripheral airways; raising the volume step by step follows the mucus centrally.
  • The cough is held back until the mucus is central, saving energy and avoiding bronchospasm.

Phases

PhaseLung volumeEffect
1. UnstickLow (expiratory reserve)Loosens mucus in the peripheral airways
2. CollectMid (tidal range)Gathers mucus in the middle airways
3. EvacuateHigh (inspiratory reserve)Moves mucus centrally; cleared by a gentle huff

Technique

Sit upright, or lie, with the upper airways clear. Breathe in slowly through the nose, hold 2–3 s, then sigh out through an open mouth and throat, as fast as possible without wheeze. The physio's hand on the chest or the sound of crackles shows when to change phase. Hold back the cough. Sessions last 30–45 min, once or twice a day.

Indications

  • Bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis (from about 8–12 years).
  • COPD or chronic bronchitis with sputum.
  • Hyper-reactive or collapsible airways (wheeze, tracheobronchomalacia), where forced huffs or coughs cause spasm or collapse.
  • Cough-induced incontinence or exhaustion.
  • Patients wanting an independent, equipment-free technique; an assisted form exists for infants.

Advantages: independent, gentle, no equipment, less bronchospasm and desaturation. Disadvantages: hard to learn, needs concentration and good body awareness, slow, not for young children or confused patients.
