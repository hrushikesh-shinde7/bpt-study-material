Muscles incised in a posterolateral thoracotomy

The posterolateral thoracotomy runs from below the nipple line, round under the tip of the scapula and up between the scapula and the spine, usually entering through the 5th intercostal space. Layers cut, from outside in:

  1. Skin and subcutaneous tissue.
  2. Latissimus dorsi.
  3. Serratus anterior (sometimes spared or retracted).
  4. Trapezius and rhomboids if the cut is extended upwards.
  5. Intercostal muscles (external, internal, innermost), then the parietal pleura.

Physio meaning: it is the most painful incision; because shoulder-girdle muscles are cut, shoulder exercises start from day 1 and posture is corrected (lean towards the incision).

Indications of intercostal drainage

  • Large or breathless pneumothorax; tension pneumothorax after needle decompression; any pneumothorax in a ventilated patient.
  • Haemothorax (blood in the pleura, usually trauma).
  • Empyema or an infected effusion.
  • Large or malignant pleural effusion, and before pleurodesis; chylothorax.
  • After thoracic or cardiac surgery (lobectomy, CABG).

Usually inserted in the safe triangle (front edge of latissimus dorsi, side edge of pectoralis major, line at nipple level) and connected to an underwater seal.

Complications of pulmonary surgery

Thoracic surgery reduces lung volume and cough strength and cuts shoulder-girdle muscles, so complications are common, especially in smokers and patients with poor lung function.

Complications and post-operative management after right lower lobectomy

A right lower lobectomy (usually through a posterolateral thoracotomy, 5th space) removes one lobe; the remaining lobes must expand to fill the space.

Complications

Sputum retention, atelectasis and pneumonia of the remaining lobes; air leak; haemorrhage; surgical emphysema; atrial fibrillation; DVT; wound infection; shoulder stiffness and poor posture; post-thoracotomy pain.

Post-operative physiotherapy

  1. Day 0–1: sit upright; high side-lying on either side; deep breaths with a 3-second hold, thoracic expansion to the right lower chest (5–10 every hour while awake); ACBT; supported huff and cough (pillow or hand over incision and drain); ankle pumps; sit out and march on the spot or walk with the drain; active-assisted right shoulder movement.
  2. Day 2–4: walking 3–4 times a day, stairs before discharge; active shoulder flexion and abduction, trunk side-bending away from the incision and rotation; mirror posture correction; clearance until the chest is clear.
  3. After discharge: daily walking, shoulder stretching and strengthening, thoracic mobility, exercise training; heavy work after about 6–8 weeks.

Drain care

Check swing and bubbling; bottle upright and below the chest; carry it low when walking; never clamp a bubbling drain; support the drain site during coughing.

Stop and report: sudden breathlessness or chest pain, heavy fresh blood, new irregular pulse, spreading surgical emphysema.

Physiotherapy after right middle lobectomy

The right middle lobe lies at the front of the right chest between the horizontal and oblique fissures. After its removal the upper and lower lobes expand to fill the space; the incision is usually a posterolateral (or anterolateral) thoracotomy or VATS ports.

Assessment

Chart (incision, drains, analgesia, X-ray); breathing pattern, SpO₂, expansion, breath sounds, sputum; pain on coughing; right shoulder range; mobility.

Physiotherapy

  • Positioning: upright sitting; high side-lying alternating sides. To drain the right middle area of the remaining lung: lying on the left side turned a quarter backwards with a pillow behind, foot of the bed raised about 15°, if tolerated.
  • Lung expansion: deep breaths with a 3-second hold and thoracic expansion to the right anterior and lower chest, 5–10 every hour while awake; incentive spirometer only as an add-on.
  • Secretions: ACBT with supported huffing and coughing (pillow over the incision and drain).
  • Circulation and mobility: ankle pumps; sit out on day 0–1; walking with the drain, 3–4 times a day; stairs before discharge.
  • Shoulder and posture: active-assisted then active right shoulder flexion and abduction (10 reps, 3–4 times a day); trunk side-bending away from the incision; mirror posture.
  • Drain care as for any chest drain; analgesia before sessions.

Later

Home walking programme, shoulder strengthening, thoracic mobility, exercise training; stop smoking; report fever, more breathlessness or wound discharge.

Intercostal drainage and the underwater seal

An intercostal drain (ICD) is a tube passed between the ribs into the pleural space to remove air, blood, fluid or pus, connected to an underwater seal that lets air and fluid out but not back in.

Indications

Large or breathless pneumothorax, tension pneumothorax after needle decompression, pneumothorax on a ventilator, haemothorax, empyema, large or malignant effusion (and before pleurodesis), after thoracic or cardiac surgery.

Site

The safe triangle: front edge of latissimus dorsi, side edge of pectoralis major, a line at nipple level (about the 5th intercostal space), apex below the axilla.

Underwater seal principle

  • The tube from the chest ends about 2 cm under water in the bottle: air and fluid bubble out on expiration and coughing, but the water blocks air being sucked back. The water acts as a one-way valve.
  • The bottle must stay below chest level; lifted above it, water would siphon back into the pleura.
  • Swinging of the water with breathing = drain open and in the pleural space. Bubbling = air leak from the lung. No swing = tube kinked or blocked, or lung fully expanded.
  • Low suction may be added to help re-expand the lung.

Physiotherapy with a drain

Check swing, bubbling, drainage and kinks before each session; deep breathing, ACBT and supported cough; early walking carrying the bottle low (or a portable drain); shoulder exercises. Never clamp a bubbling drain. If disconnected: chest end under sterile water (or a brief clamp per protocol) and call the nurse; if it falls out: sealed dressing and call for help.

Complications of thoracotomy

  • Pain from the incision, rib spreading and drains → shallow breathing, poor cough.
  • Pulmonary: sputum retention, atelectasis, pneumonia, respiratory failure.
  • Pleural: air leak, bronchopleural fistula, haemothorax, empyema, surgical emphysema.
  • Cardiac: atrial fibrillation; DVT and PE.
  • Nerve: recurrent laryngeal (hoarseness, weak cough), phrenic (raised diaphragm), intercostal (post-thoracotomy pain syndrome).
  • Musculoskeletal: stiff shoulder, poor posture, scoliosis towards the operated side.
  • Wound infection.

Physio prevention: pre-operative teaching, deep breathing, ACBT, supported cough, early walking, shoulder and trunk exercises from day 1, posture correction.

Physiotherapy after pneumonectomy

Pneumonectomy removes a whole lung, usually through a posterolateral thoracotomy (4th or 5th space). The empty space slowly fills with fluid and the mediastinum shifts towards it; all gas exchange now depends on one lung.

Positioning

  • Supine, sitting up, or turned a quarter towards the operated side.
  • Never full side-lying on the remaining lung: fluid in the space could spill through a leaking bronchial stump into the only lung. No head-down tilt.

Breathing and secretions

Deep breathing and thoracic expansion of the remaining lung, 5–10 every hour; supported huff and cough; humidification; early sitting and walking. Avoid high-pressure techniques (aggressive bagging, high PEP) near the fresh stump.

Shoulder and posture

Active shoulder exercises on the operated side from day 1 (the cut muscles move the shoulder); patients lean and drop the shoulder towards the operated side, and scoliosis can develop, so correct posture in a mirror and add trunk mobility.

Mobilisation

Sit out day 0–1, walk with the drain (usually clamped or balanced, removed early), progress daily; ankle pumps.

Watch and report

  • Irregular pulse (atrial fibrillation is common).
  • Breathlessness with crackles in the remaining lung (fluid overload).
  • Fever; coughing thin, watery or blood-stained fluid, especially lying on the good side, or air still bubbling days later = bronchopleural fistula.

Long term: exercise capacity is lower but improves with a graded walking and exercise programme; stop smoking.
