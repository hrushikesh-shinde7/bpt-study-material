Three electrotherapy modalities used to treat an infected wound

  1. Ultraviolet C (UVC): short-wave UV (about 254 nm) given in short exposures set by a test dose, protecting the surrounding skin and eyes; kills bacteria in the wound.
  2. High-voltage pulsed current (electrical stimulation): twin-peak monophasic pulses, about 100 pulses per second, sensory level (100–150 V), 45–60 min daily; the negative pole over an infected wound has an antibacterial effect and also draws in repair cells.
  3. Pulsed therapeutic ultrasound: 20% pulsed, about 0.5 W/cm², 3 MHz, 5–10 min on the skin around the wound (not into an open wound without a sterile coupling); helps the wound move through inflammation.

Always with wound cleaning, dressings and antibiotics from the team; check circulation and sensation first; no heat on infected or ischaemic tissue.

Physiotherapy for glycaemic control in diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is raised blood glucose from lack of insulin, poor response to insulin, or both. Type 2 is the commonest and is linked to obesity and inactivity. Diagnosis: fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour ≥ 200, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%.

Why exercise lowers blood sugar

Contracting muscle takes up glucose even without insulin; after training, insulin sensitivity stays better for a day or more; exercise also lowers weight, BP and lipids and heart risk.

Exercise prescription (ADA)

AerobicAt least 150 min a week of moderate to vigorous activity over 3–7 days; no more than 2 days in a row without exercise
Resistance2–3 sessions a week on non-consecutive days; 1–3 sets of 10–15 repetitions
Flexibility, balance2–3 days a week; balance work with neuropathy and in older adults
Less sittingA few minutes of light activity every 30 minutes of sitting

Hypoglycaemia: rule of 15

Sugar below 70 mg/dL or sweating, shaking, confusion: give 15 g of fast sugar, recheck in 15 minutes, repeat if still low, then a snack. Can occur hours after exercise with insulin or sulfonylureas.

Precautions

  • Check sugar before and after exercise if on insulin or sulfonylureas; carry glucose; avoid exercise at the peak of insulin action.
  • No exercise with very high sugar and ketones (mainly type 1).
  • Retinopathy: avoid heavy lifting, jumping, head-down positions and breath-holding.
  • Neuropathy: proper footwear, foot checks after each session; autonomic neuropathy: rise slowly, use RPE not heart rate.

Monitoring and education: HbA1c (target about 7%), weight and waist; diet, foot care, medicines; goals of 150 min a week with diaries or step counts.

Role of physiotherapy in diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disease with raised blood glucose. Physiotherapy helps control the sugar, prevent and treat complications and keep the patient active.

1. Assessment

Type, duration, medicines (insulin, sulfonylureas), HbA1c, hypoglycaemia episodes; BMI, waist; BP lying and standing; fitness (6MWT); strength, balance; feet: skin, callus, deformity, monofilament sensation, pulses, ulcers.

2. Exercise for glycaemic control

Aerobic ≥ 150 min a week (no more than 2 days off); resistance 2–3 days a week; flexibility and balance; break up sitting. Muscle takes up glucose without insulin and training improves insulin sensitivity.

3. Prevention and care of complications

  • Diabetic foot: daily inspection, footwear, offloading of ulcers, non-weight-bearing exercise while an ulcer heals.
  • Wounds and pressure sores: pressure relief, electrical stimulation, ultrasound, UVC as add-ons.
  • Neuropathy: balance training, fall prevention, protection of numb skin.
  • Cardiovascular disease and PAD: cardiac rehab, supervised walking for claudication.
  • Frozen shoulder, stiff hands: ROM, mobilisation.
  • Amputation: stump care and prosthetic training.

4. Weight management and education

Diet with the dietitian, 5–10% weight loss, stop smoking, foot care for life, hypoglycaemia plan (rule of 15).

5. Precautions

Check sugar around exercise; carry glucose; avoid vigorous exercise in proliferative retinopathy; no heat on numb skin; use RPE with autonomic neuropathy; watch for silent heart disease (unusual breathlessness).

Diabetic foot: what it is, how to care for it, and why

Diabetic foot is infection, ulceration or destruction of foot tissues in a person with diabetes, usually because of neuropathy and/or peripheral arterial disease. Most diabetic amputations start with a preventable foot ulcer.

Why the foot ulcerates

  • Sensory neuropathy: no protective feeling, so injuries, tight shoes and burns are not noticed.
  • Motor neuropathy: claw toes and prominent metatarsal heads raise forefoot pressure; callus forms and breaks down underneath.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: no sweating, so dry cracked skin lets bacteria in.
  • Ischaemia slows healing; high sugar weakens immunity.

How to care for it, and why

Physiotherapy: screening with a 10 g monofilament and pulses; with an active plantar ulcer, offload (cast, boot, crutches) and switch to non-weight-bearing exercise; balance training; Wagner grading (0 at-risk foot to 5 whole-foot gangrene) guides urgency.

Physiotherapy for pressure sores

A pressure sore (pressure injury) is damage to the skin and underlying tissue from pressure, or pressure with shear, usually over a bony point: sacrum, heels, greater trochanters, ischial tuberosities, back of the head, elbows. At risk: bed- or chair-bound, numb skin (diabetes, spinal cord injury), ICU, poor nutrition, incontinence, old age.

Stages (NPIAP)

1 non-blanching redness of intact skin; 2 partial-thickness loss (shallow wound or blister); 3 full-thickness loss, fat visible; 4 muscle, tendon or bone exposed; unstageable (base covered by slough or eschar); deep tissue injury (persistent maroon or purple area). Risk score: Braden scale, 18 or less = at risk.

Physiotherapy

  1. Relieve pressure: reposition on an individual schedule (at least 2-hourly, or as skin and surface require); 30° tilted side-lying rather than lying on the hip; head of bed no higher than 30° unless breathing needs it; float the heels with a pillow under the calves.
  2. Surfaces: high-specification foam or alternating-pressure mattress; pressure-relieving cushion; teach chair users weight shifts (push-ups, leaning).
  3. Avoid shear: lift, do not drag; slide sheets.
  4. Mobilise: bed mobility, sitting out and walking early; active and passive exercise for circulation and range.
  5. Help healing: electrical stimulation has the best evidence of the modalities for slow stage 2 and stage 3–4 sores; ultrasound and UVC as add-ons; wound care, nutrition and continence by the team.
  6. Teach: daily skin checks; never sit or lie on a red area.

Modalities used for wound healing

A wound heals through haemostasis → inflammation → proliferation → remodelling. Physical modalities are add-ons that help a slow wound move through these stages; they never replace wound care, offloading and treating the cause.

Other physical measures

  • Compression and calf-pump exercise for venous ulcers (after ABPI).

Factors affecting wound healing, and modalities that promote it

Wound healing passes through haemostasis (minutes–hours), inflammation (days 1–5), proliferation (day 4–week 3) and remodelling (week 3 to 1–2 years). Many factors can slow it.

In a diabetic foot the main factors are high sugar, ischaemia, unnoticed pressure, infection and smoking.

Modalities that promote healing

  • High-voltage pulsed current: ~100 pps, sensory level, 45–60 min daily; negative pole for infection.
  • Pulsed ultrasound: 20%, ~0.5 W/cm², 3 MHz, 5–10 min around the wound.
  • UVC: ~254 nm test-dosed exposures to kill bacteria.
  • Low-level laser: ~1–4 J/cm² to the wound edges.

Physiotherapy measures that remove the slowing factors

Pressure relief and offloading; compression with calf-pump exercise for venous ulcers; exercise for circulation; elevation for oedema; advice on sugar control, nutrition and stopping smoking. Check circulation and sensation before any modality; no heat on ischaemic or numb skin.
