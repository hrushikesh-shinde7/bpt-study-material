Three causes of altered ventilation–perfusion ratio

Normally alveolar ventilation (about 4 L/min) and lung blood flow (about 5 L/min) are matched, giving an overall V/Q of about 0.8. A mismatch lowers PaO₂.

  1. Low V/Q (shunt when there is no ventilation): blood passes alveoli that get too little air: secretions and mucus plugs, atelectasis, consolidation (pneumonia), pulmonary oedema, ARDS, bronchospasm, lying flat, obesity, pain after surgery.
  2. High V/Q (dead space when there is no perfusion): air reaches alveoli with too little blood: pulmonary embolism, low cardiac output or blood loss.
  3. Destroyed or over-stretched alveoli: emphysema (capillaries destroyed), high PEEP, pulmonary vascular disease.

Low V/Q improves with oxygen and positioning (affected area up to drain, good lung down to oxygenate); a true shunt barely responds to oxygen until the alveoli re-open.

Physiological zones of ventilation

In the upright lung both ventilation and perfusion increase from apex to base, but perfusion increases more steeply (gravity), so V/Q is about 3 at the apex and about 0.6 at the base. West described three zones (PA alveolar, Pa arterial, Pv venous pressure):

  • Zone 1 (apex): PA > Pa > Pv: no blood flow (alveolar dead space); not present normally, appears with low BP or high airway pressure (PEEP).
  • Zone 2 (middle): Pa > PA > Pv: flow depends on the difference between arterial and alveolar pressure.
  • Zone 3 (base): Pa > Pv > PA: continuous flow; best perfused.

If the question means the airway zones: conducting zone (generations 0–16, no gas exchange, dead space about 150 mL) and respiratory zone (generations 17–23, gas exchange).

Positioning in the ICU: physiological effects, benefits and examples

Therapeutic positioning uses gravity and body position to improve breathing, circulation and comfort and to prevent complications.

Physiological effects and benefits

  • Better V/Q matching and oxygenation; larger lung volume (FRC); re-opens collapsed areas.
  • Gravity drainage of secretions; lower work of breathing.
  • Head-up positions reduce reflux, aspiration and ventilator-associated pneumonia.
  • Regular turns prevent pressure injuries, contractures and nerve compression.
  • Upright positions keep BP reflexes and muscles working, raise alertness and prepare for mobilisation.
  • Comfort, communication and dignity.

Exceptions to "good lung down": lung abscess or bleeding (could spill into the good lung) and infants. On controlled ventilation, side-lying can worsen matching: check SpO₂.

Precautions: check SpO₂, HR and BP before and after every turn; secure the tube, lines and drains; allow time to settle (SpO₂ can dip for minutes).

Physiological application of sitting upright as a therapeutic position

Sitting upright (in bed, over the edge or in a chair) is the simplest and most useful therapeutic position. Lying flat lets the abdominal contents push the diaphragm up and the lung volume fall.

Respiratory effects

  • The diaphragm descends and the abdominal contents fall away: larger FRC and tidal volume.
  • Fewer collapsed basal alveoli; better V/Q and oxygenation; lower work of breathing.
  • A deeper breath before a cough, so a stronger cough.
  • Less reflux and aspiration.

Cardiovascular effects

  • Keeps the baroreceptor (BP) reflexes working and prevents the postural hypotension that follows bed rest.
  • With the legs down it lowers venous return, which eases breathlessness in pulmonary oedema and heart failure (orthopnoea).

Neurological and other effects

  • Wakefulness, orientation, communication and safer swallowing.
  • Head-up position lowers intracranial pressure.
  • First step towards standing and walking; relieves pressure on the back.

Used in

Post-operative patients, ICU and ventilated patients (and breathing trials), COPD and asthma (with forward lean), pulmonary oedema, neurological patients, older people on bed rest.

Precautions: sit up in stages after long bed rest (postural drop); watch for sliding into a slumped position, which squashes the abdomen and loads the sacrum; tetraplegia may need lying or a binder.
