Others: paramedian, transverse, rooftop (Chevron), inguinal, laparoscopic ports. Physio meaning: upper abdominal incisions lie close to the diaphragm and cause more painful breathing and more lung complications than lower or laparoscopic ones.

Rationale of any three goals of pre-operative physiotherapy

  1. Teach deep breathing, huffing and supported cough: learned while the patient is pain-free and alert, they are done correctly after surgery, when pain and drowsiness make learning hard; they restore lung volume and prevent collapse and pneumonia.
  2. Teach log-roll, getting out of bed and early walking: less pain and fear when moving, so the patient sits out and walks sooner; walking raises lung volume and prevents DVT.
  3. Assess and record a baseline: finds high-risk patients (smoker, COPD, obesity, older age, upper abdominal incision) who need more care, and gives a comparison after surgery.

Also: reduce anxiety with education (better co-operation); prehabilitation in high-risk patients (fitter patients recover faster). One 30-minute pre-operative session roughly halved lung complications in the LIPPSMAck-POP trial.

Physiotherapy after gastrectomy

Gastrectomy is removal of part or all of the stomach, usually for cancer, through an upper midline or rooftop incision. Being upper abdominal, it carries a high risk of post-operative pulmonary complications (atelectasis, pneumonia).

Pre-operative

Assess risk (smoking, lung disease, fitness); explain; teach deep breathing with a hold, ACBT, supported cough, log-roll and ankle pumps; stop smoking; prehabilitation if time allows.

Post-operative

  • Day 0–1: sit upright or high side-lying; deep breaths with a 3-second hold, 5–10 every hour while awake; ACBT if sputum; supported huff and cough with a pillow over the wound; ankle pumps, quadriceps and gluteal sets; log-roll to sit; sit out; walk with help.
  • Day 2 to discharge: walk 3–4 times a day, increasing the distance; breathing exercises until fully mobile; gentle trunk mobility and posture correction (patients bend forward to protect the wound).
  • After discharge: daily walking; gentle core activation, progressed after about 6 weeks; no heavy lifting for about 6 weeks.

Special points

  • Nasogastric tube, drains and epidural: secure lines before moving; check leg power and BP before standing with an epidural.
  • Weight loss and weakness from cancer and reduced eating (small frequent meals): graded endurance and strength training; liaise with the dietitian.
  • Chemotherapy may follow: adjust exercise to fatigue and blood counts.

Watch for

Fever, falling SpO₂, reduced breath sounds at the bases (collapse); calf pain (DVT); wound leakage or abdominal swelling; report early. Incentive spirometry only as an add-on.

Importance of pre-operative chest physiotherapy in major abdominal surgery

Post-operative pulmonary complications (PPCs), such as atelectasis and pneumonia, are common after major abdominal surgery and lengthen hospital stay. Anaesthesia lowers functional residual capacity; wound pain stops deep breaths and coughing; the diaphragm works poorly for days after upper abdominal surgery.

Who is at high risk

Upper abdominal or long operations, open surgery, emergencies; older age, smoking, COPD or asthma, obesity, poor fitness and nutrition, heart failure, sleep apnoea.

What pre-operative physiotherapy includes

  1. Assessment to identify high-risk patients and record a baseline.
  2. Education: what happens after surgery, why breathing and early walking matter, pain relief.
  3. Teaching and practice: deep breathing with a 3–5 second hold, ACBT, supported cough (hands or pillow over the wound), log-roll bed mobility, ankle pumps, getting out of bed.
  4. Prehabilitation for high-risk patients: aerobic and strength training and inspiratory muscle training for a few weeks.
  5. Stop smoking as early as possible.

Importance

  • Techniques learned while pain-free and alert are done correctly after surgery.
  • Evidence: in the LIPPSMAck-POP trial (441 upper abdominal surgery patients) a single 30-minute pre-operative education and breathing session roughly halved PPCs (hazard ratio 0.48).
  • Less anxiety, better co-operation, earlier mobilisation, shorter stay, fewer DVTs.
  • Fits enhanced recovery (ERAS) programmes.

Complications after general surgery under general anaesthesia and their prevention

General anaesthesia relaxes the diaphragm and lowers lung volume; surgery, pain, opioids and bed rest add more risk. Most complications are preventable with good pain control, pre-operative teaching and early mobilisation.

Complications of abdominal surgery, and physiotherapy of deep vein thrombosis

Complications of abdominal surgery

  • Pulmonary: atelectasis, sputum retention, pneumonia (highest after upper abdominal incisions), respiratory failure.
  • Thromboembolic: DVT and pulmonary embolism.
  • Wound: infection, dehiscence, incisional hernia.
  • Gut and bladder: paralytic ileus, nausea, urinary retention.
  • General: pain, weakness, deconditioning, pressure sores.

Prevention: pre-operative teaching, deep breathing, supported cough, log-roll, early walking, wound support, no heavy lifting for 6 weeks.

DVT: why it happens

Virchow's triad: stasis (bed rest, no calf pump), vessel injury (surgery) and hypercoagulability (cancer, dehydration, the post-operative state).

Prevention

  1. Ankle pumps 10 every hour while awake; static quadriceps and gluteal sets; knee bending.
  2. Early mobilisation: sit out on day 0–1, walk 3–4 times a day.
  3. Deep breathing (helps venous return); hydration; no pillows behind the knees.
  4. Stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression and heparin as prescribed.

After a DVT is diagnosed

  • Confirmed by duplex ultrasound (Homans' sign is unreliable).
  • Start walking once anticoagulation is therapeutic; progress walking and leg exercises; elevate the leg at rest; compression for symptoms.
  • No massage or heat on the leg; gentle handling (bleeding risk).
  • Watch for PE: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, fast heart rate, coughing blood: stop and call for help.

Physiotherapy after exploratory laparotomy

Exploratory laparotomy is a long vertical midline incision, often an emergency for perforation and peritonitis, obstruction, trauma or bleeding. Patients are often sicker than after planned surgery: sepsis, ICU stay, nasogastric tube, several drains, catheter, sometimes a stoma, and a swollen abdomen that pushes the diaphragm up. The risk of lung complications is high.

In ICU or ventilated

Positioning, airway clearance, suction and early mobilisation within the ICU safety criteria.

On the ward

  1. Position: sit upright (diaphragm descends); avoid slumping.
  2. Lung expansion: deep breathing with a 3-second hold, 5–10 every hour while awake.
  3. Secretions: ACBT and huffing with a pillow pressed over the wound; supported cough.
  4. Circulation: ankle pumps, leg exercises.
  5. Mobility: log-roll to sit; sit out and walk from day 1 if stable, managing tubes and drains.

Protect the wound

The long midline wound risks dehiscence and a later incisional hernia: support it when coughing; no straining or heavy lifting; abdominal binder if the surgeon uses one. With a stoma: support it when coughing; gentle core work later to lower the risk of a parastomal hernia.

Later rehabilitation

Graded walking and endurance, posture correction, gradual core strengthening after about 6 weeks, return to work.

Watch for: fever, low SpO₂, breathlessness, wound leakage, abdominal swelling, falling BP (sepsis): report.

Rationale and procedure of physiotherapy on day 2 after cholecystectomy

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (usual) uses four small ports: low risk of lung complications, home in 1–2 days; gas irritates the diaphragm and causes shoulder-tip pain (phrenic nerve, C3–5), which walking helps. Open cholecystectomy (Kocher's right subcostal incision) causes painful breathing and low lung volume at the right base: high risk of lung complications.

Principles of physiotherapy after abdominal surgery

After abdominal surgery, anaesthesia, wound pain, opioids and lying flat reduce lung volume and cough, and bed rest adds DVT risk and weakness. Physiotherapy follows these principles:

  1. Control pain first: treat when analgesia is working; support the wound with a pillow or hands for breathing, coughing and moving.
  2. Restore lung volume: upright positions, deep breathing with a hold, early walking.
  3. Clear secretions: ACBT and supported huff and cough when sputum is present.
  4. Mobilise early: sit out of bed on the day of surgery or the next day, then walk several times a day (the basis of enhanced recovery, ERAS).
  5. Prevent circulatory problems: ankle pumps and walking against DVT; check for calf pain and swelling.
  6. Protect the wound: log-roll to get up; no heavy lifting or sit-ups for about 6 weeks, or as the surgeon advises.
  7. Restore posture and trunk movement: patients bend forward to protect the wound; correct posture, gentle trunk and core exercise, progressed after about 6 weeks.
  8. Watch for complications: fever, low SpO₂, breathlessness, wound leakage, abdominal swelling; report them.
  9. Educate and plan discharge: home walking programme, safe lifting, return to work; stop smoking.

Incentive spirometry is only an add-on; pre-operative teaching makes all of these work better.
