Precautions following radical mastectomy

  1. Shoulder flexion and abduction only to about 90° while drains are in (about a week); no vigorous stretching over a fresh wound.
  2. Watch for seroma, wound infection and flap problems; report swelling, redness or discharge.
  3. Protect the operated arm: avoid cuts, burns, insect bites, tight sleeves and bangles; use the other arm for injections where possible.
  4. No heat (hot packs) or vigorous massage on a swollen arm.
  5. Build up lifting gradually; avoid sudden heavy or unaccustomed loads.
  6. During chemotherapy: reduce or hold exercise with fever, very low blood counts or severe fatigue; no electrotherapy over tumour sites; no heavy loading of bones with metastases.

Refer urgently: red, hot, painful arm with fever (cellulitis); sudden arm or calf swelling (DVT).

Any three goals of physiotherapy in radical mastectomy

  1. Prevent post-operative chest complications and DVT: deep breathing, supported cough and early walking, because anaesthesia and pain reduce lung volume.
  2. Restore full, pain-free shoulder range and posture: graded ROM (to 90° while drains are in, then full), scapular exercises; prevents frozen shoulder, axillary cording and a raised, protracted shoulder.
  3. Prevent and control lymphoedema: hand pumping, elevation, skin care, gradual loading and arm measurement, because axillary nodes are removed for life.

Also: regain strength and fitness for work and home (progressive resistance and aerobic exercise from about 6 weeks), and support body image with a prosthesis.

Three types of bandages used for lymphoedema

  1. Short-stretch (low-stretch) bandages: the main lymphoedema bandage; low pressure at rest (safe at night) and high pressure when muscles contract against them, which pumps fluid out of the limb.
  2. Multilayer lymphoedema bandaging: tubular stockinette, gauze on each finger, padding (soft wool or foam) to even the shape and protect bony points, then several layers of short-stretch bandage, firmest at the hand and less towards the shoulder.
  3. Adjustable compression wraps (Velcro type): the patient puts them on and tightens them herself; useful for self-management and the maintenance phase.

Not used: long-stretch elastic bandages, which keep high pressure at rest and can act like a tourniquet.

Physiotherapy after radical and modified radical mastectomy

Radical (Halsted) mastectomy removes the breast, pectoralis major and minor and all axillary nodes; modified radical mastectomy (MRM) keeps pectoralis major. Both cause shoulder stiffness and lymphoedema risk; radical surgery adds more shoulder weakness.

Assessment

Surgery, side and hand dominance, drains, nodes removed, chemo or radiotherapy; pain; shoulder range both sides; posture and scapular winging; arm circumference every 4 cm from the wrist; sensation of the inner arm; job and home load.

Before surgery

Baseline shoulder range and arm measurements; teach breathing exercises, first-week arm exercises and posture.

Days 1–7 (drains in)

  • Deep breathing, supported cough, early walking.
  • Hand pumping, wrist and elbow movements, shoulder shrugs: 10 repetitions several times a day.
  • Shoulder flexion and abduction only to about 90° (full range early increases drainage and seroma).
  • Arm on a pillow when lying, hand higher than the elbow.

After drains are removed

  • Full active and active-assisted range: wand, finger wall-climbing, pulley, hands behind the head and back; stretches held 20–30 s, 3–5 times, 2–3 times a day.
  • Scapular setting, retraction and posture correction; serratus anterior exercises for winging.
  • Scar massage once healed; stretching of axillary cords; keep stretching through radiotherapy.

From about 6 weeks

Progressive resistance training (start light, supervised), aerobic exercise (about 30 min, 3 times a week), work-specific tasks such as lifting, cooking and writing on a board.

Lymphoedema care and education

Skin care, avoid cuts and burns, no tight bangles, gradual loading, regular arm measurement; decongestive therapy and a sleeve if swelling appears. Breast prosthesis, psychological support, return to work.

Physiotherapy for a 33-year-old receptionist with lymphoedema

Lymphoedema is chronic swelling from protein-rich fluid that the lymphatics cannot drain; after breast cancer treatment it affects the arm, hand or chest wall. Diagnosis: ≥ 2 cm larger circumference or about 10% larger volume than the other arm. Stages 0–III (ISL).

Assessment

Circumference every 4 cm from the wrist on both arms, volume, pitting, skin texture, infection signs, shoulder range, function at work (typing, telephone, long sitting), DASH.

Complete decongestive therapy

1. Intensive phase (daily, 2–4 weeks)Manual lymph drainage: light, slow skin-stretching strokes, first clearing the neck, the other axilla and the trunk, then moving fluid from the upper arm to forearm and hand. Multilayer short-stretch bandaging day and night. Exercise in the bandage. Skin care
2. Maintenance (lifelong)Fitted compression sleeve (and glove if the hand swells) during the day; simple self lymph drainage; regular exercise; skin care; weight control

Exercise

Hand pumping, elbow and shoulder movements and deep breathing with the bandage or sleeve on (muscle pump); slowly progressive, supervised resistance training is safe ("start low, go slow"); aerobic exercise.

Workstation advice for a receptionist

Arm supported on the desk at elbow height; regular movement breaks; sleeve during work; avoid tight bangles or watch on that side; carry bags on the other side or with both hands.

Education and precautions

Keep skin clean and moisturised; treat every cut at once; no heat or saunas on the arm; no vigorous massage. Red, hot, painful arm with fever = cellulitis: stop bandaging and massage, refer urgently. Elevate the arm when resting; pneumatic compression only as an add-on.

Rationale of physiotherapy after modified radical mastectomy

MRM removes the breast and axillary nodes but keeps pectoralis major. Physiotherapy answers four problems: chest complications, a stiff shoulder, lymphoedema and loss of function.
