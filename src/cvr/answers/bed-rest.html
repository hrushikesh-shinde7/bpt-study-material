Effects of bed rest on any three systems

Memory: "heart, lungs, legs".

  1. Cardiovascular: orthostatic hypotension (blood volume falls and BP reflexes are unused), higher resting heart rate, lower fitness (3 weeks of bed rest cut VO₂max by about 27%).
  2. Respiratory: lower lung volumes and basal collapse, retained secretions and pneumonia (diaphragm pushed up when lying flat, shallow breathing).
  3. Musculoskeletal: muscle wasting and weakness (about 1–1.5% strength a day), contractures, bone loss; plus DVT in the legs.

Others: pressure sores (skin), constipation, urinary stasis, insulin resistance, delirium and low mood. Prevention: early, graded mobilisation.

Complications of bed rest and immobility on different body systems (ICU patient)

Lying flat removes two loads at once: gravity along the body (which trains BP reflexes and fluid balance) and muscle work against gravity (which keeps muscle, bone and fitness). Every system adapts downwards within days, fastest in critically ill and older patients.

ICU-acquired weakness: generalised symmetrical weakness during critical illness with no other cause; MRC sum score below 48/60; risks: sepsis, organ failure, long ventilation, immobility, high sugar, steroids. It slows weaning and affects walking for months.

Prevention: early mobilisation within safety criteria: turns, ROM, bed cycling, sitting over the edge, standing (tilt table), walking, with monitoring before, during and after.

Adverse effects of bed rest on the cardiovascular and pulmonary systems

Bed rest removes the gravitational load on the circulation and lets the abdominal contents push the diaphragm up. Within days the heart and lungs adapt downwards (3 weeks of bed rest cut young men's VO₂max by about 27%, Dallas 1968).

Cardiovascular effects

↑ Resting heart rate, ↓ stroke volumeBlood volume falls, the heart fills less → sit and stand early, active exercise
Orthostatic hypotensionFluid shifts to the chest → more urine, plasma volume falls; BP reflexes unused → sit up in stages, dangle legs, tilt table, ankle pumps before standing, stockings
↓ Aerobic fitnessLower output; muscles lose capillaries and mitochondria → bed cycling, walking, graded endurance
DVT and pulmonary embolismStasis (no calf pump) and sticky blood after illness or surgery → ankle pumps 10 an hour, early walking, compression, anticoagulants as prescribed

Pulmonary effects

↓ Lung volumes, basal collapseDiaphragm pushed up lying flat; shallow breathing; small airways close → upright positions, deep breaths with a hold, turning, walking
Retained secretions, pneumoniaSecretions pool in dependent areas; weaker cough; slower mucociliary clearance; aspiration when flat → turn 2–4-hourly, head up 30–45°, huffing and coughing
Weaker breathing musclesDisuse; the diaphragm wastes on a ventilator → spontaneous breathing as soon as possible, inspiratory muscle training
↓ OxygenationV/Q mismatch in collapsed dependent lung → positioning, mobilisation

Conclusion: bed rest is a treatment with side effects; use it only when needed and for as short a time as possible, and prevent its effects with early, graded mobilisation, breathing exercises and positioning.
