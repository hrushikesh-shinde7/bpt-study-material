Three indications of bronchial hygiene therapy

Bronchial hygiene therapy covers the techniques that help a patient clear airway secretions: ACBT, autogenic drainage, gravity-assisted positions, percussion and vibration, PEP devices, assisted cough and suction, with humidification and nebulised saline to loosen mucus.

  1. Excess or hard-to-clear sputum (more than about 25–30 mL a day): bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, chronic bronchitis, lung abscess, resolving pneumonia; done as a daily routine in chronic suppurative disease.
  2. Retained secretions with mucus plugging and lobar or segmental collapse: after surgery, in the ICU; signs are coarse crackles, reduced breath sounds, falling SpO₂, collapse on X-ray.
  3. Weak or painful cough: neuromuscular disease, spinal cord injury, pain after surgery, sedation, intubated and ventilated patients (the tube blocks the cough and dries the cilia).

Goal: clear the airways, prevent infection and collapse, and improve gas exchange.

Various airway clearance techniques: application, advantages and disadvantages

Normal airway clearance relies on the mucociliary escalator and the cough. Airway clearance techniques (ACTs) are needed when mucus is excessive or thick (bronchiectasis, CF, chronic bronchitis, infection), when the cilia work poorly (smoking, CF, dry gas through a tube), or when the cough is weak or painful. Aims: remove mucus, break the cycle of infection and inflammation, improve ventilation and gas exchange, and reduce cough and breathlessness.

Classification

  • Breathing techniques: ACBT, FET and huffing, autogenic drainage, directed or assisted cough.
  • Gravity and manual: postural drainage, percussion, vibration, shaking.
  • Devices: PEP mask, oscillating PEP (Flutter, Acapella), bubble PEP, vest, cough assist.
  • Exercise, and suction for patients who cannot cough.

Choosing a technique

No single technique is best (BTS/ACPRC 2009): choose by age, condition, ability, preference and what the patient will keep doing. Infants: modified drainage positions and gentle percussion; older children: PEP, bubble PEP, ACBT; wheezy or floppy airways: AD or PEP; weak cough: assisted cough, cough assist, suction. Order of treatment: bronchodilator → mucoactive (hypertonic saline) → clearance → inhaled antibiotic or steroid. Review the technique regularly.

Mucus removal techniques in chronic respiratory disease

In chronic hypersecretory disease (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, chronic bronchitis and COPD, ciliary dyskinesia) retained mucus causes infection and inflammation, which damage the airways and produce more mucus (the vicious cycle). A daily mucus-clearance routine breaks this cycle and reduces exacerbations, cough and breathlessness.

1. Assessment

Daily sputum amount, colour and stickiness; ease of clearing; lobes involved (X-ray, HRCT); crackles; SpO₂ and spirometry; techniques tried before; adherence; leakage of urine with coughing.

2. Preparing the mucus

Good fluid intake; humidification; nebulised saline (isotonic or hypertonic, after a bronchodilator); mucolytics or dornase alfa (in CF) as prescribed; bronchodilator first in reactive airways.

3. Techniques

ACBTBreathing control, 3–4 deep breaths with a 3-s hold, breathing control, 1–2 huffs; repeat until the huffs are dry
Autogenic drainageBreathing at low, mid and then high lung volumes (unstick, collect, evacuate), then a gentle huff
Gravity-assisted positionsPosition for the worst lobe, about 10 min a position; modified (no head-down) when tilting is contraindicated
Manual techniquesPercussion, vibration, shaking on expiration, as adjuncts

4. Daily routine and dose

One main technique the patient can do alone, in the worst lobe's position if tolerated, 10–30 min once or twice a day; 2–3 times a day during an exacerbation, with early medical review. Pelvic floor: contract before huffs and coughs if coughing causes leakage.

5. Review

Sputum weight, cough and bronchiectasis quality-of-life scores, number of exercise sessions and exacerbations.

Precautions: haemoptysis (no vigorous huffing or percussion), untreated pneumothorax, bronchospasm, reflux in head-down positions, fatigue and desaturation (add oxygen, shorten cycles).
