Energy conservation techniques for a breathless patient

Energy conservation means organising daily activities so they use less energy and cause less breathlessness. Remember the 4 Ps:

  1. Plan: spread heavy jobs over the week; do them at the best time of day (after airway clearance and medicines); gather everything needed first.
  2. Pace: slow and steady; rest before becoming very breathless; "blow as you go" (breathe out on the effort).
  3. Prioritise: drop or share non-essential jobs.
  4. Position: sit to bathe, dress, iron and cook; keep things between waist and shoulder height; avoid bending and overhead work; use aids (trolley, long-handled sponge, shower stool, raised toilet seat).

Also: pursed lips when walking; stairs one step per breath out, with rests on landings; loose clothes; avoid smoke, dust and extremes of temperature.

Activity pacing in a breathless patient, with examples

Activity pacing means doing activity in planned, manageable amounts with rests, staying below the breathlessness that forces a stop. It avoids the "boom and bust" pattern of overdoing it on a good day and then needing days to recover.

  • Break tasks into steps: make the bed in stages; shop in two short trips.
  • Alternate heavy and light tasks; plan rests through the day.
  • Stop, rest, recover, restart when breathlessness reaches a moderate level (Borg about 3–4).
  • Stairs: one step per breath out; rest on the landing.
  • Walking: intervals at a slower, steady pace, with pursed lips.
  • Washing and dressing: sit down; rest between items; put on a towelling dressing gown instead of drying with a towel.

Pacing lets the patient do more over the day with less breathlessness and anxiety, and gradually build tolerance.

Rationale of therapeutic positioning to relieve dyspnoea

Positions of ease (relaxation positions) reduce breathlessness, especially in COPD:

  • High side-lying with pillows and the top arm supported.
  • Forward-lean sitting, forearms on the thighs or on pillows on a table.
  • Relaxed sitting back with the arms on armrests.
  • Forward-lean standing against a wall, sill, rail or walking frame; relaxed standing with the back against a wall.

Rationale

  1. Forward lean: the abdominal contents push a flattened diaphragm up into a dome, so it contracts more effectively.
  2. Arms fixed: the accessory muscles (pectorals, serratus) can pull on the ribs instead of moving the arms.
  3. Supported positions reduce postural muscle work, oxygen demand and anxiety.
  4. Upright positions increase lung volume and improve V/Q matching.

Dyspnoea: definition, mechanism of perception and treatment

Definition (ATS 2012): dyspnoea is "a subjective experience of breathing discomfort that consists of qualitatively distinct sensations that vary in intensity". The main sensations are air hunger, work or effort, and chest tightness, with an emotional (distress) part. It is measured by the patient's own rating: modified Borg (0–10) during activity, mMRC grade (0–4) for daily life. Related terms: orthopnoea (breathless lying flat, as in heart failure), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (wakes breathless), platypnoea (breathless upright, relieved lying).

Mechanism of perception

  1. Motor drive and effort: a copy of the motor command to the breathing muscles (corollary discharge) reaches the sensory cortex. Weak or overloaded muscles need a bigger command, which is felt as effort.
  2. Chemoreceptors: central (CO₂ and acidity, medulla) and peripheral (low O₂, carotid and aortic bodies) increase drive and cause air hunger.
  3. Mechanoreceptors: stretch and irritant receptors in the airways, J receptors (stiff, wet lungs), and muscle spindles and tendon organs in the chest wall report how the lungs and chest move.
  4. Mismatch (neuromechanical dissociation): high drive while the lungs cannot respond (hyperinflation, stiffness, weakness) is the main source.
  5. Emotion: the limbic system adds fear; anxiety speeds breathing, which worsens hyperinflation and breathlessness.

Causes: increased demand (exercise, fever, acidosis, hypoxia), increased load (obstruction, stiff lungs, hyperinflation), weak muscles, anxiety.
