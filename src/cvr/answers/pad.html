Ankle–brachial index: method, grades and interpretation

ABI = ankle systolic pressure ÷ the higher arm (brachial) systolic pressure, measured with a cuff and hand-held Doppler, for each leg.

Method: patient supine, rested 5–10 min; systolic pressure in both arms (take the higher); at the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial of each ankle (take the higher); divide.

Three special tests to assess arterial insufficiency

  1. Buerger's test: patient supine, raise the leg slowly and note the angle at which the foot turns pale (Buerger's angle; below about 20° = severe ischaemia); then sit with legs hanging down: slow return of colour and a dusky red flush (dependent rubor) is positive.
  2. Capillary refill time: press the nail bed or pulp for a few seconds and release; colour taking longer than about 2 seconds to return means poor perfusion.
  3. Ankle–brachial index: Doppler ankle pressure ÷ higher arm pressure; ≤ 0.90 = PAD, below 0.40 severe.

Others: pulse palpation (femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial; graded 0 to 4+), venous filling time (over 20 s abnormal), treadmill walking test (pain-free and maximum walking distance).

Intermittent claudication

Intermittent claudication is cramping pain in a muscle group, usually the calf, that comes on after walking a fairly fixed distance and goes away within a few minutes of standing still. It is the main symptom of peripheral arterial disease.

  • Cause: a narrowed artery cannot raise blood flow when walking increases the muscles' oxygen demand; metabolites build up and cause pain; rest lowers the demand.
  • Site shows the block: aorto-iliac → buttock and thigh; femoro-popliteal (commonest) → calf; tibial → foot.
  • Grading (Fontaine): IIa walks over about 200 m; IIb under about 200 m; III rest pain; IV ulcer or gangrene.
  • Differs from neurogenic claudication (spinal stenosis): that is relieved by sitting or bending forward and the pulses are normal.

Treatment: supervised walking to moderate pain, rest, repeat, plus stopping smoking.

Evaluation parameters for peripheral arterial disorders

  1. History: claudication site, distance or time to onset and to stopping, recovery time; rest pain; smoking (pack-years, bidi); diabetes, BP, heart disease; job and walking demands.
  2. Inspection and palpation: colour, hair loss, nails, muscle wasting, ulcers and gangrene; skin temperature on both sides; pulses with grades.
  3. Special tests: ABI, Buerger's test, capillary refill, venous filling time; 10 g monofilament if diabetic.
  4. Function and outcome measures: treadmill or 6-minute walk test with pain-free and maximum walking distance, claudication pain scale; ankle range and calf strength; Walking Impairment Questionnaire, VascuQoL.

Ankle–brachial index and its significance

The ankle–brachial index (ABI) is the ratio of the systolic pressure at the ankle to the systolic pressure in the arm. It is a simple, non-invasive bedside test for peripheral arterial disease (PAD).

Method

  1. Patient lies flat and rests 5–10 minutes.
  2. Measure systolic pressure in both arms with a cuff and hand-held Doppler; take the higher.
  3. Cuff just above the ankle; measure systolic pressure at the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries; take the higher for each leg.
  4. ABI = ankle pressure ÷ higher arm pressure, for each leg.

Interpretation

Above 1.40Calcified, non-compressible arteries (diabetes, kidney disease): falsely high; use toe–brachial index
1.00–1.40Normal
0.91–0.99Borderline
0.70–0.90Mild PAD (claudication)
0.40–0.69Moderate PAD
Below 0.40Severe: rest pain, ulcers, gangrene likely

Significance

  • Diagnosis of PAD (≤ 0.90) and its severity.
  • Cardiovascular risk: a low ABI marks widespread atherosclerosis and a high risk of heart attack and stroke.
  • Safety check before compression: full compression only at ABPI ≥ 0.8; reduced 0.5–0.8; none below 0.5.
  • Planning exercise: supports the walking programme and the precautions (no heat on an ischaemic limb).
  • Monitoring: change after exercise, angioplasty or bypass; with treadmill walking distance.

Limitation: falsely high in diabetics with calcified arteries; normal at rest in some patients who drop it only after exercise.

Physiotherapy in Buerger's disease

Buerger's disease (thromboangiitis obliterans) is an inflammatory, non-atherosclerotic disease of small and medium arteries and veins of the hands and feet in young smokers (onset before about 45–50; Shionoya and Olin criteria). Features: foot (instep) claudication, cold sensitive fingers and toes, Raynaud's-like colour changes, migrating superficial phlebitis, painful ulcers and gangrene of the digits.

Assessment

Smoking history; pulses, Allen's test (hand), ABI, Buerger's test, capillary refill; skin, ulcers; claudication distance; sensation.

Physiotherapy

  1. Absolute stop of all tobacco (cigarettes, bidi, chewing tobacco, passive smoke): the only measure proven to halt the disease; even a few a day keep it active.
  2. Walking programme within tolerance: walk to moderate pain, rest, repeat; build to 30–45 min, at least 3 times a week; foot (instep) and calf exercises.
  3. Buerger–Allen exercises (classic): lying with the legs raised 45–90° until the skin blanches (1–2 min) → sitting with legs hanging down until red (2–5 min) with ankle and toe movements → lying flat (about 5 min); repeat several cycles a few times a day.
  4. Warmth without heat: socks and gloves, avoid cold; never apply direct heat (ischaemic skin burns).
  5. Foot and hand care: daily inspection, proper footwear, never barefoot, careful nail care; protect ulcers.
  6. Rest pain: leg slightly dependent at night; do not elevate an ischaemic leg.
  7. After digit or limb amputation: stump care and prosthetic training.

Evidence: Buerger–Allen exercises have weak evidence; supervised walking has the strongest evidence and is the main exercise whenever the patient can walk.

Physiotherapy for claudication in arterial disease of the popliteal artery

A block in the femoro-popliteal segment (the commonest site) causes calf claudication: cramping calf pain after a fixed walking distance, relieved by standing still.

Assessment

Popliteal and foot pulses (weak or absent), ABI, Buerger's test, skin and ulcers, treadmill test (pain-free and maximum walking distance), claudication pain scale, calf strength, risk factors.

Supervised exercise therapy: "30 / 3 / 12" (ACC/AHA 2024, Class 1A)

Why it works

Better oxygen use by the calf muscles, collateral vessels open around the block, better arterial lining, more efficient walking: the block stays, but he walks much further before pain.

Add

  • Heel raises and sit-to-stand, 2–3 sets of 10–15, 2–3 times a week; calf stretches 30 s × 3; ankle ROM.
  • If walking is impossible: arm cranking or cycling.
  • Stop smoking; control diabetes, BP, lipids; daily foot care.

Precautions: no heat on the limb; check heart rate and BP (heart disease is common); report sudden pain, pallor or coldness (acute ischaemia), new rest pain or ulcers.

Assessment and physiotherapy of peripheral arterial disease

PAD is narrowing or block of the limb arteries, almost always from atherosclerosis; its main symptom is intermittent claudication, progressing to rest pain, ulcers and gangrene (Fontaine I–IV).

Assessment

  • History: claudication site, onset and stopping distance, recovery; rest pain; smoking pack-years; diabetes, BP, heart disease; job and walking needs.
  • Examination: colour, hair, nails, wasting, ulcers; temperature both sides; pulses graded 0–4+.
  • Tests: ABI (≤ 0.90 PAD), Buerger's test, capillary refill, venous filling time; monofilament if diabetic.
  • Function: treadmill or 6MWT with pain-free and maximum distance; Walking Impairment Questionnaire, VascuQoL.

Physiotherapy

  1. Supervised exercise therapy (first-line): walk to moderate claudication pain, rest, repeat; 30–45 min, ≥ 3 times a week, ≥ 12 weeks; progress speed or gradient.
  2. Home walking programme with a log if supervision is not possible; arm or cycle ergometry if walking is impossible.
  3. Strengthening of calf and thigh; ankle range and calf stretches.
  4. Buerger–Allen exercises as an add-on.
  5. Rest pain and ulcers: leg slightly dependent at night, padded heels, gentle ankle and toe movement, offloading.
  6. After angioplasty or bypass: early walking as allowed, groin wound care, then the walking programme.
  7. Education: stop all tobacco; foot care; diet; medicines (antiplatelet, statin).

Precautions

No heat or compression on an ischaemic limb; monitor heart rate and BP; diabetics check sugar and feet; stop for chest pain or sudden acute ischaemia (6 P's: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, perishing cold).
