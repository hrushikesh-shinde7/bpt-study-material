PEP mask and its use

A PEP mask is a face mask with a one-way valve, an expiratory resistor and a manometer. The patient breathes out against the resistance, keeping a positive pressure of 10–20 cmH₂O in the airways during expiration.

Principle: the back-pressure splints the small airways open. Air also enters behind the mucus through collateral channels, then pushes it towards the large airways on expiration.

Use: sit with elbows on a table and hold the mask firmly over the nose and mouth. Take a breath slightly deeper than normal, then breathe out actively, not forcefully. Do 8–10 breaths, remove the mask, then 2–3 huffs and a cough. Repeat for 15–20 min, once or twice a day.

Indications: CF, bronchiectasis, COPD with sputum, prevention of collapse after surgery. Main contraindication: untreated pneumothorax.

Acapella and Flutter devices and their use

Both are oscillating PEP devices. Breathing out through them gives positive expiratory pressure, which splints the airways open, plus a rapid vibration that shakes mucus off the airway walls and makes it less sticky.

Principle of PEP, with two PEP devices

Positive expiratory pressure (PEP): the patient breathes out against a resistance, so airway pressure stays above atmospheric throughout expiration (target 10–20 cmH₂O).

  • Splints the airways: back-pressure stops floppy small airways collapsing early, like pursed-lip breathing.
  • Gets air behind mucus: lung volume rises slightly and collateral channels fill; that air pushes the mucus centrally on expiration.
  • Huffs and a cough then clear it.

Two devices:

  1. PEP mask: face mask, one-way valve, fixed resistor and manometer; 8–10 breaths, then huffs.
  2. Flutter (oscillating PEP): a steel ball in a cone vibrates as the patient breathes out, adding oscillation to the PEP; used in sitting. (Alternatives: Acapella, which works in any position; bubble PEP, a tube in water where 10 cm depth ≈ 10 cmH₂O.)

Any three uses of a PEP device

  1. Airway clearance in chronic sputum: bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, COPD with sputum. It is quick, portable and independent, so it suits a daily home routine.
  2. Splinting unstable, collapsible airways (COPD, tracheobronchomalacia): back-pressure keeps the airways open during expiration and reduces air trapping, like pursed-lip breathing.
  3. Lung expansion after surgery: increases lung volume and helps prevent or reverse atelectasis.
  4. Better aerosol delivery when a nebuliser is added to the PEP mask.
  5. Children: bubble PEP turns breathing practice into play.

Avoid with an untreated pneumothorax, active haemoptysis, raised ICP, or recent face, skull or oesophageal surgery.

PEP devices: principle, classification and types

Positive expiratory pressure (PEP) therapy means breathing out against a resistance, so that airway pressure stays above atmospheric throughout expiration. It is an independent, portable airway clearance technique.

Principle

  1. Airway splinting: back-pressure stops floppy small airways closing early in expiration (as with pursed lips).
  2. Collateral ventilation: a slightly larger lung volume fills the alveoli behind the mucus through the pores of Kohn and other channels. On expiration that air pushes the mucus towards the large airways.
  3. Oscillation (oscillating devices): the pressure flutters on and off many times a second, shearing mucus off the walls and making it less sticky.
  4. Huffs and a cough then clear the mucus.

Classification

  • By oscillation: non-oscillating (PEP mask, high-pressure PEP) or oscillating (Flutter, Acapella, RC-Cornet, Aerobika, bubble PEP).
  • By pressure: low-pressure PEP (10–20 cmH₂O) or high-pressure PEP.
  • By position: gravity-dependent (Flutter, sitting only) or position-independent (Acapella, RC-Cornet, Aerobika).

Technique (PEP mask)

Sit with elbows on a table; seal the mask; breathe in slightly deeper than normal, then out actively, not forcefully, to the target pressure. After 8–10 breaths, remove the mask, do 2–3 huffs and a cough, and rest with breathing control. Repeat for about 15–20 min, once or twice a day.

Indications: bronchiectasis, CF, COPD with sputum, collapsible airways, after surgery. Contraindications: untreated pneumothorax (main); active haemoptysis; raised ICP; haemodynamic instability; recent face, skull or oesophageal surgery; ear problems or a perforated eardrum.

Choosing and precautions

  • Choose by condition, age, ability and cost, and what the patient will keep using; teach and check the technique.
  • Positive-pressure devices: no undrained pneumothorax; care with bullae, haemoptysis, recent face, skull or oesophageal surgery, raised ICP.
  • Clean after each use and never share devices (cross-infection, especially in CF).
