Neurophysiological facilitation of respiration

Neurophysiological facilitation (NPF) of respiration uses external proprioceptive and tactile stimuli (touch and pressure) to produce reflex changes in breathing: deeper breaths, more movement in a chosen area of the chest, or a change in rate. It was described by Bethune (1975) in unconscious adults.

For patients who cannot follow commands: unconscious or sedated patients (including ventilated patients who breathe on their own), head injury, stroke, confusion, young children.

Aims: deeper breathing and lung expansion, better basal and segmental ventilation, movement of secretions, better oxygenation.

TechniqueHowResponse
Perioral pressureFirm, maintained finger pressure on the upper lipMore upper-abdominal movement, deeper breaths, sighs; mouth closure and swallowing (sucking reflex)
Intercostal stretchPush the upper border of a rib down to widen the space above it; holdMore expansion in that area (intercostal stretch reflex)
Vertebral pressureFirm pressure on the spinous processes: upper (T2–T5) or lower (T7–T10) thoracicUpper: more abdominal movement and deeper breaths; lower: more apical movement

Dose: hold each stimulus through several breaths (30 s to 1 min) and repeat while it works; watch the breathing pattern, SpO₂ and heart rate.

Precautions: no pressure over fractured ribs, an unstable spine, osteoporosis, painful wounds or burns. Stop if the patient becomes agitated, HR or BP become unstable, or SpO₂ falls. With raised ICP, keep stimuli short and gentle.

Evidence: small studies; the effects last minutes, so combine NPF with positioning and other chest techniques.

PNF techniques for facilitation of respiration and lung expansion

Proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) uses proprioceptive input to improve muscle response. For breathing, the limb PNF principles (manual contacts, stretch, resistance, verbal commands, irradiation from strong patterns, timing and approximation) are applied to the breathing muscles in patients who can co-operate. Unlike NPF, which works by reflex in unconscious patients, the patient here actively joins in.

Techniques

1. Repeated quick stretch with resistanceHands on the lower lateral ribs (or upper abdomen). A quick stretch in and down at the end of expiration, then light resistance through inspiration, re-stretched during it ("push my hands out"). The stretch reflex gives more expansion under the hands.
2. Bilateral D2 flexion with inspirationArms move up and out (D2 flexion) while breathing in, and back (D2 extension) while breathing out; can be resisted. Irradiation from the arms expands the upper chest.
3. Trunk lift and chopLift (diagonal upwards with extension and rotation) while breathing in; chop (diagonal downwards with flexion) while breathing out or coughing.
4. Scapular posterior elevationSide-lying; resisted posterior elevation of the scapula during inspiration expands the upper and lateral chest.
5. Rhythmic initiationThe breathing rhythm moves from passive to active-assisted, active and then resisted, teaching a co-ordinated pattern.
6. Approximation; timing for emphasisRib compression at the end of expiration; holding a strong pattern to direct expansion to a weak area.

For "three techniques" (W24): write repeated quick stretch with resistance, bilateral D2 flexion with inspiration, and trunk lift and chop, each with method and effect.

Effects

Greater chest expansion and tidal volume in the targeted area; re-expansion of collapsed basal lung; better co-ordination and strength of the breathing muscles; a stronger cough after chop patterns.

Indications

Weak or unco-ordinated breathing (stroke, spinal cord injury, neuromuscular disease); low expansion after surgery or an ICU stay; basal collapse; poor chest mobility.

Dose: a few repetitions of each technique with breathing control between them, repeated through the day while the response is good; stop if the patient becomes dizzy or tired.

Precautions: rib fractures, osteoporosis, wounds and drains under the hands, pain, unstable cardiovascular status.
