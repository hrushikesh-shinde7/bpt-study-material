Metabolic equivalent (MET): definition, value and METs of daily activities

MET: the energy used while sitting quietly at rest, taken as oxygen consumption of 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min (about 1 kcal/kg/h). An activity of 4 METs uses four times the resting energy.

Uses: Phase I cardiac rehab stays at about 3 METs or less; 7 METs or more means good capacity; sexual activity is usually safe once 3–5 METs are tolerated; return-to-activity advice.

Three differences between aerobic and anaerobic exercise

Aerobic exercise is sustained, rhythmic activity in which ATP comes mainly from oxidative metabolism (walking, cycling). Anaerobic exercise is short, intense activity in which ATP comes mainly from creatine phosphate and glycolysis without oxygen (sprinting, heavy lifting).

Cardio-respiratory changes with ageing, and a fitness programme

Ageing gradually lowers the reserve of the heart, lungs and muscles. Inactivity speeds the decline, and exercise slows it at any age.

Changes with ageing

Heart and vessels↓ maximum heart rate; stiffer arteries, so ↑ systolic BP; slower HR response to exercise; ↓ maximal cardiac output and VO₂max (faster in inactive people); more postural hypotension
Lungs↓ elastic recoil; stiffer chest wall (costal cartilage calcification, kyphosis); ↑ residual volume, ↓ vital capacity; airways close earlier; weaker breathing muscles; slightly lower PaO₂; weaker cough
Muscle and boneSarcopenia (fast fibres lost first), bone loss, stiff joints, poorer balance and falls
OtherPoorer heat tolerance; more chronic disease and medicines

Before starting

Screen for heart disease and other conditions, medicines (beta-blockers blunt HR), falls risk, and joint problems; measure resting HR and BP, 6MWT or a step test, and balance.

Principles: start low and go slow; longer warm-up and cool-down (10 min each); progress duration before intensity; use RPE with beta-blockers; avoid breath-holding in strength work; good footwear, hydration; exercise in a group for motivation.

Benefits: higher VO₂max, lower BP, better strength, balance and bone density, fewer falls, better mood, independence and quality of life.

Methods of measuring exercise intensity

Intensity is how hard the patient works. It is the key part of the exercise prescription (FITT-VP) that decides both benefit and safety.

Methods

  1. Heart rate reserve (Karvonen): target HR = resting HR + intensity × (maximum HR − resting HR). Maximum HR = 220 − age (or 208 − 0.7 × age). Example: age 60, resting HR 70 → maximum 160, reserve 90; at 50%, target = 70 + 45 = 115.
  2. Percentage of maximum heart rate (simpler, less accurate).
  3. Oxygen uptake: % of VO₂max or VO₂ reserve, measured by a cardiopulmonary exercise test (the gold standard).
  4. METs: energy cost of the activity compared with rest.
  5. Rating of perceived exertion: Borg 6–20 (12–13 = moderate) or CR10 (3–5 in pulmonary rehab).
  6. Talk test: can talk but not sing = moderate.
  7. Field-test speed: walking at about 80% of the 6MWT or shuttle speed.
  8. Thresholds: ventilatory or lactate threshold; in angina, train at least 10 beats below the ischaemic threshold.
  9. Strength: % of one-repetition maximum (1RM).

Special cases: with beta-blockers, atrial fibrillation, pacemakers or a heart transplant, heart rate does not reflect effort, so use RPE and the talk test. In lung disease, breathlessness (CR10) and SpO₂ often limit before heart rate does.

Combine an objective measure (HR, METs) with a subjective one (RPE, talk test), and adjust as fitness improves.

Types of energy systems, with examples

Muscles contract using ATP, but they store only enough for a few seconds. Three energy systems remake ATP. They work together all the time; which one leads depends on how hard and how long the effort is.

SystemHow it worksDurationExamples
1. ATP–PC (phosphagen)Creatine phosphate quickly remakes ATP; no oxygen needed; very fast but tiny storeMaximal efforts up to about 10 s100 m sprint, a jump, a single heavy lift, a fast bowler's delivery
2. Anaerobic glycolysis (lactic acid system)Glucose and glycogen broken down without oxygen to lactate; fast but little ATP; lactate and acidity cause fatigueHard efforts of about 10 s to 2 min400 m run, 100 m swim, several flights of stairs climbed fast
3. Aerobic (oxidative)Carbohydrate and fat (some protein) fully broken down with oxygen in the mitochondria; slow but large ATP yield; by-products CO₂ and waterEfforts longer than about 2 min, and all restWalking, jogging, cycling, marathon, most daily activities

Contribution against time: ATP–PC peaks in the first 10 s, glycolysis from 10 s to 2 min, aerobic rising to dominate after 2 min.

Fuel use

At rest and in low-intensity exercise, fat supplies most of the energy; carbohydrate takes over as intensity rises. Respiratory quotient: carbohydrate 1.0, fat 0.7.

Training each system

  • ATP–PC: short maximal bursts with full rest (sprints, plyometrics).
  • Glycolytic: high-intensity intervals of 30 s to 2 min (lactate tolerance).
  • Aerobic: continuous or interval endurance training; more capillaries, mitochondria and VO₂max.

Physio relevance: cardiac and pulmonary rehab mainly train the aerobic system. Interval training lets breathless patients work harder in short bouts. Patients with heart or lung disease reach the anaerobic threshold early, so lactate, breathlessness and fatigue appear at low workloads.
