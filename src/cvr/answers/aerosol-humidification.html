Humidification and its uses

Humidification is adding water vapour to inspired gas to warm and moisten it. Normally the nose does this, so that a few centimetres below the carina air is at 37 °C, 100% relative humidity, about 44 mg H₂O/L (the isothermic saturation boundary).

Methods: heat and moisture exchanger (HME, "artificial nose"; passive, ≥ 30 mg/L); heated humidifier (active, 33–44 mg/L at 34–41 °C); bubble humidifier (little real humidity); saline nebulisation.

Uses:

  1. Intubated and tracheostomy patients (the nose is bypassed).
  2. Oxygen at high flows or for long periods; high-flow nasal oxygen; NIV.
  3. Thick, sticky secretions: to loosen sputum before airway clearance.
  4. Dry, irritated airways.

Hazards: overheating and airway burns, water collecting in the tubing (washed into the lungs, infection), contamination, extra resistance and dead space with an HME.

Need for humidification in an intubated patient

Normally the nose and upper airway warm and moisten inspired air. An endotracheal or tracheostomy tube bypasses the nose, so cold, dry gas reaches the lower airways. This causes:

  • Thick, sticky secretions that are hard to suction and can block the tube.
  • Slowed cilia and damage to the airway lining, so mucus clearance fails.
  • Atelectasis, infection, and loss of heat and water.

AARC 2012: humidify every invasively ventilated patient.

  • HME (≥ 30 mg/L): short-term ventilation. Avoid with thick, copious or bloody secretions, a large leak, hypothermia, during nebulisation, or with low tidal volumes (dead space).
  • Heated humidifier (33–44 mg/L at 34–41 °C): long ventilation, thick secretions.

For the physio: thinner secretions make suction and airway clearance easier.

Define humidification and nebulisation

Humidification: adding water vapour (water as a gas) to inspired gas to warm and moisten it, as the nose normally does. It is used for intubated and tracheostomy patients, high-flow oxygen and thick secretions. Devices: HME, heated humidifier.

Nebulisation: turning a liquid (usually a drug solution) into an aerosol of fine droplets to be breathed in. Droplets of 1–5 µm reach the small airways and alveoli. Used for bronchodilators, hypertonic saline, antibiotics. Devices: jet, ultrasonic and vibrating-mesh nebulisers.

Aerosol: tiny liquid or solid particles suspended in a gas.

Difference: humidification adds invisible water vapour (molecules); nebulisation produces visible droplets that carry a drug or saline to a target in the airway.

Metered-dose inhalers

A metered-dose inhaler (MDI) is a pressurised canister holding the drug with a propellant. A metering valve releases a fixed dose with each press (for example salbutamol 100 µg per puff).

Technique:

  1. Remove the cap and shake; breathe out fully, away from the inhaler.
  2. Seal the lips around the mouthpiece.
  3. Start a slow, deep breath in (3–5 s) and press once at the start.
  4. Hold the breath for up to 10 s; wait 30–60 s before a second puff; rinse the mouth after steroids.

Advantages: small, quick, cheap. Disadvantages: needs co-ordination of press and breath; much drug lands in the throat; the cold spray can make the patient stop breathing in.

Spacer: puff into the chamber and breathe it in over a few breaths, so less co-ordination is needed and less drug lands in the throat (with a mask for young children). In acute asthma an MDI with a spacer works as well as a nebuliser.

Principle of working of jet and ultrasonic nebulisers

A nebuliser converts a liquid drug into an aerosol of fine droplets that the patient breathes in. Droplet size decides where it lands: over 10 µm in the mouth and throat; 5–10 µm in the large airways; 1–5 µm in the small airways and alveoli (respirable); under 1 µm breathed out again.

Jet (pneumatic) nebuliser

Parts: compressor or oxygen/air supply, tubing, drug cup with a fine feed tube, a narrow jet opening, a baffle, and a mouthpiece or mask.

Principle (Bernoulli or Venturi effect):

  1. Compressed gas at about 6–8 L/min rushes through a narrow opening, so its speed rises and its pressure falls.
  2. The low pressure draws liquid up the feed tube from the cup.
  3. The fast gas stream shatters the liquid into droplets.
  4. A baffle knocks out the large droplets, which fall back into the cup; the small ones are carried to the patient.

Gas jet → narrow opening → liquid drawn up the feed tube → droplets hit the baffle → fine mist to the mouthpiece.

Ultrasonic nebuliser

Principle (piezoelectric effect):

  1. Electricity makes a piezoelectric crystal vibrate at ultrasonic frequency.
  2. The vibration passes into the liquid and sets up waves on its surface.
  3. The crests of these waves break off as fine droplets.
  4. The patient's breath (or a fan) carries the mist out.

Newer: the vibrating-mesh nebuliser pushes liquid through a plate with tiny holes. It is efficient, quiet and portable, and is used in-line on ventilators, but it is expensive.

Technique: sit up; 2–5 mL in the cup; normal mouth breathing with occasional deep breaths until it splutters; wash and dry the parts after use.

Nebulisation: indications, contraindications and types of nebulisation systems

Nebulisation turns a liquid drug into an aerosol of fine droplets to be breathed in. Droplets of 1–5 µm reach the small airways and alveoli. Larger droplets settle in the mouth and large airways; smaller ones are breathed out.

Interfaces: mouthpiece (preferred: less drug lost to the face and eyes); mask for children and very breathless patients; in-line in a ventilator circuit; with a PEP mask.

Indications

  • Acute asthma or COPD exacerbation: bronchodilators (salbutamol, ipratropium).
  • CF and bronchiectasis: hypertonic saline or dornase alfa before airway clearance; inhaled antibiotics.
  • Ventilated patients: in-line bronchodilators.
  • Croup: adrenaline or steroid.
  • Sputum induction for TB and other tests.
  • Patients who cannot use inhalers (very young, very ill, poor co-ordination).

Contraindications and precautions

  • Allergy to the drug.
  • Hypertonic saline can cause bronchospasm: give a bronchodilator first and check peak flow with the first dose.
  • COPD with CO₂ retention: drive the nebuliser with air, not oxygen, and give oxygen by cannula (BTS).
  • Side effects: fast heart rate and tremor (salbutamol), low potassium with frequent high doses.
  • Infection: aerosol spreads infection; clean and dry the parts; never share.

Technique: sit upright; 2–5 mL of solution; normal mouth breathing with occasional deep breaths until the nebuliser splutters; rinse the mouth after steroids. Physio: time bronchodilators and hypertonic saline before airway clearance and inhaled antibiotics after it.
