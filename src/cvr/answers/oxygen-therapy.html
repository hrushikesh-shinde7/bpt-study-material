Methods of delivery of oxygen therapy

Low-flow (variable performance) devices deliver less than the patient's inspiratory flow, so room air mixes in and FiO₂ varies. High-flow (fixed performance) devices supply the whole inspiratory flow, so FiO₂ is fixed.

Hazards of oxygen therapy

  1. CO₂ retention in at-risk patients (COPD, obesity, chest wall and neuromuscular disease), mainly from worsened V/Q matching and the Haldane effect (loss of hypoxic drive plays a small part): drowsiness, confusion, acidosis.
  2. Absorption atelectasis: nitrogen is washed out and the oxygen is absorbed, so poorly ventilated alveoli collapse.
  3. Oxygen toxicity with FiO₂ above about 0.6 for long periods; retinopathy of prematurity in preterm babies.
  4. Fire: no smoking or open flames (including the chulha); no grease on fittings.
  5. Masking deterioration: a good SpO₂ can hide worsening breathing; watch the respiratory rate.
  6. Local: dry nose, nosebleeds, pressure sores on the ears and nose, mask claustrophobia, aspiration if vomiting into a mask; tripping on tubing.

Prevention: prescribe oxygen to a target SpO₂: 94–98% for most patients, 88–92% for those at risk of CO₂ retention.

Low-flow oxygen therapy

Oxygen is a drug, prescribed with a target SpO₂ to correct or prevent hypoxaemia. Low-flow (variable-performance) oxygen therapy delivers oxygen at a flow lower than the patient's own inspiratory flow. The rest of each breath is room air, so the FiO₂ the patient actually receives varies with the breathing pattern.

(An oxygen catheter appears in older books.) By contrast, the Venturi mask and high-flow nasal oxygen are fixed-performance systems.

Targets

Most acutely ill adults: 94–98%. At risk of CO₂ retention (COPD, obesity, chest wall or neuromuscular disease, CF): 88–92%, guided by ABG.

Indications

Mild to moderate hypoxaemia in stable patients (pneumonia, heart failure, after surgery); home and ambulatory oxygen; long-term oxygen therapy; desaturation on exercise. Not for breathless patients whose SpO₂ is normal.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: simple, cheap, comfortable; the patient can eat, talk and walk.
  • Disadvantages: FiO₂ is not exact (higher with slow, shallow breathing; lower with fast breathing), so it suits less well when CO₂ must be controlled; dry nose.

Humidification: not needed at low flows; consider it for higher flows over about 24 h, a tracheostomy, or dryness.

Monitoring and hazards

SpO₂ against the target, respiratory rate, drowsiness (rising CO₂), ABG if at risk. Hazards: CO₂ retention, absorption atelectasis, oxygen toxicity, fire, pressure sores, masking of deterioration.

Physiotherapy role

Check the prescription and target; never change oxygen without it. Keep oxygen on during clearance and positioning; check SpO₂ during walking and keep it above about 88–90% in exercise training (portable cylinder or concentrator). Teach long-term oxygen users: at least 15 hours a day, no smoking, keep the cannula clean, pace activities.
