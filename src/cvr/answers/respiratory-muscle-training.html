Two types of inspiratory muscle training

Inspiratory muscle training (IMT) strengthens the breathing muscles by making the patient breathe in against a load (progressive overload).

  1. Threshold loading: a spring-loaded valve opens only when the patient produces a set inspiratory pressure. The load does not depend on how fast the patient breathes. It is easy to set as a percentage of maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), and is the most used type.
  2. Inspiratory resistive loading: breathing in through a small opening. The load depends on flow, so the patient must keep a set breathing pattern (targeted devices show this).

Others: normocapnic hyperpnoea (endurance training), tapered-flow resistive devices.

Dose: start at ≥ 30% of MIP, about 30 breaths twice a day, 5–7 days a week for at least 6–8 weeks. Used in COPD with weak inspiratory muscles, heart failure, before high-risk surgery, weaning failure.

Therapeutic exercise programme to strengthen the respiratory muscles

The breathing muscles may be weak (neuromuscular disease, spinal cord injury, after ventilation, steroids) or overloaded (stiff lungs, obstruction, hyperinflation). This causes breathlessness, poor exercise tolerance, a weak cough and weaning failure. Like limb muscles, they respond to progressive overload.

Assessment

MIP and MEP with a mouth pressure meter (MIP stronger than about −80 cmH₂O rules out important weakness); breathing pattern, chest expansion, peak cough flow, Borg and mMRC scores, posture.

Programme

  1. Diaphragmatic breathing with facilitation (quick stretch, sniff) and manual resistance; half-lying → sitting → standing.
  2. Threshold IMT at the dose below.
  3. Expiratory muscles: expiratory threshold training and huffing if the cough is weak; abdominal strengthening (bracing, curl-ups as allowed).
  4. Accessory and postural muscles: scapular and upper-limb exercise (arm ergometer, resisted PNF patterns); thoracic mobility.
  5. Whole-body aerobic training: walking or cycling; the extra breathing it demands also trains the breathing muscles.
  6. Endurance: normocapnic hyperpnoea; for children, singing and blowing games or wind instruments.

Dose of threshold IMT (FITT)

Frequency5–7 days a week, twice a day, for at least 6–8 weeks, then maintenance
IntensityStart at about 30% of MIP; progress to 50–60% or more; re-measure MIP every 1–2 weeks
TimeAbout 30 breaths (or 15 min) per session, in sets with rests
TypeUpright; nose clip if needed; quick, strong breath in through the device, slow relaxed breath out

Evidence: IMT alone improves strength, endurance, breathlessness and walking, most in weak patients. Added to full pulmonary rehabilitation it raises strength but not exercise capacity, so it is an add-on, not a replacement for whole-body exercise. It helps in weaning failure.

Precautions

Recent or untreated pneumothorax; unhealed chest surgery; acute COPD or asthma exacerbation; unstable heart failure or recent MI; perforated eardrum; raised ICP; active haemoptysis. Stop with fatigue (harmful in neuromuscular disease), dizziness or undue breathlessness.

Review every 1–2 weeks: MIP, Borg, 6MWT, symptoms; adjust the load.
