Two special tests to assess venous insufficiency

Venous insufficiency is failure of the leg vein valves, so blood refluxes and venous pressure stays high. Special tests show which valves have failed.

1. Brodie–Trendelenburg test

  1. Patient lying; raise the leg to empty the veins.
  2. Press over the saphenofemoral junction (or apply a tourniquet high on the thigh).
  3. Stand the patient up and watch the veins.

Result: veins stay empty and then fill quickly when the pressure is released = saphenofemoral valve incompetent. Veins fill quickly with the pressure still on = perforators below are incompetent. Slow filling (over about 30 s) with pressure on = normal.

2. Perthes test

  1. Patient standing; tourniquet below the knee to block the superficial veins.
  2. Patient walks or does heel raises.

Result: varicose veins empty = deep veins are open and the calf pump works. Veins swell and the leg hurts = deep veins blocked: do not strip the superficial veins.

Other tests

  • Tap (percussion) test: tap the vein at the knee and feel at the groin; an impulse felt above means incompetent valves between.
  • Cough impulse: a thrill at the saphenofemoral junction on coughing means an incompetent junction.
  • Duplex ultrasound is the gold standard; ABPI must be checked before compression.

Physiotherapy and management of venous (varicose) ulcers

A venous ulcer is a break in the skin of the lower leg, usually above the medial malleolus (gaiter area), caused by venous hypertension, unhealed for about 2 weeks. It is shallow, moist, with an irregular edge, surrounded by pigmentation, oedema and hard skin; pulses are present.

Assessment

Ulcer site, size (tracing or photo), base, edge, exudate, infection; oedema and calf girth; varicose veins; ABPI; ankle range and calf strength (single-leg heel raises); sensation if diabetic; standing hours at work.

Management

  1. Compression (the main treatment): about 40 mmHg at the ankle with multilayer bandages or wraps if ABPI ≥ 0.8; reduced compression at 0.5–0.8 under supervision; none below 0.5.
  2. Calf muscle pump exercise with the bandage on: ankle pumps 10–20 every hour; heel raises 2–3 sets of 10–15; progressive resistance calf exercise helps healing.
  3. Walking 30 min a day in compression.
  4. Ankle range of motion: a stiff ankle stops the pump working.
  5. Elevation above heart level for 30 min, 3–4 times a day; foot of the bed slightly raised.
  6. Skin care: clean, moisturise the surrounding skin, avoid scratching and injury; simple dressings by the team; antibiotics only for spreading infection.
  7. Electrotherapy as an add-on for slow ulcers: electrical stimulation, therapeutic ultrasound, ultraviolet for infected ulcers.
  8. Treat the underlying vein (ablation) to speed healing and prevent recurrence.

Education and prevention of recurrence

Lifelong stockings, daily walking and calf exercises, avoid long standing and sitting, healthy weight, report new wounds early.

Precautions: never compress without ABPI; loosen if toes turn pale, blue, cold or numb; no heat on the leg.

Prevention and physiotherapy of deep vein thrombosis

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a clot in a deep vein, usually of the calf or thigh; a piece may travel to the lungs as a pulmonary embolism (PE). Causes follow Virchow's triad: stasis (bed rest, surgery, long travel, paralysis), vessel wall injury (trauma, fractures, surgery) and hypercoagulability (cancer, pregnancy, the pill, dehydration).

Features and diagnosis

One-sided calf or thigh swelling, pain, warmth, tenderness; often silent after surgery. Wells score, D-dimer and duplex ultrasound (Homans' sign is unreliable and not used).

Prevention (any immobile or post-operative patient)

  • Early mobilisation and walking.
  • Ankle pumps 10 every hour while awake; static quadriceps and gluteal sets; deep breathing.
  • Hydration; no crossed legs or pillows behind the knees.
  • Mechanical prophylaxis as prescribed: graduated compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression; heparin as prescribed.

Physiotherapy after a DVT is diagnosed

  • Check the anticoagulant and when it started; begin walking once anticoagulation is therapeutic (bed rest does not lower PE risk).
  • Progress walking and leg exercise; elevate the leg at rest; compression for persistent swelling and pain.
  • Gentle handling (bleeding risk); no massage or heat on the affected limb.

Stop and call for help: sudden breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, fast heart rate, coughing blood (PE).

Patient education in varicose veins

Varicose veins are dilated, twisted superficial leg veins (3 mm or more) from failed valves. Standing raises the pressure; walking pumps it down. Education aims to lower venous pressure, prevent ulcers and keep the patient working.

Do

  • Walk every day; do ankle pumps and heel raises whenever standing or sitting for long.
  • Raise the legs above heart level several times a day (about 30 min, 3–4 times); sleep with the foot of the bed slightly raised.
  • Wear compression stockings from morning till night (put on before getting up); check the skin daily.
  • At work: short sitting or walking breaks, shift weight from foot to foot, use a footrest.
  • Keep a healthy weight; wear comfortable low-heeled shoes; moisturise the lower legs.

Don't

  • Stand or sit still for long periods; cross the legs.
  • Wear tight garters, tight sock bands or tight waistbands.
  • Use hot-water bags or sit close to a fire with bare legs.
  • Scratch itchy skin or injure the legs.

Foot and skin care (especially with diabetes)

Inspect feet daily; wash in lukewarm water and dry well; cut nails straight; never walk barefoot; report any wound, colour change or swelling early.

Warning signs

A bleeding vein (lie down, raise the leg, press firmly); a red, hot, tender cord (thrombophlebitis); sudden calf swelling (DVT); sudden breathlessness (PE): seek help at once.

Assessment of a patient with venous insufficiency

Subjective

Job and hours of standing; leg aching, heaviness, cramps, itching, worse by evening and relieved by elevation; swelling pattern; previous DVT, pregnancies, surgery; diabetes, smoking; home stairs; effect on work and daily life.

Observation (standing)

Varicose veins and their distribution; oedema; skin changes: pigmentation, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche; ulcer (site, size, depth, base, edge, exudate, smell). Grade by CEAP (C0 none to C6 active ulcer).

Measurement and palpation

Calf and ankle circumference on both sides; ulcer tracing or photo; temperature, pitting oedema, tenderness; pulses (to exclude arterial disease).

Special tests

  • Brodie–Trendelenburg test: saphenofemoral or perforator incompetence.
  • Perthes test: patency of deep veins.
  • Tap test, cough impulse; ABPI before any compression; duplex ultrasound report.

Function

Ankle dorsiflexion range; calf strength (number of single-leg heel raises); gait; sensation with a 10 g monofilament if diabetic.

Outcome measures

Venous Clinical Severity Score, ulcer area, pain scale, quality of life (CIVIQ).

The findings give the impairments (aching, oedema, weak calf pump, skin risk) and decide whether compression is safe.
