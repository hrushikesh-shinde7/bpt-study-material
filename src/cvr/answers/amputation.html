Characteristics of an ideal stump

  • Adequate length: transtibial about 12–15 cm below the knee joint line; transfemoral about 25 cm from the greater trochanter (room for a prosthetic knee).
  • Conical (cylindrical) shape, firm, not bulbous or flabby.
  • Well-healed, mobile scar that is not adherent and not over the weight-bearing end.
  • Good soft-tissue padding; bone ends smooth and bevelled (fibula slightly shorter than the tibia).
  • Muscles anchored to bone (myodesis), so they stay strong.
  • Good circulation and sensation; no neuroma or painful spots.
  • Full range of the joint above, with no contracture.

Physiotherapy after below-knee amputation

A below-knee (transtibial) amputation keeps the knee, which makes prosthetic walking far easier. Common causes: diabetes and PAD (gangrene), trauma.

Assessment

Cause, side, day; diabetes, BP, heart; stump (length, shape, circumference, wound, scar, sensation, pain, neuroma); hip and knee range (contracture); strength of both legs, trunk and arms; the other leg (pulses, skin, sensation); balance, transfers; phantom sensation and pain; job and home (steps, toilet).

Pre-prosthetic phase

  • Chest and circulation: breathing exercises, ankle pumps of the other leg, early sitting out.
  • Positioning: knee straight; no pillow under the stump or knee; stump board in the wheelchair; prone lying 20–30 min, 2–3 times a day.
  • Oedema and shaping: figure-of-eight elastic bandage (10 cm) or a shrinker sock once healed.
  • Exercises: isometric then active quadriceps, hip extensors and abductors; full exercise of the other leg, trunk and arms (for crutches).
  • Function: bed mobility, transfers, wheelchair skills, standing balance in parallel bars, short hops with a walker.
  • Phantom pain: tapping, massage, rubbing with textures, mirror therapy, TENS; reassurance.

Prosthetic phase

  • Prosthesis (PTB or total-surface-bearing socket; SACH or Jaipur foot) once the stump is healed and its volume stable.
  • Donning and doffing; daily skin checks; build wearing time gradually.
  • Weight shifting → standing → stepping → walking with fewer aids; correct gait deviations; stairs (up with the sound leg, down with the prosthesis), ramps, kerbs, uneven ground, getting up from the floor.

Return to work and education

Work-specific training (driving with adapted controls, door-to-door walking); daily stump and other-foot care; control sugar and BP; stop smoking; keep a steady weight. Outcome measures: AMPPRO, Timed Up and Go, 2- or 6-minute walk, K-level.

Stump management after below-knee amputation

Aims: heal the wound, control oedema, shape the stump into a firm cone for the socket, prevent contracture and pain, and keep the skin healthy.

1. Stump bandaging

  • Elastic bandage about 10 cm (4 in) wide for a transtibial stump (15 cm for transfemoral).
  • Diagonal, figure-of-eight turns, no wrinkles; pressure firmest at the end, decreasing towards the knee; no circular turns (tourniquet).
  • Anchor above the knee; leave the knee free to bend.
  • Re-apply every 4–6 hours and whenever loose; worn day and night except for bathing and exercise.
  • A shrinker sock once the wound has healed (easier, more even pressure).

2. Positioning against knee flexion contracture

Knee straight; no pillow under the stump or knee; stump not hanging over the bed edge; stump board in the wheelchair; prone lying 20–30 min, 2–3 times a day.

3. Exercises

Isometric quadriceps from day 1, then active knee extension and hip extensor and abductor strengthening; stretches to keep full knee extension.

4. Pain and sensation

Desensitisation (tapping, massage, rubbing with different textures), mirror therapy and TENS for phantom pain; find causes of stump pain (infection, neuroma, bony end, tight bandage).

5. Skin care

Inspect daily with a mirror; wash with mild soap and dry well; scar massage once healed; report redness, blisters or discharge.

Precautions: check the wound before bandaging; vascular stumps heal slowly; never leave a tight bandage unchecked overnight.
