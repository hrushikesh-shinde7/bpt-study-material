Characteristics of deep partial-thickness burns

A deep partial-thickness (deep second-degree) burn destroys the epidermis and the deep dermis; only some hair follicles and sweat glands survive.

  • Look: blotchy red and white, drier than a superficial burn; blisters may be absent or broken.
  • Blanching: slow or absent capillary refill on pressure.
  • Sensation: less painful; pressure is felt more than pain, because many nerve endings are destroyed.
  • Healing: takes more than 3 weeks; high risk of hypertrophic scar and contracture; often excised and grafted.
  • Physio relevance: anti-deformity positioning and splinting from day 1, early ROM, then scar management.

Rule of nines for burns evaluation

Wallace's rule of nines estimates the burnt area as a percentage of total body surface area (TBSA) in adults.

  • Head and neck 9%; each upper limb 9%.
  • Front of trunk 18%; back of trunk 18%.
  • Each lower limb 18%; perineum 1%.
  • The patient's own palm with fingers ≈ 1% (small or scattered burns).
  • Children: the head is bigger and legs smaller, so use the Lund and Browder chart.
  • Count only partial- and full-thickness burns, not first-degree.

Define inhalational burns

An inhalation injury is damage to the airway and lungs from breathing in hot gases, smoke or toxic products of burning, usually in a fire in a closed space.

  • Upper airway: heat injury causes swelling that can block the airway within hours.
  • Lower airways and alveoli: smoke chemicals cause bronchospasm, sloughing, collapse and pneumonia.
  • Systemic: carbon monoxide and cyanide poisoning stop oxygen delivery and use.

Suspect it with burns in a closed space, face and neck burns, singed nasal hair, soot in the mouth, black sputum, hoarse voice or stridor, and confusion.

Any three complications of inhalation burns

  1. Upper airway oedema and obstruction: stridor, hoarse voice; may need early intubation.
  2. Carbon monoxide (and cyanide) poisoning: confusion, drowsiness, tissue hypoxia; pulse oximetry reads falsely normal, so check carboxyhaemoglobin on the ABG.
  3. Lower airway injury: bronchospasm, sloughing and mucus casts → atelectasis and pneumonia.

Also ARDS and respiratory failure. Physio: upright positioning, humidified oxygen, airway clearance, early mobilisation; report rising breathing rate or falling SpO₂.

Common deformities in burns and anti-deformity positioning to prevent them

Why deformities form: deep burns heal by scar, and myofibroblasts keep the scar contracting for months. Pain makes the patient rest in the position of comfort (flexion and adduction), so the scar shortens in that position. "The position of comfort is the position of contracture": position the opposite way, stretching the burnt surface.

How to apply

  • Start from day 1; use pillows, foam wedges, slings and splints; keep burnt limbs elevated to control oedema.
  • Combine with active ROM of every burnt joint hourly while awake; analgesia before sessions.
  • Splints at rest and at night; remove for exercise, dressing and skin checks; re-check fit daily as swelling changes.
  • After grafting, immobilise the graft in the anti-deformity position for about 3–5 days.
  • Continue in rehabilitation with night splints and stretching while the scar matures (1–2 years).

Common deformities

Scar contracts into the position of comfort: neck flexion; shoulder adduction and internal rotation; elbow flexion; claw hand (dorsal burns) or flexed fingers (palm); trunk flexion; hip flexion and adduction; knee flexion; ankle plantarflexion; microstomia and eyelid ectropion. Also hypertrophic scars and keloids, and boutonnière deformity when the extensor hood is exposed.

Prevention: anti-deformity positioning and splints from day 1, hourly ROM, stretching and pressure garments in rehabilitation.

Role of splinting in burn injuries

A splint is an orthosis (usually thermoplastic) that holds a joint or body part in a chosen position. In burns it is part of anti-deformity care, with positioning and exercise.

Aims

  1. Hold the anti-deformity position when the patient cannot (pain, sedation, sleep), against scar contraction.
  2. Protect exposed tendons and joints (finger extensor hood: splint straight) and fresh grafts (immobilise about 3–5 days).
  3. Keep the range gained by exercise, especially at night.
  4. Correct established contractures later (dynamic splints, serial casting).

Wearing schedule

Precautions

Check fit and pressure areas daily (swelling changes the fit); pad bony points; watch for sores, nerve compression and maceration; keep splints clean; never let a splint replace active exercise.

Management of burns after skin grafting

Deep burns are excised and covered with a split-thickness skin graft (donor site often the thigh; meshed to cover more area); full-thickness grafts and flaps are used for the face, hands and joints. The graft must not move or shear until it "takes".

Phase 1: immobilisation (about day 0–5)

  • Immobilise the grafted area about 3–5 days (the surgeon decides), in the anti-deformity position with a splint.
  • Elevate grafted limbs; move all other joints actively; breathing exercises; ankle pumps against DVT.
  • The donor site is painful but can usually be moved early.

Phase 2: graft take (about day 5–14)

  • Gentle active ROM over the graft once allowed; no friction or shear.
  • Leg grafts: wrap in a compression bandage before standing (dependent legs swell and grafts fail), then progressive walking.
  • Continue splints at rest and at night; self-care training.

Phase 3: healed graft, maturing scar (1–2 years)

  • Slow sustained stretches until the scar blanches, 20–30 s or longer, several times a day.
  • Pressure garments about 23 h a day for 12–18 months; silicone gel sheets; scar massage; moisturiser.
  • Strengthening, endurance, hand function, return to work; sun protection.

Precautions

No movement of a fresh graft until permitted; no shear or friction; report bleeding, lifting or infection of the graft; no heat on grafts; gentle handling of fragile new skin.

Inhalation burns and their management

Inhalation injury is damage to the airway and lungs from breathing in hot gases, smoke or toxic products of burning, usually in a closed space.

Pathophysiology

  • Upper airway: heat → swelling that worsens over the first day → obstruction.
  • Lower airway and alveoli: smoke chemicals → bronchospasm, sloughing, casts, collapse, pneumonia, ARDS.
  • Systemic: carbon monoxide and cyanide → tissue hypoxia.

Features and tests

Closed-space fire, face and neck burns, singed nasal hair, soot, black sputum, hoarseness, stridor, wheeze, confusion. ABG with carboxyhaemoglobin (SpO₂ falsely normal), chest X-ray (often normal at first), bronchoscopy.

Medical

Airway first, early intubation if swelling is likely; high-flow humidified oxygen (reservoir mask for CO); bronchodilators; ventilation if needed.

Physiotherapy

  1. Upright positioning to reduce airway swelling and ease breathing.
  2. Humidified oxygen as prescribed.
  3. Deep breathing, ACBT, huffing and assisted cough to clear sloughed material; suction when needed.
  4. Early mobilisation; limb exercises and anti-deformity positioning for skin burns.
  5. If ventilated: regular repositioning, airway clearance, careful bagging with suction.

Precautions

Watch for stridor, rising breathing rate and falling oxygen: call the team early. Do not trust SpO₂ in CO poisoning. Avoid vigorous percussion over burnt chest skin and fresh grafts.
