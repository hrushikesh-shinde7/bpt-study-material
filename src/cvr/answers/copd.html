Radiological findings of emphysema (X-ray findings of COPD)

  • Hyperinflated, hyperlucent (very black) lung fields with fewer vessel markings at the periphery.
  • Low, flattened diaphragms; more than 6 anterior or 10 posterior ribs visible above the diaphragm.
  • Ribs more horizontal; intercostal spaces wider.
  • Long, narrow ("tubular") heart shadow.
  • Increased air space behind the sternum on the lateral film; bullae may be seen.

Chronic bronchitis may show a near-normal film, more prominent markings ("dirty chest") or thick bronchial walls (tram lines). Cor pulmonale: large hilar arteries and an enlarged heart.

Physio meaning: flat diaphragm → forward-lean positions, pursed-lip breathing; bullae → no high-pressure techniques (pneumothorax risk).

Pulmonary function tests in obstructive lung disease and COPD

  • FEV1 reduced; FVC normal or slightly reduced.
  • FEV1/FVC below 0.70 after a bronchodilator (diagnostic of COPD).
  • Little reversibility after a bronchodilator (asthma shows a large rise: ≥ 12% and 200 mL).
  • Peak expiratory flow reduced; flow–volume loop with a "scooped" (concave) expiratory curve.
  • Lung volumes: RV, TLC and RV/TLC raised (air trapping); DLCO reduced in emphysema.

GOLD grade by FEV1 % predicted: 1 mild ≥ 80; 2 moderate 50–79; 3 severe 30–49; 4 very severe below 30.

Effects of smoking on pulmonary function

  1. Paralyses and destroys cilia: mucociliary clearance fails, mucus is retained.
  2. Enlarges mucus glands and goblet cells: more sputum (chronic bronchitis).
  3. Inflames and narrows the airways and makes them more reactive.
  4. Proteases destroy alveolar walls: emphysema, loss of elastic recoil, air trapping.
  5. FEV1 falls faster than with normal ageing; diffusing capacity falls.
  6. Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin and lowers oxygen carriage; infections become more common.

Pursed-lip breathing: effects and rationale in COPD

Technique: breathe in through the nose; breathe out slowly through loosely pursed lips (as if blowing on hot tea), taking about twice as long to breathe out as to breathe in, without forcing.

Rationale and effects:

  • Back-pressure splints the small airways open during expiration, so they do not collapse early (lost elastic recoil in COPD).
  • Less air trapping, so less hyperinflation and a better-shaped diaphragm.
  • Slower, deeper breathing: lower rate, larger tidal volume, better SpO₂.
  • Less breathlessness and anxiety; helps during walking and stair climbing.

Importance of peripheral muscle strengthening in COPD

  • COPD weakens and wastes limb muscles (inactivity, low oxygen, inflammation, steroids, poor nutrition).
  • Weak thighs tire early and make lactic acid sooner, which drives breathing harder; many patients stop exercise because of leg fatigue, not breathlessness.
  • Strength training increases muscle strength and size, improves exercise tolerance and daily tasks (stairs, standing up, carrying) and lowers breathlessness at the same workload.
  • It helps bone health and reduces falls, and causes less breathlessness than endurance training, so very breathless patients can do it.

Dose: 2–3 days a week, about 60–70% of 1RM, 8–12 repetitions, 1–3 sets, legs first.

Hypoxic drive in COPD

Classic theory: in chronic CO₂ retention the brain's chemoreceptors stop responding to CO₂, so breathing is driven mainly by low oxygen (the hypoxic drive). Giving high-concentration oxygen removes this drive, breathing slows and CO₂ rises (CO₂ narcosis).

Current understanding (BTS): reduced drive plays only a small part. Most of the CO₂ rise comes from worse ventilation–perfusion matching (oxygen opens vessels in poorly ventilated lung) and the Haldane effect (oxygenated haemoglobin carries less CO₂).

Practice: never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient; give controlled oxygen (Venturi mask 24–28% or low-flow cannula) to a target SpO₂ of 88–92%, and check ABG; watch for drowsiness.

Energy conservation techniques for COPD patients and their importance

Energy conservation means doing daily tasks in ways that use less energy and oxygen, so a breathless patient can do more with less breathlessness. Memory: the 4 Ps: Plan, Pace, Prioritise, Position.

PrincipleExamples
PlanSpread heavy jobs (washing clothes, cleaning, shopping) over the week; do them at the best time of day (often late morning, after airway clearance and medicines); gather everything before starting
PaceSlow, steady movement; rest before becoming very breathless; breathe out on the effort part ("blow as you go") when lifting, standing up, reaching
PrioritiseDrop or share tasks that are not essential; ask family to do heavy work
PositionSit to bathe, dress, iron and cook; keep things used often between waist and shoulder height; avoid bending, lifting and working with arms above the head

More techniques

  • Aids: trolley or bag on wheels, long-handled sponge, shower stool, raised toilet seat.
  • Breathing with activity: pursed-lip breathing while walking; stairs one step per breath out, resting on landings; plan trips to climb fewer times.
  • Bathing and dressing: sit; rest between washing and dressing; a dressing gown instead of towelling off.
  • Environment: avoid smoke, dust and extremes of temperature; loose clothes; a fan nearby.

Importance

  • Lowers the oxygen cost of daily tasks, so breathlessness and fatigue fall.
  • Keeps the patient independent in self-care and household roles.
  • Breaks the breathlessness–inactivity spiral and builds confidence; less anxiety and panic.
  • Allows more activity overall and supports the exercise programme of pulmonary rehabilitation.
