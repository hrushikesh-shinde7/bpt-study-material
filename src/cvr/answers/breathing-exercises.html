Three exercises to increase thoracic wall mobility

Thoracic mobility exercises combine trunk or arm movements with deep breathing. They free a stiff chest wall, spine and shoulder girdle, and improve ventilation of the stretched area.

  1. Trunk rotation with breathing: sitting, hands on the shoulders; turn to one side while breathing out, return to the centre while breathing in; 10 each way.
  2. Side-bending with the arm overhead: raise the arm on the tight side and bend away from it while breathing in deeply, which stretches and expands that side; return while breathing out. Used after thoracotomy, in pleural thickening, and on the concave side of a scoliosis.
  3. Bilateral arm elevation: arms up and out while breathing in (upper chest expands), down while breathing out.

Also: a pectoral stretch in a doorway, or thoracic extension over a rolled towel or the chair back, holding 20–30 s with deep breaths. Used in ankylosing spondylitis, kyphosis, scoliosis, after chest surgery or chest burns, and for COPD posture.

Diaphragmatic breathing exercises

Diaphragmatic breathing emphasises movement of the diaphragm (the upper abdomen rises) with a quiet upper chest and minimal accessory muscle work. It improves basal ventilation and the efficiency of breathing.

Technique:

  1. Half-lying with the head and knees supported (the diaphragm is domed and at a good length); shoulders relaxed.
  2. A hand on the upper abdomen just below the ribs.
  3. Breathe in slowly through the nose so the hand rises and the upper chest stays still; breathe out gently, relaxed, not forced.
  4. 5–10 breaths, several times a day; progress to sitting, standing, walking and stairs. A quick stretch at the end of expiration or a sniff helps recruit the diaphragm.

Uses: after surgery, neuromuscular disease and spinal cord injury, weaning, upper-chest breathing, anxiety. Caution: watch for dizziness from over-breathing. In severe COPD with a flat diaphragm, forced abdominal breathing can increase breathlessness; prefer pursed lips and positions of ease.

Diaphragm recruitment and strengthening

The diaphragm is the main muscle of inspiration (phrenic nerve, C3–C5). It is weakened or inhibited after upper abdominal and chest surgery (pain and reflex inhibition), in neuromuscular disease and spinal cord injury, after prolonged ventilation (disuse wasting), and when hyperinflation flattens it in COPD. Recruitment teaches the patient to use it again; strengthening applies progressive overload.

Assessment

Breathing pattern (upper-chest or paradoxical abdominal movement), abdominal rise on inspiration, chest expansion, vital capacity, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), SpO₂ and breathlessness.

Recruitment (facilitation)

  1. Position: half-lying with the knees bent and head and shoulders supported; progress to sitting, standing and walking. In tetraplegia, lying is easier (abdominal contents push the diaphragm into a dome); use an abdominal binder in sitting.
  2. Tactile cue: a hand on the upper abdomen; "breathe into my hand" while the upper chest stays quiet; relaxed breath out.
  3. Quick stretch: a quick push inwards and upwards on the upper abdomen at the end of expiration, then light resistance as the patient breathes in.
  4. Sniffing: short sniffs recruit the diaphragm strongly.
  5. In patients who cannot follow commands: NPF (perioral pressure, vertebral pressure, abdominal co-contraction).

Strengthening

  1. Manual resistance on the upper abdomen during inspiration, increased as the patient improves.
  2. Abdominal weights (classic method): small weights on the upper abdomen in supine, increased while the patient can still breathe without accessory muscles.
  3. Threshold inspiratory muscle training (best evidence): start at ≥ 30% of MIP, about 30 breaths twice a day, 5–7 days a week for at least 6–8 weeks; re-measure MIP and progress.
  4. Lung expansion: sustained maximal inspiration and incentive spirometry.
  5. Function: use the pattern in walking, stairs and speech; whole-body exercise.

Precautions

  • Severe COPD with a flat diaphragm: forced abdominal breathing can raise the work of breathing; use pursed lips and positions of ease, and stop if breathlessness increases.
  • Fatigue (harmful in neuromuscular disease), dizziness from over-breathing; support wounds after surgery; untreated pneumothorax.

Outcome measures: MIP, vital capacity, abdominal movement, SpO₂, Borg score.

Breathing retraining

Breathing retraining teaches a slower, calmer and more efficient breathing pattern to people whose breathing has become dysfunctional: fast, upper-chest, mouth breathing with frequent sighs, breath-holding or over-breathing (hyperventilation). The symptoms (breathlessness, chest tightness, dizziness, tingling, palpitations, fatigue) are out of proportion to any lung disease. It is common in asthma and anxiety. The Nijmegen questionnaire helps screen for it.

Programme

  1. Education: over-breathing washes out CO₂ and causes the symptoms; reassure that they are not dangerous.
  2. Posture and relaxation: supported positions, relaxed shoulders and jaw.
  3. Nose breathing at rest and in light activity: it warms, filters and slows the breath.
  4. Lower-chest breathing: a hand on the upper abdomen; quiet upper chest.
  5. Slower, smaller, gentler breaths with a short pause after the breath out.
  6. Control sighs and yawns (swallow or hold briefly instead); phrase speech so that breaths come at natural pauses.
  7. Integration: the same pattern while walking, on stairs, talking and exercising; breathing control in a position of ease during an attack.

Dose: practise 10–15 min once or twice a day, then use it in daily activities; a few sessions over several weeks with home practice.

Methods

  • Papworth method: integrated breathing and relaxation training.
  • Buteyko-based methods: add reduced-breathing exercises.
  • In COPD the same ideas appear as breathing control, pursed-lip breathing, positions of ease and pacing.

Evidence

Breathing retraining improves asthma quality of life and symptoms (Cochrane 2020). A 2018 UK trial showed that a DVD-based home programme works. It does not replace inhalers.

Outcome measures: Nijmegen score, asthma control and quality-of-life questionnaires, breathing rate and pattern, symptom diary.
