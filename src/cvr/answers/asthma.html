Rationale of ACBT in bronchial asthma

The active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT) is a flexible cycle of breathing control, thoracic expansion exercises and the forced expiration technique (huffs). In asthma, airway narrowing comes from bronchospasm, mucosal swelling and mucus plugs; plugs can block airways, especially as an attack settles, so gentle clearance helps once bronchospasm has eased.

Rationale of each component

Breathing controlRelaxed lower-chest tidal breathing between the active parts: the "rest" of the cycle; prevents bronchospasm, fatigue and desaturation that repeated deep or forced breaths can trigger in sensitive airways
Thoracic expansion exercises3–4 deep breaths with a hold: air travels through collateral channels (pores of Kohn, canals of Lambert) behind mucus plugs and re-opens closed units
Forced expiration techniqueHuffs from mid lung volume move mucus towards the mouth (airways narrow beyond the equal pressure point and the fast air shears mucus) without the airway narrowing and coughing fits that repeated forceful coughing causes

Why it suits asthma

  • The patient controls the pace and can lengthen breathing control or stop when wheeze increases.
  • No equipment; independent; can be done at home in sitting.
  • Avoids percussion and head-down tilt, which can worsen acute bronchospasm.

How to apply

Give it after the bronchodilator has worked; use more breathing control and fewer huffs; sitting position; stop and return to breathing control if wheeze, breathlessness or SpO₂ worsen.

Pulmonary rehabilitation for a child with asthma

Childhood asthma causes episodic wheeze, breathlessness and cough; fear of attacks often keeps the child from play and school, so fitness falls. Rehabilitation aims at control, fitness and full participation.

Assessment

Triggers, attack frequency, night symptoms, reliever use, inhaler technique; school days missed; play and sport; peak flow, SpO₂; shuttle or 6-minute walk test (or step test); Childhood Asthma Control Test.

Programme

  1. Education with the family: what asthma is, triggers, inhaler with spacer (and mask in young children), preventer versus reliever, written action plan, when to seek help.
  2. Breathing exercises as games: blowing bubbles, windmills, cotton-ball races (long gentle breath out), "smell the flower, blow the candle"; nose breathing; relaxed lower-chest breathing.
  3. Exercise through play: warm-up games, interval play (short bursts with rests), skipping, cycling, swimming (warm humid air suits asthma); 2–3 sessions a week, progressing time and intensity.
  4. Preventing exercise-induced symptoms: 10–15 min warm-up with short bursts; reliever before play if prescribed; nose breathing; avoid cold dry air and smoke; gradual cool-down.
  5. Strength, posture and flexibility through body-weight games.
  6. Parent and teacher counselling: asthma can be controlled; exercise is good; keep the inhaler at school; no smoking at home; avoid over-protection; medical review if on reliever only.

Outcome measures

Childhood ACT, Paediatric Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire, peak-flow diary, shuttle or 6-minute walk test, school days missed, number of attacks and reliever use, time in active play.

Physiotherapy in an acute exacerbation of asthma

An acute exacerbation is a worsening of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough beyond the usual, needing a change in treatment. Airways narrow from bronchospasm, swelling and mucus plugs.

Recognise severity (BTS/SIGN)

Acute severe: peak flow 33–50% of best, RR ≥ 25, HR ≥ 110, cannot finish sentences. Life-threatening: PEF below 33%, SpO₂ below 92%, silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion, confusion, normal or rising CO₂ → medical emergency.

Physiotherapy

  1. Positioning: high sitting or forward-lean sitting with arms supported, to ease the work of breathing.
  2. Breathing control and relaxation: slow, gentle tidal breathing with the lower chest, relaxed shoulders, calm reassurance; no deep, forced breathing during bronchospasm.
  3. Oxygen and nebulised bronchodilator as prescribed (SpO₂ 94–98%); time any airway clearance after the bronchodilator.
  4. Airway clearance once bronchospasm eases, if mucus is present: ACBT with gentle huffs and long breathing control.
  5. No percussion, vibration or head-down postural drainage in acute bronchospasm (they can worsen it).
  6. Encourage fluids; early mobilisation as the attack settles.

Before discharge

Check inhaler and spacer technique; written action plan; trigger avoidance; stop smoking; follow-up. Discharge when symptoms are controlled and peak flow is over about 75% of best.

Get help at once: cannot complete sentences, SpO₂ below 92%, silent chest, blue lips, drowsiness, or the reliever is not working.

Importance of patient education in the control of bronchial asthma

Why education matters

Most severe attacks and asthma deaths follow poor control: preventer medicines not taken, relievers over-used, poor inhaler technique and no plan for worsening symptoms. Self-management education with a written action plan and regular review reduces hospital admissions, emergency visits, night symptoms and days off work or school.

What to teach

  1. The disease: chronic airway inflammation; attacks are preventable.
  2. Medicines: take the preventer (steroid-containing) inhaler even when well; the reliever is for symptoms; rinse the mouth after steroid inhalers.
  3. Inhaler technique: MDI with a spacer (mask for young children); check at every visit.
  4. Action plan by peak-flow zones: green above 80% of best (usual treatment), yellow 50–80% (step up as the plan says), red below 50% (emergency); keep a peak-flow diary.
  5. Triggers: dust mites (wash bedding in hot water, sun-dry), smoke (chulha, mosquito coils, incense), pollen, pets, cold air, infections; no smoking.
  6. Exercise: regular exercise with a 10–15 min warm-up; breathing exercises daily.
  7. Influenza vaccine; healthy weight; masks for dusty work.
  8. When to seek help: cannot speak in sentences, lips blue, reliever not helping or needed more than every 4 hours.

Role of the physiotherapist

Teach and re-check inhaler technique, breathing control and exercise; give the plan in the patient's language; involve family and school; review at each visit.
