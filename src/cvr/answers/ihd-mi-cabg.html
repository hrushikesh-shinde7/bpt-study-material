Risk factors for coronary artery disease (three modifiable and three non-modifiable)

A risk factor is a feature that raises the chance of developing coronary artery disease (CAD), whose main cause is atherosclerosis.

For "three of each" write the first three rows. The modifiable factors are the targets of education and exercise in cardiac rehabilitation.

Phase I of cardiac rehabilitation

Phase I is the in-hospital phase, from the first stable day after MI or heart surgery to discharge.

  • Start when stable: no new chest pain, no new ECG changes or dangerous arrhythmia, troponin stable or falling; after CABG on day 1.
  • Limits (ACSM): RPE below 13; HR ≤ rest + 20 and ≤ 120 after MI; ≤ rest + 30 after surgery; activities up to about 3 METs.
  • Content: breathing exercises, ankle pumps, active limb exercises → sitting → standing → walking in the room, then the corridor → one flight of stairs before discharge; bouts of 3–5 min, 3–4 times a day.
  • Education: risk factors, warning signs, GTN use, home walking plan; referral to Phase II.

ECG criteria for the diagnosis of acute and evolved myocardial infarction

  • Acute STEMI: new ST elevation in two neighbouring (contiguous) leads: at least 1 mm in most leads; in V2–V3 at least 2 mm in men aged 40 or over, 2.5 mm in men under 40, 1.5 mm in women. Sequence: tall (hyperacute) T waves → ST elevation → Q waves → T inversion.
  • NSTEMI and unstable angina: new ST depression or T-wave inversion (troponin rises in NSTEMI).
  • Evolved (old) MI: pathological Q waves in two contiguous leads (at least 0.03 s wide and 1 mm deep; older rule: wider than 0.04 s or deeper than a quarter of the R wave), with T inversion and the ST segment back to baseline.
  • Site: II, III, aVF = inferior (right coronary); V1–V4 = anteroseptal (LAD); I, aVL, V5–V6 = lateral (circumflex).

Grafts used in CABG

  • Left internal mammary (thoracic) artery, LIMA: stays attached at its origin and is joined to the LAD beyond the block; the standard graft because it stays open longest.
  • Right internal mammary artery (RIMA): a second arterial graft. Using both IMAs raises the risk of sternal wound problems, especially with diabetes and obesity.
  • Radial artery: taken from the forearm; leaves a forearm wound.
  • Great saphenous vein: taken from the inner leg; joined from the aorta to the artery beyond the block.

Physio relevance: sternal precautions; care of the leg or forearm wound (elevation, ankle pumps, walking for leg swelling).

Karvonen's formula in cardiac rehabilitation

The heart-rate reserve (HRR) method sets a target training heart rate.

Target HR = resting HR + intensity % × (maximum HR − resting HR), where maximum HR = 220 − age and HRR = maximum HR − resting HR.

Example: age 50, resting HR 70 → maximum 170, HRR 100 → 40% = 70 + 40 = 110; 60% = 70 + 60 = 130. Target range 110–130 beats/min.

  • Phase II uses 40–80% HRR, starting at the low end, and stays at least 10 beats below the heart rate that brings on angina.
  • Limitation: beta-blockers, atrial fibrillation and pacemakers make HR unreliable: use RPE 11–13 and the talk test instead.

Indications of coronary artery bypass grafting

  • Significant left main coronary artery disease.
  • Triple vessel disease, especially with diabetes or a weak left ventricle (low EF).
  • Two-vessel disease involving the proximal LAD.
  • Angina not controlled by medicines when PCI is unsuitable (long, diffuse or complex blocks).
  • Failed PCI or a complication during PCI.
  • At the time of other heart surgery (valve replacement), or for mechanical complications of MI (ventricular septal rupture, papillary muscle rupture, ventricular aneurysm).

Phase I cardiac rehabilitation following myocardial infarction

Phase I is the inpatient phase of cardiac rehabilitation, from the first stable day after MI to discharge (usually days).

Aims

Prevent the effects of bed rest (deconditioning, DVT, lung collapse); safe self-care and walking up to about 3 METs; educate on risk factors, warning signs and GTN; plan the home programme; refer to Phase II.

Criteria to start

Start once there is no new chest pain, no new ECG changes or dangerous arrhythmia, troponin is stable or falling, and there is no uncontrolled heart failure (usually within the first day).

Limits and dose (ACSM): "13 / 20 / 30 / 120"

IntensityRPE below 13; HR ≤ rest + 20 and ≤ 120 after MI (≤ rest + 30 after surgery)
DurationBouts of 3–5 min with 1–2 min rest, about 20 min in total
Frequency3–4 times a day on days 1–3, then twice a day
ProgressionBuild to 10–15 min continuous, then raise intensity

Progression by stage

  1. CCU (1–2 METs): breathing exercises, ankle pumps, active limb movements, sitting up, self-feeding, washing the face, bedside commode.
  2. Ward (2–3 METs): standing, walking in the room then the corridor, washing in the bathroom, sitting out for meals.
  3. Before discharge (about 3 METs): 10–15 min of continuous walking, one flight of stairs under supervision, taking own pulse and using the RPE scale.

Monitoring and stop criteria

HR and rhythm, BP, SpO₂, RPE and symptoms before, during and after every session. Stop for angina, marked breathlessness, diastolic BP ≥ 110, systolic BP falling over 10 mmHg as work rises, new arrhythmia or heart block, ischaemic ECG changes, dizziness, pallor or cold sweat. Inferior MI: watch for bradycardia and heart block.

Education and discharge

Risk factors, warning signs, GTN use (sit, take it; not better 5 min after the first dose → emergency help), home walking plan. Discharge when vitals are stable, self-care and walking at about 3 METs and one flight of stairs are managed without symptoms; refer to Phase II.

Importance of lifestyle modification in the prevention of ischaemic heart disease

Ischaemic heart disease is caused mainly by atherosclerosis, and most of its risk comes from modifiable factors: smoking, hypertension, diabetes, abnormal lipids, obesity, inactivity, poor diet, alcohol and stress. South Asians develop it earlier and more severely, so lifestyle change matters even more in India. It works for primary prevention (before disease) and secondary prevention (after MI, PCI or CABG).

Importance

  • Treats the cause (the atherosclerotic process), not only the symptoms.
  • Reduces first and repeat heart attacks; exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation reduces MI by about 28% and admissions by about 35% (Cochrane 2026).
  • Improves fitness, quality of life and the ability to work; cheap and available to all.

Role of the physiotherapist

Exercise prescription (FITT-VP), education in the patient's language, goal setting with diaries or step counts, family involvement, regular review and progression to lifelong (Phase IV) activity.

Patient education for lifestyle disorders

Lifestyle disorders are diseases largely caused by how people live: coronary heart disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, obesity and metabolic syndrome, and smoking-related COPD. Education aims at understanding, self-management and lasting behaviour change.

Content

Physical activityAt least 150 min a week of moderate activity (brisk walking, cycling) + strength work on 2 days; break up sitting every 30 min
DietSalt under 5 g a day; fewer sugary drinks, sweets, fried snacks and refined flour; more vegetables, pulses, fruit, whole grains; smaller portions
Weight and waistBMI under 23; waist under 90 cm (men), 80 cm (women); aim to lose 5–10% if overweight, about 0.5 kg a week
Tobacco and alcoholStop all tobacco; avoid or limit alcohol

Medicines and monitoringTake medicines regularly; home BP checks, blood sugar and HbA1c, lipids; foot care in diabetes
Warning signsChest pain, sudden breathlessness, fainting, very high BP or sugar, low-sugar symptoms: when to seek help

Methods that work

  • Assess what the patient knows and wants; set small SMART goals chosen with the patient.
  • Simple words in the patient's language (Marathi or Hindi), pictures and demonstrations; check understanding by asking the patient to explain back.
  • Link exercise to daily routines; activity and food diaries, step counts.
  • Involve the family (meals, support); group sessions; phone or WhatsApp reminders.
  • Praise small gains; review and adjust the plan at follow-up visits.

Good education improves adherence, risk-factor control and quality of life, and reduces heart attacks, strokes and admissions.

Risk assessment and stratification for coronary artery disease

Risk stratification sorts patients with CAD into low, moderate or high risk of a cardiac event during exercise, before Phase II cardiac rehabilitation. The AACVPR scheme is the standard. (Risk of developing CAD in the first place is judged by counting risk factors and using a risk chart such as the WHO chart for South-East Asia.)

Assessment

History of the event and complications; symptoms; ejection fraction (echo); exercise test (functional capacity in METs, angina, ST changes, arrhythmia, BP response); signs of heart failure; depression.

RiskFeaturesMeaning for rehab
LowEF ≥ 50%; ≥ 7 METs; no angina or complex arrhythmia; normal HR and BP response; uncomplicated MI or revascularisation; no heart failure or depressionStandard supervised programme; faster progression
ModerateEF 40–49%; symptoms only at high effort (≥ 7 METs); mild silent ischaemia (ST depression under 2 mm); capacity under 5 METsCloser supervision; ECG monitoring for the first sessions
HighEF under 40%; symptoms under 5 METs or in recovery; ST depression ≥ 2 mm; BP fails to rise or falls; complex arrhythmia; survived cardiac arrest; complicated course; heart failure; clinical depressionContinuous ECG monitoring; start low; progress slowly

One high-risk feature makes the patient high risk. Memory: "EF 50 / 40, METs 7 / 5".

Why it matters

Risk decides the level of supervision and ECG monitoring, the starting intensity and the speed of progression, and keeps exercise safe while still training the patient.

Phase 2 cardiac rehabilitation for a 45-year-old executive after PTCA to the LAD who wants to play sport

Phase II is the supervised outpatient phase (about 8–12 weeks after discharge). After PCI there is no sternotomy, so arm work and resistance training can start early and progress faster than after CABG.

Assessment first

Symptom-limited exercise test (safe HR range, remaining ischaemia, BP response); risk stratification (usually low risk); check the puncture site; risk factors and goals (his sport).

Precautions

  • Puncture site: radial → avoid heavy gripping and wrist loading (racquet and bat sports) for about a week; femoral → avoid heavy lifting and strenuous leg work for about a week.
  • Antiplatelets cause easy bruising: avoid collision injuries; never stop them without the cardiologist.

Exercise prescription (FITT-VP)

Aerobic3–5 days a week; start 40–60% HRR (RPE 12–13), progress to 60–80% HRR; 20–60 min; walking, jogging, cycling; add interval training once continuous work is comfortable
Resistance2–3 days a week; all major muscle groups; 1–3 sets of 10–15, progressing load ("2 for 2"); breathe out on effort, no breath-holding
Every sessionWarm-up and cool-down 5–10 min; stay at least 10 beats below any ischaemic HR

Sport-specific stage and return to sport

  • Skill work at low intensity first, then match-like efforts; avoid sudden all-out bursts in the early months.
  • Recreational sport when he trains at the needed intensity without symptoms, ischaemia or arrhythmia; competitive sport only after a cardiologist's assessment (ESC 2020 sports cardiology guideline).

Education

Risk-factor control even in a fit person; warning signs (chest pain, unusual breathlessness, palpitations, fainting): stop and seek help; self-monitoring with pulse and RPE; then lifelong Phase III–IV activity.

Cardiac markers and their importance in cardiac rehabilitation

Cardiac markers are substances released into the blood when heart muscle is damaged (or, for BNP, stretched). They confirm MI and guide the safety and pace of rehabilitation.

MarkerBehaviour
Troponin I or TMost specific for heart-muscle damage; rises within a few hours, peaks around a day, stays raised up to about 2 weeks
CK-MBRises within hours, back to normal in 2–3 days, so a fresh rise suggests a new infarct
Myoglobin; LDHMyoglobin rises earliest but is not specific; LDH rises late and stays up about a week (older markers)
BNP or NT-proBNPReleased by a stretched, failing ventricle: marks heart failure

Importance in cardiac rehabilitation

  1. Diagnosis: a rise and fall of troponin with symptoms or ECG changes confirms MI and separates NSTEMI from unstable angina.
  2. Size and risk: a higher peak means more muscle lost, usually a lower EF and a higher risk group for exercise.
  3. When to start Phase I: once markers are stable or falling and there is no new chest pain.
  4. Safety during progression: new chest pain is checked with ECG and troponin; a fresh rise means a new infarct, so exercise stops and the plan is reset.
  5. After CABG: some troponin rise is expected from surgery; the surgeon decides if it is more than expected.
  6. Heart failure: BNP shows severity and response to treatment, and so how hard and how fast to train.
