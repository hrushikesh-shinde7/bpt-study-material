Respiratory complications of mechanical ventilation

  1. Ventilator-induced lung injury: over-stretching (high volumes), high pressures and repeated opening and closing of alveoli.
  2. Pneumothorax (barotrauma).
  3. Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP).
  4. Atelectasis and retained secretions; oxygen toxicity with high FiO₂.
  5. Auto-PEEP (air trapping) in COPD and asthma; patient fighting the ventilator.
  6. Airway problems: blocked or displaced tube, tube in the right main bronchus, laryngeal and tracheal injury, later tracheal stenosis; sinusitis.

Physio prevention: head of bed 30–45°, regular turning, airway clearance, humidification, secure the tube on turns, early weaning and mobilisation.

Complications of long-term mechanical ventilation

  • Airway: tracheal stenosis or softening (tracheomalacia) from the tube and cuff.
  • Lungs: repeated ventilator-associated pneumonia; ventilator-induced lung injury.
  • Diaphragm wasting and ventilator dependence (failure to wean).
  • ICU-acquired weakness, contractures, pressure injuries, DVT.
  • Psychological: delirium, poor sleep, anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress.

Patients ventilated for more than about 1–2 weeks often get a tracheostomy (more comfortable, less sedation, easier weaning). Physio: early mobilisation, inspiratory muscle training, positioning, ROM.

Memory: CPAP = PEEP for a patient who breathes alone. Both raise FRC and keep alveoli open; high levels lower venous return and BP.

Three indications for mechanical ventilation

  1. Type 1 respiratory failure (PaO₂ below about 60 mmHg despite high oxygen): pneumonia, ARDS, pulmonary oedema.
  2. Type 2 respiratory failure (high CO₂ with acidosis): COPD exacerbation when NIV fails, severe asthma, drug overdose.
  3. Failure of the breathing pump: Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenic crisis, high spinal cord injury, flail chest, exhausted breathing muscles.

Others: airway protection (GCS 8 or less, head injury, stroke), cardiac arrest and shock, during and after major surgery. Warning signs: RR above about 35, exhaustion, falling SpO₂ despite oxygen, rising CO₂ with falling pH, falling vital capacity in neuromuscular disease.

Modes of mechanical ventilation and the role of physiotherapy in the ICU

Mechanical ventilation moves gas into the lungs with positive pressure when the patient cannot breathe adequately; it is invasive (endotracheal tube, tracheostomy) or non-invasive (mask). The mode decides how breaths are started and delivered.

Why the mode matters to a physiotherapist: on CMV or A/C at a high rate the muscles rest and waste (positioning, passive movement, clearance); on SIMV, PSV or CPAP the patient breathes, so active exercise, breathing exercises and inspiratory muscle training are possible.

Role of physiotherapy in the ICU

  1. Assessment and safety check: settings, vitals, ABG, X-ray, sedation, lines.
  2. Positioning: head up 30–45° (prevents VAP), 2–4-hourly turns, affected lung uppermost for drainage.
  3. Airway clearance: manual techniques, manual or ventilator hyperinflation, suction.
  4. Limbs: passive then active ROM, splints; prevent contractures, pressure sores and DVT.
  5. Early mobilisation: sitting, standing, walking within safety criteria.
  6. Weaning support: upright position, IMT; after extubation breathing exercises and huffing.

Criteria for weaning a patient off the ventilator

Weaning is the process of freeing the patient from the ventilator: a daily readiness check, a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT), then extubation.

Readiness criteria (daily screen)

  1. Cause improving: the reason for ventilation is resolving.
  2. Oxygenation: SpO₂ ≥ 90% on FiO₂ ≤ 0.4–0.5 and PEEP ≤ 5–8 cmH₂O (PaO₂/FiO₂ above about 150–200).
  3. Circulation: stable HR and BP, little or no BP-support drugs, no active ischaemia.
  4. Brain: awake or easily roused, follows commands, can cough and protect the airway.
  5. Breathing: breathes alone; pH ≥ 7.25; few secretions; no fever; normal electrolytes.

Bedside weaning indices

Rapid shallow breathing index (rate ÷ tidal volume in L)Below 105 favours weaning
Maximal inspiratory pressureStronger than about −20 to −30 cmH₂O
Vital capacityMore than about 10 mL/kg
Minute ventilationBelow about 10 L/min

Example: 35 breaths of 0.25 L → RSBI 140 (likely to fail); 20 breaths of 0.5 L → 40 (likely to succeed).

Spontaneous breathing trial

30–120 min on low pressure support (5–8 cmH₂O) or a T-piece. Stop if: RR above 35 for over 5 min, SpO₂ below 90%, HR above 140 or a change over 20%, systolic above 180 or below 90 mmHg, agitation, sweating, accessory muscles or see-saw breathing. Passed → extubation; NIV after extubation in high-risk patients.

Outcome: simple (first trial), difficult (up to 3 trials or 7 days), prolonged (more than 3 trials or 7 days).

Physiotherapy in weaning

Keep airways clear; sit the patient upright for trials; inspiratory muscle training for those who fail; early mobilisation for strength; after extubation deep breathing, huffing and walking.
