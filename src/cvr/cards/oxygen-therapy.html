1What oxygen is for

Oxygen is a drug given to correct or prevent hypoxaemia (low oxygen in the blood). It does not treat breathlessness itself, and it should be prescribed with a target saturation.

Target SpO₂ (BTS)
PatientTarget
Most acutely ill adults94–98%
At risk of high CO₂ (COPD, severe obesity, chest wall deformity, neuromuscular disease, cystic fibrosis with high CO₂)88–92%, adjusted with blood gases
Critically ill, peri-arrest, carbon monoxide poisoningHigh concentration first (reservoir mask 15 L/min), then titrate
  • Indications: low SpO₂ in acute illness (pneumonia, asthma, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, after surgery); critical illness; carbon monoxide poisoning; desaturation on exercise; long-term oxygen in chronic hypoxaemia (criteria on the COPD page).
  • Not routinely for breathless patients with normal saturation, or in heart attack when SpO₂ is 90% or more.

2Methods of delivery (3 marks in S23)

Devices are grouped by whether they supply all the air the patient breathes in. Low-flow (variable-performance) devices give less than the patient's inspiratory flow, so room air mixes in and the FiO₂ changes with the breathing pattern. High-flow (fixed-performance) devices supply all of it, so the FiO₂ stays fixed.

FiO₂ figures for low-flow devices are approximate and vary between sources, because they depend on the patient's breathing. Sources of oxygen: piped wall supply, cylinders, oxygen concentrators at home.

3Low-flow oxygen therapy (7 marks in S20)

  • Definition: oxygen given at a flow lower than the patient's own inspiratory flow, so the gas breathed is a mix of oxygen and room air and the FiO₂ varies.
  • Devices: nasal cannula, simple face mask, reservoir mask, tracheostomy mask (oxygen catheter in older texts).
  • Uses: mild to moderate hypoxaemia in stable patients; home and ambulatory oxygen; during exercise in patients who desaturate.
  • Advantages: simple, cheap, comfortable (cannula), patient can eat, talk and walk.
  • Disadvantages: FiO₂ is not known exactly (higher with slow, shallow breathing, lower with fast breathing), so it is less suitable when the CO₂ level must be controlled precisely; nasal dryness at higher flows.
  • Humidification: not needed for low-flow oxygen; consider it for high flows used for more than about 24 hours, for a tracheostomy, or if the nose and mouth feel dry.
  • Monitoring: SpO₂ against the prescribed target, breathing rate, drowsiness (rising CO₂), ABG in those at risk.

4Hazards of oxygen therapy (3 marks in S25)

HazardDetails
CO₂ retention (hypercapnic respiratory failure)In COPD and other at-risk patients, uncontrolled high oxygen raises CO₂ (worse V/Q matching and the Haldane effect; reduced drive plays a small part): drowsiness, confusion, acidosis. See hypoxic drive
Absorption atelectasisHigh FiO₂ washes nitrogen out of alveoli; the oxygen is absorbed and poorly ventilated alveoli collapse
Oxygen toxicityLung injury from prolonged high concentrations (FiO₂ above about 0.6 for long periods)
Retinopathy of prematurityHigh oxygen damages developing retinal vessels in preterm babies
FireOxygen feeds fire: no smoking, no flames or chulha nearby, no oil or grease on fittings; burns of the face in smokers using oxygen
Masking deteriorationA good SpO₂ on oxygen can hide worsening breathing; watch the rate and the patient
Local problemsDry nose and mouth, nosebleeds, pressure sores behind the ears and on the nose; masks cause claustrophobia and aspiration risk if vomiting
EquipmentTrips over tubing; empty cylinders during walks; heavy cylinders

5The physiotherapist's role

  • Check the prescription and target before treatment; never stop or change oxygen without it.
  • Exercise: measure SpO₂ during walking; give oxygen (as prescribed) to keep SpO₂ above about 88–90% during training; use portable cylinders or concentrators for ambulatory oxygen.
  • Keep oxygen on during airway clearance and positioning when needed; watch SpO₂ after turns and suction.
  • Teach long-term oxygen users: at least 15 hours a day, safety (no smoking), cleaning the cannula, and pacing.
  • Pulse oximetry limits: wrong readings with cold fingers, poor circulation, movement, nail polish and carbon monoxide (falsely normal); oximeters can over-read in darker skin, so act on the whole clinical picture.

3 mList three low-flow and two high-flow oxygen devices with flows.

Low-flow: nasal cannula 1–4 L/min; simple face mask 5–10 L/min; reservoir mask 15 L/min. High-flow (fixed performance): Venturi mask (flow printed on the valve, FiO₂ 24–60%); high-flow nasal cannula (up to about 60 L/min).

MCQA COPD patient at risk of CO₂ retention needs oxygen. The best first choice is:A. Reservoir mask 15 L/minB. Simple face mask 3 L/minC. 24% or 28% Venturi mask, target SpO₂ 88–92%D. No oxygen

C. A fixed, low FiO₂ with the 88–92% target; a simple mask below 5 L/min risks rebreathing CO₂.

MCQWhich hazard of oxygen is caused by nitrogen washout?A. Absorption atelectasisB. Retinopathy of prematurityC. NosebleedsD. Fire

A. Without nitrogen to hold them open, poorly ventilated alveoli collapse as the oxygen is absorbed.
