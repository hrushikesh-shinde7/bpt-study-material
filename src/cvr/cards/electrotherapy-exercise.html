1Pain

  • TENS after chest surgery: electrodes on either side of and parallel to a thoracotomy, sternotomy or abdominal wound (not on it); conventional (high-frequency, about 80–100 Hz, short pulses, strong comfortable tingling) during breathing exercises and coughing. A systematic review of 11 trials found TENS gave better pain relief after thoracic surgery than standard pain relief alone; it is an add-on to medicines, not a replacement.
  • Supported cough and positioning: a pillow over the wound, side-lying with the wound uppermost, relaxed upright sitting.
  • Cold for acute musculoskeletal chest-wall pain and after minor trauma; superficial heat for muscle spasm in the neck, shoulders and back of breathless patients, with care (see precautions).
  • Relaxation and breathing control lower pain-related muscle guarding and anxiety.

3Wound healing (S16, S22, S24, S25)

Electrical stimulation (high-voltage pulsed current), therapeutic ultrasound, ultraviolet and low-level laser, with typical parameters and effects, are tabulated on the diabetes and wound page, together with the factors that affect healing and pressure-sore care. Electrical stimulation has the best evidence of these for slow-healing pressure ulcers.

4Electrical stimulation of weak muscles

  • Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) of the quadriceps produces visible contractions without breathlessness, so it suits very breathless or bed-bound patients: severe COPD, heart failure, ICU and after major surgery.
  • Evidence is mixed: a 2016 meta-analysis of nine trials found better quadriceps strength and exercise capacity in moderate to severe COPD, while a 2014 review found no clear benefit; a 2023 review after heart and abdominal surgery found a small gain in leg strength. Use it as a bridge to active exercise.
  • Practical: electrodes over the quadriceps; a frequency that gives a smooth, strong contraction; on–off cycles; about 20–30 minutes, several times a week; progress intensity as tolerated.

5Precautions for electrotherapy in these patients

Check before every modality
  • Pacemakers and ICDs: no shortwave or microwave diathermy; TENS and other stimulation only away from the chest and device, with the device team's advice.
  • Ischaemic limbs (PAD, diabetes): no heat (the skin cannot lose heat and burns); check sensation and circulation first.
  • Anticoagulants and bleeding: gentle handling; avoid vigorous percussion and deep massage.
  • DVT: no massage or heat over the affected limb until treated.
  • Cancer: no heat, ultrasound or stimulation over a known tumour.
  • Never over the front of the neck (carotid sinus), the pregnant abdomen, broken or numb skin, or in a confused patient who cannot report discomfort.

Buerger–Allen exercises (classic, for arterial disease)

  1. Lie with the legs raised (about 45° or more on a support) until the feet go pale, usually 1–3 minutes.
  2. Sit with the legs hanging down until the feet turn pink, about 2–5 minutes, moving the ankles and toes.
  3. Lie flat with the legs level, covered, for about 5 minutes.
  4. Repeat the cycle several times, a few times a day.

These postural exercises are traditional and still asked about, but the evidence for them is weak; supervised walking has strong evidence and is the first-line exercise for claudication. Avoid them in severe rest pain, ulceration or gangrene.

Test yourself

3 mHow would you use TENS after a posterolateral thoracotomy?

Electrodes on either side of and parallel to the incision, not on the wound or drain site; conventional high-frequency TENS at a strong, comfortable tingle during breathing exercises and coughing; alongside analgesia. Check for a pacemaker and intact sensation first.

MCQBefore applying compression bandaging for a leg ulcer you must check:A. Blood sugarB. ABPIC. Peak flowD. Body temperature

B. Arterial disease must be ruled out; compression on an ischaemic leg can cause ulcers or worse.

MCQHeat is contraindicated over:A. A tight trapezius in COPDB. An ischaemic foot in PADC. Normal skinD. A healed scar with normal sensation

B. Ischaemic skin cannot dissipate heat and burns easily.
