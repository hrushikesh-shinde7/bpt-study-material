Asked: 15-mark cases (cook with varicose veins; teacher with infected ulcers; air hostess; bus conductor with an ulcer) as ICF + management + do's and don'ts or foot care. 7 marks (many): venous or varicose ulcer physiotherapy, DVT prevention and physiotherapy, patient education, assessment of venous insufficiency, two special tests.

Definitions

  • Varicose veins: dilated, twisted superficial leg veins ≥ 3 mm when standing, from faulty valves.
  • Chronic venous insufficiency: long-term venous hypertension → swelling, skin changes, ulcers.
  • Venous ulcer: skin break of the lower leg, usually above the inner ankle, unhealed in ~2 weeks.
  • DVT: clot in a deep vein (calf, thigh); a piece to the lung = PE. Distal (calf) lower PE risk; proximal (popliteal, femoral, iliac) higher.
  • Superficial thrombophlebitis: red tender cord along a superficial vein. Phlebothrombosis: older term for a deep clot with little inflammation.

Causes

Varicose veins: prolonged standing jobs (cook, teacher, shop worker, traffic police, air hostess, conductor); female, pregnancy, family history, age, obesity; previous DVT; poor calf pump (inactivity, stiff ankle).

DVT, Virchow's triad: stasis (bed rest, surgery, long travel, paralysis, heart failure); vessel wall injury (trauma, fracture, surgery, lines, past DVT); hypercoagulability (cancer, pregnancy and after delivery, the pill, dehydration, clotting disorders, obesity).

Calf pump to ulcer (S26: pathophysiology)

Calf contracts → squeezes deep veins → blood up; valves stop backflow; relaxation refills deep veins from superficial via perforators. Valve failure → reflux on standing → venous hypertension even when walking → veins stretch (aching, heaviness worse by evening) → fluid, red cells, protein leak → oedema, haemosiderin pigmentation, eczema → inflammation hardens skin (lipodermatosclerosis) → a minor knock becomes an ulcer. Stiff ankle or weak calf worsens the pump.

Ulcer theories: fibrin cuff (leaked fibrinogen cuffs capillaries, blocks O₂ and nutrients); white-cell trapping (trapped white cells release damaging enzymes).

Standing still: ankle venous pressure = height of the blood column. Walking pumps it down; exercise strengthens the calf and keeps the ankle mobile.

Clinical features

Venous insufficiency: dull ache, heaviness, worse after standing and in the evening, relieved by elevation; evening ankle swelling; night cramps; itching; visible veins standing; pigmentation, eczema, hard skin above the ankle.

Special tests (S16: two tests; 7m W19)

  • Brodie–Trendelenburg: lying, empty the veins by raising the leg; press the saphenofemoral junction (or thigh tourniquet); stand up. Fill fast on release = SFJ incompetent; fill fast with pressure on = perforators incompetent.
  • Perthes: tourniquet below the knee, standing; heel raises or walking. Veins empty = deep veins open; veins swell and hurt = deep veins blocked (do not strip superficial veins).
  • Tap test (impulse felt above = incompetent valves between); cough impulse at the SFJ.
  • Duplex ultrasound = gold standard (reflux, blocks, DVT). ABPI before any compression.
  • DVT: Wells score, D-dimer (normal helps rule out), compression ultrasound; CT pulmonary angiogram for PE. Homans' sign is unreliable: no longer used.

Medical management

Varicose veins: stockings, exercise, elevation, weight; endovenous laser or radiofrequency ablation (first line); foam sclerotherapy; ligation and stripping; phlebectomy. Ulcer: compression ~40 mmHg at the ankle (multilayer or wraps) once ABPI allows; clean, simple dressings; antibiotics only for spreading infection; ablate the vein. DVT: heparin then oral anticoagulant ≥ 3 months; thrombolysis or IVC filter in some. Thrombophlebitis: NSAIDs, warm compresses, compression, keep walking.

Complications

Ulcer; bleeding varicose vein (lie flat, raise leg, press firmly); cellulitis; PE (stop, sit up, call for help); post-thrombotic syndrome; ankle stiffness and calf weakness.

PT assessment (7m W19)

History: standing hours, pain pattern through the day, swelling, itch, past DVT, pregnancies, surgery, diabetes, smoking, stairs. Inspect standing: veins, oedema, pigmentation, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, ulcer (site, size, depth, base, edge, exudate, smell). Measure: calf and ankle girth both sides, ulcer tracing or photo. Palpate: temperature, pitting, tenderness, pulses. Trendelenburg, Perthes, ABPI. Ankle dorsiflexion; calf strength (single-leg heel raises); gait; monofilament if diabetic.

Outcome measures: Venous Clinical Severity Score, ulcer area, pain scale, CIVIQ.

ICF (S17: 55, diabetic widow, cook, 2nd floor no lift, aching and evening swelling, dilated veins)

  • Body function and structure: aching, heaviness; evening oedema both legs; dilated veins; ↓ calf pump endurance; ulcer risk (worse with diabetes). Reasons: standing builds pressure, reflux stretches veins, diabetes slows healing.
  • Activity: standing long to cook, long walks, climbing to the 2nd floor. Participation: full working day, household and social roles.
  • Environment: standing job, hot kitchen, 2nd floor, maybe no family help (barriers); work, care access (facilitators). Personal: 55, widow, diabetic, maybe overweight.

Goals: short-term (2–4 weeks): less pain with stockings and elevation, smaller evening ankle girth, hourly ankle pumps, daily foot checks. Long-term (3 months): full shifts with little aching, swelling controlled, 20 single-leg heel raises, no ulcers.

PT management

  • Calf pump: ankle pumps 10–20 every hour; heel raises 2–3 × 10–15; toe walking; walking 30 min most days; dorsiflexion stretches.
  • Elevation: legs above heart 30 min, 3–4 times a day; foot of bed slightly raised; deep diaphragmatic breaths during elevation.
  • Stockings (after ABPI): on in the morning before getting up, off at night. Weight loss, fitness.
  • After ablation or surgery: walk the same day, compression as advised, no long standing or sitting for weeks.
  • Venous ulcer (7m W16, W20, W21): compression ~40 mmHg if ABPI allows + calf exercise and walking in the bandage (they work together; progressive resistance helps healing); ankle ROM; elevation; skin care; electrical stimulation, ultrasound, UV (infected) as add-ons.

DVT (7m S18, W18, S22)

Prevention: early walking; ankle pumps 10 every hour; deep breathing; static quadriceps, gluteal sets, knee bending in bed; hydration; no crossed legs or pillows behind knees; stockings or intermittent pneumatic compression; heparin as prescribed.

After diagnosis: check the anticoagulant and start time; walk once anticoagulation is therapeutic (APTA, strong); bed rest does not lower PE risk. Progress walking and leg exercise, elevate at rest, compression for persistent symptoms; watch for PE and bleeding.

Precautions and stop

ABPI before compression (compression on an ischaemic leg → gangrene). No compression in severe heart failure, fragile neuropathic skin, acute cellulitis. No vigorous massage with suspected DVT. No heat on ulcerated or swollen legs. Gentle handling on anticoagulants. Stop and call: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, fast HR, haemoptysis (PE); pale, blue, cold or numb toes under a bandage.

Do's, don'ts, foot care (S20, W18, S17)

Do: walk daily; ankle pumps and heel raises when standing or sitting long; raise legs several times a day; stockings morning to night; breaks at work, shift weight, footrest; healthy weight; low heels; moisturise. Don't: stand or sit still long, cross legs, tight garters or waistbands, hot-water bags or sit near fire, scratch. Foot care (diabetes): inspect daily, lukewarm water, dry, moisturise (not between toes), cut nails straight, never barefoot, sugar control, report wounds early.
