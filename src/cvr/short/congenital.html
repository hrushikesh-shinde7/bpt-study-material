Asked: 3 marks only: clinical features of tetralogy of Fallot (W16, S26) and its X-ray features (S21). 7 marks possible on physiotherapy after paediatric heart surgery.

Definition and types

Congenital heart disease: structural defect of the heart or great vessels present at birth; the commonest birth defect.

Acyanotic, left-to-right shuntASD, VSD, PDA: too much lung blood → breathlessness, chest infections, poor growth, later pulmonary hypertension
Acyanotic, obstructiveCoarctation, aortic and pulmonary stenosis: pressure overload
Cyanotic (right-to-left)Blue blood reaches the body

The 5 T's

Tetralogy of Fallot, Transposition of the great arteries, Tricuspid atresia, Truncus arteriosus, Total anomalous pulmonary venous return.

Causes: mostly none; Down syndrome (AV septal defect), family history; maternal rubella (PDA), diabetes, alcohol, drugs; prematurity (PDA).

Tetralogy of Fallot

Four parts (one fault: outflow septum displaced forwards): large VSD, overriding aorta, right ventricular outflow obstruction (pulmonary stenosis), right ventricular hypertrophy. Obstruction pushes blue blood through the VSD into the aorta → cyanosis (worse with worse obstruction).

Squatting (knee–chest) kinks leg arteries, raises systemic resistance, so less blood crosses right-to-left and more goes to the lungs.

Clinical features (3m W16, S26): cyanosis from the first months, worse with crying or effort; tet (hypercyanotic) spells after waking, feeding or crying (deep cyanosis, fast breathing, irritability, fainting); squatting after exertion; clubbing; poor growth, exercise intolerance; harsh ejection systolic murmur at the left upper sternal edge; single S2.

X-ray of TOF (3m S21) and tests

  • Boot-shaped heart (coeur en sabot): upturned rounded apex (RVH).
  • Concave pulmonary bay (small pulmonary artery segment).
  • Oligaemic lungs (dark, few vessel markings).
  • Heart size usually normal; right-sided aortic arch in some.

Echo (main test), ECG (RVH), pulse oximetry (newborn screening), Hb (raised in chronic cyanosis), catheter, CT or MRI.

Other lesions

VSD: loud pansystolic murmur, left lower sternal edge; sweating on feeds, poor weight gain. ASD: often silent till adulthood; fixed split S2. PDA: continuous "machinery" murmur below the left clavicle, bounding pulses. Coarctation: weak delayed femoral pulses, high BP in arms. Eisenmenger: long-standing left-to-right shunt → pulmonary hypertension → shunt reverses → late cyanosis.

Management and complications

TOF: complete repair in infancy (VSD patch, relieve outflow); Blalock–Taussig shunt in some small infants. Tet spell: calm, knee–chest position, oxygen, call for help. VSD, ASD, PDA: many close alone; device or surgical closure; drugs for PDA in preterms. Transposition: arterial switch; single ventricle: Glenn, Fontan.

Complications: heart failure, failure to thrive, chest infections; polycythaemia, stroke, brain abscess; endocarditis; Eisenmenger; after repair arrhythmias, leaking pulmonary valve; developmental delay.

Physio after paediatric heart surgery

Assess: operation, day, lines, drains, pacing wires, ventilation, the child's target SpO₂ (may be lower than normal); rate, recession, expansion, auscultation, secretions; pain (age scale), development, posture, shoulder; parents' worries.

  • Head of bed up, side-lying, regular turns; no head-down.
  • Airway clearance only if secretions: gentle manual techniques, assisted huff, suction; breathing games (bubbles, windmills, party blowers).
  • Early sitting, standing, walking, play.
  • Sternal care: lift with a scoop under the bottom and back, not under the armpits; no pulling on arms.
  • Shoulder and trunk through play; development activities; parent teaching; later normal active play, sport as the cardiologist advises.

Goals: clear chest, sitting and walking within days, confident parents → milestones, active play.

Precautions and parent education

Don't chase normal SpO₂ in a cyanotic heart. Pulmonary hypertension: avoid crying, straining, desaturation; pre-oxygenate before suction. Secure wires and drains; gentle handling on anticoagulants; avoid dehydration (thick blood). Parents: lifting after sternotomy; breathing games; recognise a tet spell and use knee–chest; dental hygiene, vaccines, follow-up; avoid over-protection.
