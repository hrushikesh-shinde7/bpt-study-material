Asked: 15-mark cases after below-knee (and one above-knee) amputation for diabetes or PAD: ICF + management, goals, return to job, suitable prosthesis. 7 marks: physiotherapy after BKA, stump management. 3 marks: ideal stump.

Definition, levels, causes

Amputation: surgical removal of all or part of a limb; what remains is the residual limb (stump). Levels: hemipelvectomy, hip disarticulation, transfemoral (above knee), knee disarticulation, transtibial (below knee), Syme's, partial foot. Higher level = more energy to walk; keeping the knee is the biggest advantage.

Causes: PAD and diabetes (commonest in adults), trauma (commonest in the young), infection (gas gangrene, diabetic foot), tumours, burns, frostbite, congenital.

K-levels: K0 cannot walk with a prosthesis; K1 household walker; K2 limited community (kerbs, stairs); K3 community at varying speed, work; K4 high impact.

Ideal stump (3m W18)

  • Length: transtibial 12–15 cm below the knee joint line; transfemoral ~25 cm from the greater trochanter (room for a prosthetic knee).
  • Conical, firm, not bulbous; healed, mobile, non-adherent scar away from the weight-bearing end.
  • Good padding; smooth bevelled bone ends (fibula slightly shorter than tibia); muscles anchored (myodesis).
  • Good circulation and sensation; no neuroma; full range of the joint above.

Amputation to impairments

  • Loss of ankle and foot (± knee): balance, push-off and shock absorption lost.
  • Post-op oedema: the stump must shrink and shape before a socket fits.
  • Pillow under the stump, long sitting → contracture: knee flexion (transtibial); hip flexion, abduction, ER (transfemoral).
  • Cut nerves → phantom sensation and phantom pain; neuroma → local pain.
  • Bed rest and disease → weakness, low fitness; prosthetic walking costs much more energy. The other leg often has the same disease.

Features: stump pain and swelling, drains; phantom sensation and pain (burning, cramping); poor single-leg balance, falls at night getting up to the toilet; weak hip extensors, abductors, quadriceps; grief, worry about income, body image.

Tests: pulses, ABI, Doppler of both legs (S21: popliteal 1+ = slow healing); sugar, HbA1c, kidney; Hb, albumin; ECG, echo.

Surgery and prosthesis

Level removes all dead tissue but keeps length, ideally the knee; long posterior flap and myodesis or myoplasty in vascular transtibial. Wound care, pain relief, sugar and BP control, nutrition. Soft dressing with bandaging, rigid removable dressing, or a shrinker once healed. Prosthesis when healed and stump volume is stable (several weeks).

Transtibial prosthesis: socket PTB (patellar-tendon-bearing) or total-surface-bearing; suspension by supracondylar cuff, sleeve, pin-lock liner or suction; pylon; SACH or Jaipur foot (flexible rubber: squatting, cross-legged sitting, barefoot on uneven ground).

For a diabetic vascular patient (W19): fragile, possibly numb skin → total-surface-bearing socket with gel or silicone liner (or PTB with soft foam liner if cost matters); secure sleeve or pin-lock suspension; light endoskeletal pylon; stable SACH or Jaipur foot (limited community walker; floor-level living); early walking aid and interim prosthesis first; daily checks of the stump and other foot.

Complications and physio role

  • Contractures: positioning, prone lying, stump board, stretching, strengthening.
  • Oedema, bulbous stump: correct bandaging or shrinker, exercise.
  • Delayed healing, infection: report, protect the wound, sugar control.
  • Phantom pain, neuroma: desensitisation, mirror therapy, TENS, early prosthesis.
  • Falls at night; socket skin sores (daily checks, fit review); ulcer or overuse in the other leg (foot care, avoid long hopping); DVT, chest infection, deconditioning; depression (goals, peer amputees).

PT assessment

History: cause, level, date, side, diabetes, BP, heart; job demands (driving, door-to-door walking, lifting); home floors, steps, toilet; family, earning role; previous walking level. Stump: length from a bony landmark, shape, circumference at fixed points, wound, scar, skin, sensation, temperature, pain, neuroma. Hip and knee range both sides (contracture); strength of both legs, trunk, arms. Other leg: pulses, skin, sensation, ulcers. Sitting and standing balance, transfers, wheelchair, hopping with walker; phantom sensation and pain.

Outcome measures: AMPPRO, K-level, Timed Up and Go, 2- or 6-minute walk, Houghton scale, SIGAM grades.

ICF (S21: 48, driver, right BKA for PAD, day 7; right popliteal 1+, left foot pulses 2+)

  • Body structure and function: loss of right leg below knee, healing wound, weak pulse (slow healing); stump pain and oedema; knee flexion contracture risk; phantom sensation or pain; poor single-leg balance; weak hip and knee extensors; ↓ endurance.
  • Activity: standing, walking, stairs, toileting, bathing, driving. Participation: work as a driver (right foot for pedals), family income, social life.
  • Environment: prosthetic services (Jaipur foot centres), automatic car or left-foot accelerator, family (facilitators); lost income, Indian toilet, steps (barriers). Personal: 48, PAD (smoker or diabetic?), motivated.

Goals: short-term (pre-prosthetic, 4–6 weeks): wound healed, conical stump ready for casting, full knee extension, independent transfers, walks with walker or crutches, phantom pain controlled, self-care at home. Long-term (prosthetic, 3–6 months): good socket fit, full range, community walking without aids (K3), drives an adapted car, back to work.

PT management

  • Before surgery: explain, meet an amputee, teach transfers, wheelchair, exercises; strengthen arms and other leg; check the other foot.
  • Early post-op (days 1–10): breathing exercises, ankle pumps of the other leg, sit out early; positioning and prone lying; stump bandaging or shrinker, elevation without bending the knee; isometric then active quadriceps, hip extensors and abductors; full exercise of the other leg, trunk, arms; transfers and wheelchair with a stump board; balance in parallel bars; hopping with a walker only for short distances.
  • Prosthetic training: donning and doffing, daily skin checks; weight shift in bars → standing on the prosthesis → stepping → walking with fewer aids; correct gait deviations (short step, wide base, trunk lean); stairs: "good goes up, bad goes down"; ramps, kerbs, uneven ground, getting up from the floor, squatting with a Jaipur foot; build wearing time gradually.

Contracture prevention

No pillow under the stump, under the knee or between the thighs; stump not hanging over the bed edge or resting on a crutch handle; prone lying 20–30 min, 2–3 times a day; transtibial: knee straight, stump board; transfemoral: limit sitting, hip straight, not rolled out or abducted.

Stump bandaging (7m W20)

  • Elastic bandage 10 cm (4 in) for transtibial, 15 cm (6 in) for transfemoral.
  • Diagonal figure-of-eight turns, no wrinkles, firmest at the end and less towards the knee; no circular turns (tourniquet).
  • Transtibial: anchor above the knee. Transfemoral: include the groin, anchor round the waist (hip spica).
  • Re-apply every 4–6 h and when loose; day and night except bathing and exercise. Shrinker sock once healed (easier, more even).

Pain and phantom pain (S25)

Stump pain: find the cause first: infection, poor blood supply, neuroma, bony end, tight bandage; report infection or ischaemia; treat after analgesia. Phantom pain (burning, cramping in the missing part) is common and usually lessens over months: tapping, massage, rubbing with textures several times a day; mirror therapy; TENS; shrinker or prosthesis; reassure.

Above-knee amputation (S23: 60, diabetic, lives alone, one floor up)

Contracture: hip flexion, abduction, ER: prone lying, no pillows, limit sitting. Strengthen hip extensors and adductors, abductors, trunk, arms, other leg. Hip-spica bandage or shrinker. Prosthesis: ischial-containment or quadrilateral socket, suction or liner, stance-control or locked knee for an older low-activity walker, SACH or Jaipur foot. Very energy-costly: build fitness; wheelchair independence first, prosthetic walking if fitness and the other leg allow. Living alone: safe transfers, getting up from the floor, stairs with crutches and rail (or bumping on the bottom), home visit, rails, phone in reach, other-foot checks.

Return to work: driver: automatic car with left-foot accelerator or hand controls; milkman (S19): endurance walking, stairs, carrying, adapted two-wheeler, rest breaks, sugar control.

Precautions and home care

Check the wound before bandaging and exercise; no pressure on an unhealed end; sugar before exercise; monitor HR, BP and breathlessness (walking is costly); avoid long hopping; never sleep in the prosthesis or with an unchecked tight bandage.

Home: inspect the stump daily with a mirror; wash and dry it, wash socks and liner daily; keep the bandage or shrinker on; daily positioning and exercises; protect the other foot; control sugar and BP, stop smoking; stable weight (socket fit); walker and light by the bed at night.

Prognosis: most previous walkers with a transtibial amputation walk again; worse with transfemoral level, age, heart and lung disease, poor sugar control.
