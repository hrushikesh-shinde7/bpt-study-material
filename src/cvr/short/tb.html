Asked: 15-mark cases: thoracoplasty (W18 nurse), post-TB destroyed lung (S22 farmer, S24 student). TB is also the cause in 4 bronchiectasis cases and 2 lung-surgery cases.

Definitions and types

TB: infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, spread through the air by coughing; mainly lungs. Post-TB lung disease: lasting damage after cure: fibrosis, cavities, bronchiectasis, airway narrowing, pleural thickening, destroyed lung. Thoracoplasty: removal of parts of several ribs so the chest wall collapses onto a diseased lung or empyema space (now rare).

Types: latent; active primary (children) or post-primary (upper lobe cavities); extrapulmonary (pleura, nodes, spine (Pott's), meninges, abdomen); drug-sensitive or MDR. Risk: contact, crowding, poor ventilation, undernutrition, diabetes, HIV, smoking, alcohol, silicosis, steroids, incomplete treatment.

Pathophysiology and features

Granulomas → caseation → cavities → healing by fibrosis: lobe shrinks, trachea and mediastinum pulled to that side, airways distorted (traction bronchiectasis) → vicious cycle of mucus and infection → restriction, obstruction or both → low O₂, right heart strain. After thoracoplasty: chest wall deformity, early paradoxical movement, later scoliosis and shoulder problems.

Active TB: cough over 2 weeks, sputum, haemoptysis, evening fever, night sweats, weight loss, anorexia. Post-TB: chronic productive cough, breathlessness, recurrent infections, haemoptysis, flat chest and ↓ expansion over the side, tracheal shift towards it.

Tests: CBNAAT/Xpert (also rifampicin resistance), smear, culture; X-ray (upper-lobe cavities, fibrosis, calcification; destroyed lung opaque and shrunken); CT; spirometry (obstructive, restrictive or mixed); SpO₂, 6MWT; HIV and sugar.

Medical (NTEP): ~6 months daily fixed-dose tablets: 2 months HRZE (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) then 4 months HRE; longer regimens for resistance; nutrition support (Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana); surgery for destroyed lung, aspergilloma, empyema. Complications: massive haemoptysis, pneumothorax, empyema, aspergilloma, cor pulmonale; drug effects (liver, isoniazid neuropathy, ethambutol vision).

Assessment and ICF (S22: 45, farmer, destroyed lung, fever, cough, chest pain, breathless 4 days)

Assess: infectious status (how long on treatment, sputum results), adherence, nutrition, weight; cough, sputum, haemoptysis, mMRC, fever; chest shape, expansion, trachea, auscultation; posture, spine, shoulder (thoracoplasty); SpO₂ rest and exercise, 6MWT; crowding, smoke, earning role. Outcomes: 6MWT, mMRC, SGRQ, weight.

  • Impairments: breathlessness, retained sputum, pleuritic pain, fever, ↓ lung volume, ↓ exercise tolerance, low SpO₂.
  • Activity: walking, lifting, field work, self-care. Participation: farming, income, social life.
  • Environment: dust and smoke, distance to hospital (barriers); family, NTEP (facilitators). Personal: 45, smoker?, undernourished?

S24 student: right upper crackles, right pleuritic pain, HR 105, RR 28, FEV1 60% and FVC 56% (mainly restrictive): goals are studies, sport, fitness.

Goals: short-term (1–2 weeks): clears sputum alone, breathlessness controlled, walks with stable SpO₂. Long-term (2–3 months): completes rehab, better 6MWT and mMRC, lighter farm work with pacing and mask, fewer exacerbations.

PT management

  • Exacerbation: ACBT, autogenic drainage, PEP in positions that drain the area (no head-down with haemoptysis); breathing control, relaxation; better lung down for gas exchange; supported cough for pleuritic pain; mobilise as fever settles; fluids, food.
  • Pulmonary rehab: endurance and strength as in COPD, 6–12 weeks, 2–3 supervised sessions a week, oxygen if SpO₂ falls; daily clearance; IMT; thoracic mobility, posture; energy conservation for farm and housework; education.
  • After thoracoplasty (W18): deep breathing, ACBT, supported cough, early sitting and walking; firm pad early to limit paradoxical movement; shoulder exercises from day 1; mirror posture and trunk work to prevent scoliosis; later exercise training.

Infection control and stop

Until confirmed non-infectious (often ~2 weeks of effective treatment): staff wear N95, patient a surgical mask, ventilated room or outdoors; cough etiquette, covered sputum container with disinfectant; clean shared devices. Haemoptysis: stop percussion, vibration, head-down; sit up; report; massive bleed: lie on the bleeding side, call for help. Sudden breathlessness or chest pain: pneumothorax.

Education: every dose for the full course; cover coughs, open windows; protein and calories; stop smoking and alcohol; keep breathing exercises and walking after cure; report fever, weight loss, blood in sputum.
