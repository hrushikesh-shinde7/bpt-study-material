Worker care spectrum

Pre-placement → ability management (before injury: job analysis, ergonomic evaluation, injury prevention, employee fitness) → injury → disability management (acute care, FCE, work conditioning, work hardening; disability management) → return to work → back to ergonomics and prevention.

Pre-placement: after a job offer, abilities matched with job demands; baseline; no unfair exclusion of PWD (RPWD Act 2016). Ability management: keeping healthy workers healthy and productive by fitting the job to the worker and the worker to the job.

Job analysis (3m S26; S21)

Job descriptionTitle, purpose, duties, tools, materials, hours and shifts, breaks, workplace, supervision, pay (piece rate or salary)
Job demand analysisPhysical, mental and environmental demands and how often each occurs
Task analysisEach task in steps: posture, force, repetition, duration, tools, environment; risk factors found

Job demand analysis (S21): physical (lift, carry, push, pull with weights and distances; sit, stand, walk time; bend, kneel, squat, climb, reach above shoulder; grip, fine work, repetition; vibration); mental and sensory (attention, memory, decisions, vision, hearing, deadlines); environment (heat, cold, noise, light, dust, fumes, heights, wet floors, shifts).

Frequency (Dictionary of Occupational Titles): occasionally up to a third of the day; frequently a third to two-thirds; constantly more than two-thirds.

Importance of task analysis (7m S19)

  • Finds the exact steps and postures causing pain, so the fix is specific; basis for ergonomic redesign.
  • Matches job to worker in pre-placement and return to work (compare with FCE).
  • Designs work hardening (job simulation); shows light duties; guides rotation.
  • Basis for safe-method training, fair production rates, compensation decisions.
  • Example: lifting bags into a truck: bending, twisting, 50 kg, 3 ft height.

Ergonomics: definition, purpose, principles (7m S22, S23)

IEA (2000): "the scientific discipline concerned with the understanding of interactions among humans and other elements of a system"; the profession applies design to optimise human well-being and overall system performance. Simply: fitting the job to the person.

PhysicalAnatomy, anthropometry, biomechanics: postures, handling, repetition, MSDs, layout
CognitivePerception, memory, reasoning: workload, decisions, human–computer interaction, error, stress
OrganisationalShift design, teamwork, communication, pace, participation

Purpose: prevent MSDs and accidents; less fatigue and error; comfort, productivity, quality; include older and disabled workers.

Principles: neutral postures (spine in its curves, elbows by side, wrists straight, neck upright); less force (lighter loads, aids, good grips); easy reach; work at elbow height (lower for heavy, higher for fine); less repetition and static load; breaks and rotation; minimise contact stress and vibration; space to move, sit–stand; comfortable light, temperature, noise; clear displays and controls; participatory ergonomics.

Cognitive ergonomics (7m S21)

  • Mental workload: overload (errors, stress) or underload (boredom, lapses); balance tasks and breaks.
  • Vigilance: monotonous monitoring, night shifts, fatigue reduce it.
  • Displays and controls: clear labels, logical layout, warnings that stand out, controls that move as expected.
  • Human error: hard to make, easy to correct (checklists, forcing functions). Computer work: simple screens, fewer interruptions; training, decision support, stress management.
  • Examples: control rooms, drivers, pilots, surgeons, call centres.

Ergonomic PT evaluation (3m S19), importance (7m S16)

  1. Meet management and workers; injury and absence records; Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire.
  2. Walk-through and video; job and task analysis.
  3. Measure workstation (heights, reaches, seat, screen), loads, forces, frequency, environment.
  4. Score risk: RULA, REBA, NIOSH lifting equation, Strain Index.
  5. Recommend from the hierarchy of controls: redesign, aids, layout, breaks, rotation, training, exercise → re-evaluate.

Importance: finds risk before injury; prevents MSDs, accidents, absence; comfort, productivity; lower compensation; objective scores to prioritise and show change; return and inclusion of workers; legal duty.

REBA (7m W19; scale S23)

Hignett and McAtamney (2000): whole-body postures in unpredictable or varied tasks (health care, service, industry). Group A: trunk, neck, legs + load → score A. Group B: upper arm, lower arm, wrist + coupling (grip) → score B. A + B → score C + activity score (static over a minute, repeated small actions, rapid large changes) → REBA 1–15.

1Negligible: none needed
2–3Low: may be needed
4–7Medium: needed
8–10High: needed soon
11–15Very high: needed now

RULA: upper-limb version for seated and standing arm work (computer, assembly), 1–7.

Prevention, employee fitness (3m W19), health promotion (3m S18)

Injury prevention: redesign first, then safe methods, training, PPE; early reporting and PT (stage 1 CTD reversible); warm-up before heavy shifts, micro-breaks, rotation.

Employee fitness programme: needs assessment, management support, worker committee → health risk appraisal, fitness tests (BP, BMI, waist, aerobic, strength, flexibility) → classes or gym, walking groups, stretch breaks, sports; education (back care, nutrition, smoking, stress, sleep) → incentives, convenient timing, family → evaluate participation, injuries, absence. Benefits: fewer injuries and sick days, morale, productivity, lower costs.

Workplace health promotion (Luxembourg Declaration 1997): "the combined efforts of employers, employees and society to improve the health and well-being of people at work"; via work organisation, participation, personal development. Examples: ergonomic workplaces, health checks, healthy canteens, tobacco-free workplace, stress management, flexible hours.
