1What CBR is

Aspects of CBR (S16), three features of CBR (W24), 3 marks; a note on CBR (W20, 7 marks).

  • Definition (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004): "a strategy within general community development for the rehabilitation, equalization of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities", carried out by people with disabilities, their families and communities together with health, education, vocational and social services.
  • History: WHO began promoting CBR after the Alma-Ata declaration on primary health care (1978), because most disabled people in low-income countries had no access to institutions; the joint position papers (1994, 2004) widened it from medical care to rights and inclusion; the WHO CBR guidelines (2010) set out the CBR matrix.
  • Compare with institution-based rehabilitation on IBR vs CBR.

Features (aspects) of CBR

  • Takes place in the person's own home, village or town, using local resources and simple technology.
  • The person with disability and the family are active partners and decide goals.
  • Community workers (CBR workers, anganwadi workers, ASHAs, teachers, volunteers) deliver most of the work, trained and supported by professionals.
  • Covers health, education, livelihood, social life and empowerment, not only therapy.
  • Works with existing services (primary health centres, schools, panchayats, government schemes) and refers to institutions when needed.
  • Low cost, wide coverage, sustained by the community.

Benefits of teamwork in CBR

  • Disability touches health, school, work and social life, so many sectors must act together.
  • Few professionals can train and support many local workers (transdisciplinary model, see the team).
  • Shared goals avoid repeated assessments and conflicting advice; families get one consistent plan.
  • Early identification by village workers and quick referral; follow-up close to home.
  • Local resources and schemes are pooled; costs fall and the programme lasts.
  • Community attitudes change when many local people are involved.

5Strategies and approaches

CBR strategies in rural and urban areas (W19, 7 marks); approaches to CBR (S26, 7 marks).

Steps of a CBR programme

  1. Situation analysis: meet leaders, map resources, survey households to identify persons with disability.
  2. Plan with the community: form a CBR committee, set priorities using the matrix.
  3. Train local workers and families in simple assessment, home programmes and referral.
  4. Implement: home visits, camps, school inclusion, aids, livelihood links, self-help groups.
  5. Monitor and evaluate: records, outcomes, community feedback; hand over to local ownership.
SettingStrategies (syllabus 4f)
UrbanUrban health centres and community centres for clinics and camps; clubs, mahila mandals and social centres for awareness, volunteers and funds; ordinary schools for inclusion and screening; industries for vocational training, jobs and workplace adaptation; sports centres for adapted sport; NGOs and corporate social responsibility funds
RuralPrimary health centres, rural hospitals and district hospital infrastructure for screening, treatment and referral; anganwadi workers and ASHAs for identification and home programmes; locomotor aids made from local materials (bamboo crutches, wooden walkers, parallel bars and standing frames, CP chairs) by village craftsmen; panchayat support; self-help groups for livelihood; camps for certificates and aids

Approaches to CBR (7 marks in S26)

CBR began after Alma-Ata as a way of delivering rehabilitation through primary health care. It has since widened into community development and rights. Programmes differ in two ways: who starts and controls the programme, and where the work is done. Most real programmes mix several approaches.

By who starts and controls it
ApproachHow it worksStrengths and drawbacks
Top-down (government or agency-led)Planned and run from outside the village by a ministry, an international agency or experts; local workers follow set training packages. Government-led examples: national programmes in Iran and Viet Nam; in India, schemes run through DDRCs and campsFor: easy to start, run and evaluate in many places at once; more money, wider reach and better sustainability. Against: weak local leadership and commitment; "participation" may mean doing what was decided above
Bottom-up (led by the community, an NGO or disabled people's groups)Started, planned and run locally, with disabled people and families making the decisions; an outsider may help at the start. Examples: PROJIMO, a village programme run by disabled villagers in Mexico; NGO programmes such as Mobility India in BengaluruFor: flexible, fits local culture, better participation and ownership, works in difficult areas. Against: fewer resources and smaller reach; hard to fund without outside help

The WHO guidelines conclude that CBR works best with government support that stays sensitive to local culture, resources and disabled people's organisations (WHO, 2010).

By where the work is done
ApproachHow it worksStrengths and drawbacks
Home-basedA trained local worker (the "local supervisor" of the WHO 1989 manual) visits the home and teaches a family member to train the person, using simple training packagesFor: reaches remote homes; skills learnt where they are used; family as partner; low cost. Against: when aids or specialist help are needed the worker may have nowhere to turn, and referrals to the city often fail; heavy load on poor or troubled families
Community centre-basedA small village or ward centre run by local people, an anganwadi or an early intervention centre, where people come for therapy, aids, groups and playFor: visible low-cost base and back-up for home work; aids made quickly from local materials; work for disabled villagers; care when home care is too hard. Against: people must travel; reaches fewer homes
Outreach from institutionsCamps and mobile teams from hospitals, national institutes, DDRCs and NGOs bring assessment, certificates and aids to the villageFor: specialist skill and equipment close to home. Against: visits are periodic, so day-to-day work still needs local workers and families
Integrated with primary health careExisting PHC staff, ASHAs, anganwadi workers and RBSK teams identify, refer and follow upFor: uses staff and buildings that already exist, so coverage is wide and cheap. Against: early programmes of this kind stayed mainly medical (therapy, devices, surgery)
Community development and rights-basedAll five matrix components; self-help groups and disabled people's organisations lead; the twin-track approach includes disabled people in mainstream programmes and adds specific support where needed; WHO now presents CBR as a strategy for community-based inclusive developmentFor: tackles poverty, school, work and inclusion, not only impairment. Against: complex and multisectoral, so results are hard to measure and evidence is limited
  • Home and centre together: home-based work goes better with a local centre behind it (Werner, Disabled Village Children).
  • Indian example: in Amar Seva Sangam's rural Tamil Nadu programme, community rehabilitation workers see children at home or at a local early intervention centre, guided by specialists through a mobile app. Of 1,245 children, about 91% chose home-based care; children seen at the centre had higher motor and independence scores (Bhrasadiya et al., 2025, an observational study).
  • What CBR achieves: reviews describe CBR-type programmes as effective, with more independence, mobility and communication, and some signs of lower cost (WHO, 2010).

7The physiotherapist in CBR and in a special school

Role of physiotherapy and the physiotherapist in CBR (syllabus 4e); the physiotherapist in a school for physically handicapped children (W22, 7 marks).

In CBR

  • Trainer: teaches CBR workers, anganwadi workers, ASHAs, teachers and families simple assessment, positioning, exercises, transfers and when to refer.
  • Assessment and planning at camps and centres; writes simple home programmes with pictures.
  • Designs low-cost aids from local materials; checks fit and safety.
  • Prevention and health promotion: ergonomics, fall prevention, exercise for chronic disease, antenatal and postnatal advice.
  • Referral to institutions for surgery, orthoses and complex care; follow-up.
  • Advocacy for accessibility and inclusion; supervision and evaluation of the programme.

In a school for physically handicapped children (7 marks in W22)

Children in such a school may have cerebral palsy, spina bifida, muscular dystrophy, limb deficiency or the after-effects of polio or injury. The physiotherapist's aim is not therapy alone. It is to help each child move, learn and take part in school life, working with the special educator, class teachers, parents, occupational and speech therapists and the doctor.

RoleWhat the physiotherapist does
Screening and assessmentAssess each child on admission and at regular reviews: GMFCS level, tone, range and contractures, strength, posture and spine, sitting and standing balance, gait and mobility, hand function, self-care. Set goals with teachers and parents within the individual education plan
Individual programmesIndividual and small-group therapy fitted into the school day: stretching, strengthening, balance, gait training, play-based activity; a written home programme for each child
Seating and positioningAdapted chair and table so the child sits upright with the pelvis level and feet supported; change position through the day (standing frame, side-lying, prone); standing frame 30–60 min a day for a child who cannot stand
Mobility aids and orthosesPrescribe, fit, train and check ankle–foot orthoses, walkers, crutches and wheelchairs; teach wheelchair skills and safe moving around the campus
AccessibilityAudit the school: ramps (no steeper than 1:12) with handrails, accessible toilets, ground-floor classes, wide aisles, safe playground; advise the management on changes (see barriers and modifications)
Training teachers, attendants and parentsSafe lifting and transfers, positioning in class, toileting and feeding positions, use and care of aids, the home programme, and when to call the physiotherapist
Sports, play and fitnessAdapted physical education, games and sport for every child; aim for the WHO target, adapted to ability (below); limit long sitting
Prevention of secondary problemsContractures, hip and spine deformity, obesity, pressure sores, falls; refer early for orthopaedic review
Inclusion and transitionPrepare children who can move to the neighbourhood school: advise that school on seating near the door and blackboard, a peer buddy, extra time and adapted physical education; later, pre-vocational and vocational training
Links to schemesHelp families get the disability certificate and UDID card, free aids (ADIP and Samagra Shiksha camps), RBSK and DEIC review, scholarships and transport support

Physical activity target: WHO (2020) advises children and adolescents aged 5–17, including those living with disability, to do an average of 60 min a day of moderate-to-vigorous, mostly aerobic activity across the week. Vigorous activity and muscle- and bone-strengthening should be included on at least 3 days a week. Children should do what they can at their level of ability. Wheelchair users can stay active with activities that do not use the legs.

Laws and schemes the school physiotherapist uses

  • RPWD Act 2016, section 16: schools funded or recognised by government must provide inclusive education. They must admit children with disability without discrimination and give equal sports and recreation. They must make buildings accessible, provide reasonable accommodation and individual support, monitor each child's progress, and provide transport with an attendant for children with high support needs.
  • Section 17: survey school children every 5 years to identify disability; train teachers and staff; set up resource centres; give free books, learning materials and assistive devices to students with benchmark disability up to 18 years; allow extra time and a scribe in examinations.
  • Section 31: every child with benchmark disability aged 6–18 years has the right to free education in a neighbourhood school or a special school of choice.
  • Samagra Shiksha (Ministry of Education, from 2018–19) covers children with special needs from pre-school to class XII. Its inclusive education component funds identification and assessment camps, aids and assistive devices, transport and escort allowance, Braille and large-print books, a stipend for girls with special needs, and therapy at block level. It also funds ramps with handrails and disabled-friendly toilets. Children with severe disability can receive home-based education from special educators.
  • Accessibility Code for Educational Institutions (notified January 2024, added to the RPWD Rules in June 2024): child-friendly access standards for school buildings.
  • RBSK: mobile block health teams screen children aged 6–18 in government and government-aided schools for the 4Ds (defects at birth, diseases, deficiencies, developmental delays including disabilities) and refer them.

8Worked long answer: CBR for a child with cerebral palsy

From a real paper (S22, 5 + 10)

"6 years old CP child with locomotor disability has been identified during a screening at a village. His educated parents intend to send him school. Enumerate the local resources to be used and plan community based rehabilitation."

Spot the clues before you write

6-year-old with CP and locomotor disability: needs mobility, positioning and aids. village screening: rural CBR, PHC and anganwadi links. educated parents want school: education component is the priority; parents are strong partners.

Local resources (5 marks)

ResourceUse
FamilyDaily home programme, school travel, advocacy
Anganwadi worker, ASHA, ANMFollow-up visits, nutrition, immunisation, referral
PHC, RBSK team, District Early Intervention CentreMedical review, therapy, hearing and vision checks, referral for orthoses and surgery
Village school, teachers, block resource centreEnrolment under inclusive education, special educator support, classroom adaptations
Gram panchayatRamp and toilet at school, funds, transport
Village carpenter and blacksmithCP chair, standing frame, parallel bars, walker
Self-help and parents' groups, NGOsSupport, awareness, funds, advocacy
Government schemes and campsDisability certificate and UDID card, free aids (ADIP), scholarships, travel concessions

CBR plan (10 marks)

  1. Assessment at home and school: type of CP, GMFCS level, tone, contractures, sitting and walking, hand use, communication, vision and hearing, feeding, toileting; ICF summary; family goals (school).
  2. Health: home programme taught to parents and the anganwadi worker: stretching of calves, hamstrings and hip adductors daily; sitting balance, sit-to-stand and supported walking practice; positioning in sitting and lying; standing frame 30–60 minutes a day if he cannot stand; ankle–foot orthoses and a walker through the DEIC or an ADIP camp; review every month by the physiotherapist at the PHC or camp.
  3. Education: enrol in the village school; seat near the door and blackboard; adapted chair and table; ramp, railing and an accessible toilet; teacher training on handling and toileting; peer buddy; extra time and adapted physical education; special educator from the block resource centre.
  4. Social: play with peers, festivals and outings; counselling to remove stigma; parents' group.
  5. Empowerment: parents learn rights under the RPWD Act (free education, accessibility, reservation); link to a disabled people's organisation.
  6. Livelihood (family now, child later): schemes for the family; plan for vocational training as he grows.
  7. Referral and follow-up: to the district hospital for orthopaedic review, botulinum toxin or surgery if needed; record goals and review every 3 months.
