1Health and disease

WHO definition of health (S19, 3 marks).

Definition (WHO Constitution, 1948)

"Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity."

  • Dimensions of health: physical, mental, social, and often added spiritual, emotional and vocational.
  • Disease: a disturbance of the structure or function of the body or mind, with signs and symptoms, that departs from the state of health.
  • Health is a continuum from positive health through ill-health to death; the physiotherapist works along all of it, not only with disease.
  • Determinants of health: biology and genes, behaviour and lifestyle, environment, socioeconomic conditions, health services.

2Primary health care

Describe the primary health care setting (S17, 3 marks).

  • Definition (Alma-Ata, 1978): essential health care based on practical, scientifically sound and socially acceptable methods and technology, made universally accessible to individuals and families in the community through their full participation, at a cost the community and country can afford; the first level of contact with the health system, as close as possible to where people live and work.
  • Principles: equitable distribution, community participation, intersectoral coordination, appropriate technology, focus on prevention and promotion.
  • Eight elements: health education; food supply and nutrition; safe water and sanitation; maternal and child health including family planning; immunisation; control of locally endemic diseases; treatment of common diseases and injuries; essential drugs.

4Functions of the primary health centre

Functioning of primary health centres (S22, 7 marks).

  1. Medical care: outpatient and basic inpatient treatment of common illnesses and injuries; referral.
  2. Maternal and child health: antenatal, delivery and postnatal care; newborn care; family planning.
  3. Immunisation.
  4. Prevention and control of communicable diseases (tuberculosis, malaria, leprosy) and screening for non-communicable diseases (hypertension, diabetes, common cancers).
  5. National health programmes carried out locally.
  6. Health education and promotion; nutrition.
  7. Safe water and environmental sanitation.
  8. Collection and reporting of vital statistics and disease data.
  9. Basic laboratory services and essential drugs.
  10. Training and supervision of ANMs, health workers and ASHAs.
  11. School health; elderly, palliative and mental health care under the Ayushman Arogya Mandir packages.

5Physiotherapy in the health care system

Role of PT in health care delivery systems (S24), socioeconomic factors affecting PT services in India (S20, W24), 3 marks each.

LevelRole of the physiotherapist
PrimaryHealth promotion and fitness, ergonomic and back-care education, antenatal and postnatal classes, screening (posture, development, falls risk), training ASHAs and anganwadi workers, home programmes, CBR
SecondaryOutpatient and inpatient physiotherapy at CHCs and district hospitals: fractures, stroke, chest care, post-operative care, early intervention
TertiarySpecialised ICU, cardiac, neuro, paediatric, sports and rehabilitation services; teaching and research

Socioeconomic factors affecting physiotherapy services in India

  • Cost: much of health care is paid out of pocket; repeated sessions and lost wages are hard for daily earners.
  • Distribution: physiotherapists are concentrated in cities and private clinics; few government posts at CHCs and PHCs.
  • Distance and transport to services, especially for disabled and elderly people in villages.
  • Awareness and literacy: many people do not know what physiotherapy offers or think rest is the cure; preference for traditional healers.
  • Gender and family roles: women may not get permission or time to attend; preference for female therapists.
  • Housing and environment: crowded homes, floor-level living and Indian toilets affect home programmes.
  • Insurance and schemes: limited coverage of outpatient rehabilitation (PM-JAY mainly covers hospital care).
  • Solutions: CBR, camps, tele-rehabilitation, physiotherapists in Ayushman Arogya Mandirs and CHCs, simple home programmes, community awareness.

6Principles and scope of community physiotherapy

Principles and scope of community physiotherapy (W25, 7 marks).

  • Definition: the application of physiotherapy to the whole community, with a preventive and promotive emphasis, to optimise health and function where people live and work.

Principles

  • Prevention and health promotion first; then treatment and rehabilitation.
  • Equity and access: services for rural, poor, elderly, women and disabled people.
  • Community participation and ownership.
  • Holistic, biopsychosocial approach using the ICF.
  • Teamwork and intersectoral coordination (health, education, social welfare, industry).
  • Appropriate, low-cost technology and local resources.
  • Education and empowerment for self-management.
  • Evidence-based, measured outcomes; sustainability.

Scope

  • Health promotion and fitness across ages (schools, colleges, workplaces) (see fitness and obesity).
  • Women's health: antenatal, postnatal, menopause, pelvic floor (see pregnancy).
  • Geriatrics: fitness, falls, home care (see ageing).
  • Industrial health: ergonomics, injury prevention, return to work (see ability management).
  • Disability and rehabilitation, CBR (see CBR).
  • Non-communicable disease prevention and management (diabetes, hypertension, COPD, heart disease).
  • Disaster management (see disaster); sports and school health; palliative and home care.
  • Research, policy and advocacy.

3 mLevels of health care delivery. (S25)

Primary: first contact at village, sub-centre (5,000; 3,000) and PHC (30,000; 20,000), now Ayushman Arogya Mandirs. Secondary: CHC (1,20,000; 80,000; 30 beds, four specialists) and district hospitals. Tertiary: medical colleges and specialist institutes. Referral moves up; follow-up moves down.

3 mHow do socioeconomic factors affect physiotherapy services in India? (S20, W24)

Out-of-pocket cost and lost wages; urban and private concentration of physiotherapists; distance and transport; low awareness and literacy; cultural beliefs and traditional healers; gender norms; home environment; limited insurance for outpatient rehabilitation. Solutions: CBR, camps, tele-rehabilitation, posts at CHCs and AAMs.
