1Physical fitness and its components

  • Physical activity: any bodily movement by skeletal muscles that uses energy. Exercise: planned, structured, repeated activity done to improve or keep fitness. Physical fitness: a set of attributes that people have or achieve that relates to the ability to perform physical activity (Caspersen et al., 1985).

Field tests in athlete training

  • Why field tests: cheap, quick, done in the athlete's own setting and sport-specific; test many athletes at once; track training and return to play.
  • Examples: multistage 20 m shuttle run (beep test) and Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test for aerobic capacity; Cooper 12-minute run; 30–40 m sprints; vertical and broad jumps; Illinois and T-tests for agility; sit-and-reach; push-ups and curl-ups; 1RM in the gym.
  • Rules for good testing: standard warm-up, the same surface, time of day and order of tests (least tiring first, endurance last), trained tester, clear instructions, record conditions, repeat at set intervals.

4Principles of training and exercise prescription

General principles of exercise and its phases (S16, 7 marks); principles of aerobic training (S19) and the FITT principle (W20), 3 marks; principles of exercise prescription (W25, 7 marks).

Principles of training

  • Overload: the body adapts only when stressed beyond its usual level.
  • Progression: increase the load gradually (usually duration first, then frequency, then intensity; about 10% a week).
  • Specificity: the body adapts to the exact type of training (aerobic training builds endurance, not strength).
  • Reversibility: gains are lost when training stops ("use it or lose it").
  • Individuality: people respond differently; prescribe for the person's age, fitness, disease and goals.
  • Recovery: adaptation happens during rest; too little rest leads to overtraining.

  • Flexibility: 2–3 days a week, each stretch held 10–30 seconds (30–60 in older adults), repeated to total about 60 seconds.
  • Neuromotor (balance, agility): 2–3 days a week, especially for older adults.
  • Heart rate target (Karvonen): resting HR + (maximum HR − resting HR) × intensity.

Steps of exercise prescription (W25)

  1. Screen: history, risk factors, medicines, red flags; medical clearance when needed.
  2. Test fitness (section 2) and set goals with the person.
  3. Write the FITT-VP for aerobic, resistance, flexibility and balance work.
  4. Teach warm-up, cool-down, self-monitoring (talk test, RPE, pulse) and warning signs.
  5. Progress, review and retest every 4–6 weeks; adjust for adherence and enjoyment.

Phases

Warm-up (5–10 minutes)Light aerobic activity and dynamic movements; raises heart rate, muscle temperature and joint mobility; reduces injury and heart strain

A training programme also has stages: an initial stage (first few weeks, low intensity, building the habit), an improvement stage (the next few months, steady progression) and a maintenance stage (keeping gains long term).

5Circuit training

Define aerobic exercise and explain circuit training (S25, 7 marks).

  • Definition: a series of exercises (stations) done one after another with little rest, working different muscle groups and adding aerobic stations; the whole set is repeated.
  • Method: 8–12 stations; 30–60 seconds or 10–15 repetitions at each at light to moderate load; short rest (15–30 seconds) while moving to the next; 2–3 rounds; warm-up before and cool-down after.
  • Types: resistance circuit, aerobic circuit (aerobic bouts between resistance stations), sport-specific circuit.
  • Benefits: improves muscular endurance and strength with some aerobic fitness in one short session; suits groups (schools, workplaces, cardiac rehabilitation phase III, older adults with chair stations); varied and enjoyable.
  • Precautions: correct technique first; avoid breath-holding; adapt stations for joint problems; monitor heart rate or RPE in patients.

6Inactivity: sedentary lifestyle and bed rest

Hazards of a sedentary lifestyle (W18, 7 marks); deconditioning effects of bed rest (S16, 7 marks; W16, 3 marks).

Hazards of a sedentary lifestyle

  • People who are not active enough have a 20–30% higher risk of death than those who are sufficiently active (WHO).
  • Heart and metabolism: coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, obesity, high cholesterol, metabolic syndrome.
  • Cancer: colon, breast and endometrial cancer.
  • Musculoskeletal: low back and neck pain, osteoporosis, weak muscles (sarcopenia), stiffness, falls in later life.
  • Mind: depression, anxiety, poor sleep, lower cognitive function.
  • Prevention: 150–300 minutes a week of moderate activity plus strength work twice a week, and breaking up long sitting with movement (WHO, 2020); see desk workers.

Prevention: avoid unnecessary bed rest; sit out and walk early; breathing exercises; ankle pumps; active and resisted exercise; positioning and turning; standing and weight-bearing as early as possible. Full table with mechanisms on bed rest in CVR.

7Exercise in diabetes and hypertension

Precautions when prescribing fitness exercise for a diabetic (S18, 3 marks); effects of exercise in a hypertensive person (W25, 7 marks).

Diabetes: precautions

  • Dose: at least 150 minutes a week of moderate to vigorous aerobic activity over at least 3 days, with no more than 2 days in a row without activity; resistance training 2–3 non-consecutive days a week (ADA, 2016).
  • Low sugar (hypoglycaemia): check blood glucose before and after exercise if on insulin or sulphonylureas; carry fast sugar; take carbohydrate or reduce insulin as advised; do not inject insulin into the exercising limb; lows can come 6–15 hours later and the risk lasts up to 48 hours.
  • High sugar: do not exercise with high blood glucose and ketones.
  • Feet: inspect daily, good footwear and socks; non-weight-bearing exercise (cycling, swimming) if there is an ulcer.
  • Eyes: with severe or unstable retinopathy avoid vigorous and heavy resistance exercise, jumping, jarring, head-down positions and breath-holding.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: use RPE rather than heart rate; avoid quick position changes; drink enough; avoid heat.
  • Exercise at a regular time, often 1–2 hours after a meal; exercise with a partner who knows the signs of hypoglycaemia.

Hypertension: effects of exercise

  • Acute: after one session of aerobic exercise, blood pressure stays lower for up to 22 hours (post-exercise hypotension) (Pescatello et al., 2004).
  • Chronic: regular aerobic exercise lowers resting blood pressure, most in people with hypertension (about 5–7 mmHg); dynamic resistance and isometric training also help.
  • How: lower sympathetic activity, lower peripheral resistance, better endothelial function and arterial stiffness, weight loss, better insulin sensitivity.
  • Other gains: fewer cardiovascular events, better fitness and mood, less need for drugs.
  • Prescription: moderate aerobic exercise 30 minutes or more on most days; resistance training 2–3 days a week at moderate load.
  • Precautions: do not start exercise if resting BP is very high (about 200/110 or more) until it is controlled; avoid breath-holding and heavy isometric straining; long warm-up and cool-down (vasodilators and alpha-blockers cause post-exercise dizziness); use RPE with beta-blockers; watch for dehydration and low potassium with diuretics. 7 mEnergy systems. (W16)

ATP is the only direct energy for contraction and is stored for seconds. ATP–PC: creatine phosphate rebuilds ATP without oxygen, maximal efforts up to about 10 s (sprint, jump). Anaerobic glycolysis: glucose to lactate, 10 s to 2 min (400 m). Aerobic: full oxidation of carbohydrate and fat in mitochondria, beyond 2 min and at rest (walking, marathon). All overlap; intensity and duration decide the lead system. Draw the time line; add training implications (interval training for anaerobic, continuous for aerobic).

7 mDiscuss the effects of an exercise intervention on a hypertensive individual. (W25)

Acute post-exercise hypotension for up to 22 hours; chronic fall in resting BP of about 5–7 mmHg with aerobic training; mechanisms (sympathetic, resistance, endothelium, weight); other benefits; prescription (aerobic most days, resistance 2–3 days); precautions (very high resting BP, Valsalva, drugs: beta-blockers, vasodilators, diuretics; long cool-down).
