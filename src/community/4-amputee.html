1The below-knee prosthesis

  • Socket: patellar tendon bearing (PTB) or total surface bearing (TSB), usually with a soft liner.
  • Suspension: supracondylar cuff strap, supracondylar wedge (PTB-SC), sleeve, silicone liner with pin lock, suction; thigh corset for a very short or painful stump.
  • Shank: exoskeletal (hard outer shell) or endoskeletal (pylon tube with a cosmetic cover; adjustable alignment).
  • Foot: SACH (solid ankle cushion heel), single-axis, dynamic-response feet; the Jaipur foot (developed in Jaipur by Dr P K Sethi and craftsman Ram Chandra Sharma, distributed free by Bhagwan Mahaveer Viklang Sahayata Samiti) is cheap, waterproof, and allows squatting, sitting cross-legged and walking barefoot, which suits Indian life.
  • Weight-bearing areas: tolerant: patellar tendon, medial tibial flare, shaft of the fibula, pretibial muscles, popliteal area (lightly). Sensitive (relieved): tibial crest and tubercle, fibular head, distal end of tibia and fibula, hamstring tendons, patella.

2Pre-prosthetic and prosthetic training

  • Stump care: wound healing, skin inspection and hygiene, desensitisation (tapping, rubbing, textures).
  • Shaping: figure-of-eight elastic bandaging (tighter distally) or a shrinker sock, worn day and night, to reduce swelling and shape a cone.
  • Positioning: prevent knee flexion and hip flexion contractures: no pillow under the knee, knee straight in sitting (stump board), lie prone 20–30 minutes a few times a day.
  • Exercise: knee extensors and flexors, hip extensors and abductors, trunk and arms (for crutches); balance on one leg; endurance.
  • Mobility: transfers, crutch or walker walking, wheelchair if needed.
  • With the prosthesis: donning and doffing, wearing time built up gradually, skin check after each session, weight shifting in parallel bars, stepping, gait training, stairs, slopes, uneven ground, getting up from the floor, squatting with a Jaipur foot.

4Psychosocial issues of being an amputee

Psychosocial issues of being an amputee (W17, 7 marks).

  • Grief and loss: shock, denial, anger, sadness, then gradual acceptance; more severe after sudden trauma.
  • Depression and anxiety; post-traumatic stress after accidents.
  • Body image: embarrassment, hiding the limb, low self-esteem.
  • Phantom sensation and phantom limb pain; stump pain.
  • Loss of role and income: unable to return to work (drivers, farmers, labourers), financial strain on the family.
  • Dependence on others; strain on family relationships; sexual and marital concerns.
  • Stigma and social isolation; difficulty in marriage for young people; cultural beliefs (punishment, bad luck).
  • Practical worries: cost and repair of the prosthesis, travel to centres, inaccessible homes and toilets.

Helping

  • Counselling and psychology referral; meeting a successful amputee (peer support); amputee groups.
  • Early prosthetic fitting and good training, which restore mobility and confidence.
  • Manage phantom pain: explanation, desensitisation, mirror therapy, TENS, medication by the doctor.
  • Involve the family; vocational rehabilitation and adapted work; information on concessions and schemes; sport and leisure.

5Disability evaluation, difficulties and concessions

Principles of disability evaluation (W19, 5 marks); difficulties faced and concessions offered by government (W18, long answer).

Principles of disability evaluation

  • Done by a certified medical board after maximum medical improvement; based on measured loss of function, not opinion.
  • For amputations, fixed percentages by level (Indian guidelines): below-knee up to 8 cm (short stump) 70%, below-knee up to the lower third of the leg 60%, through the knee 75%, above-knee 80–85%, Syme's 50%.
  • Add 5% if the stump is unfit for a prosthesis, and up to 10% for complications (stiff proximal joint, neuroma, infection); for amputation of more than one limb, combine the values with the formula a + b(90 − a) ÷ 90 and add 10%; the total cannot exceed 100%.
  • 40% or more is a benchmark disability, qualifying for most benefits (see disability evaluation).

Difficulties faced by a below-knee amputee

  • Physical: walking long distances, uneven roads, stairs, squatting and the Indian toilet, carrying loads, stump pain and skin problems, more energy used in walking.
  • Work: driving with a right-leg amputation (adapted controls or automatic vehicle needed), heavy jobs, loss of income.
  • Psychosocial: section 4.
  • Practical: cost, repair and replacement of the prosthesis, distance to centres.

Concessions and benefits

  • Disability certificate and UDID card; free or subsidised prosthesis under ADIP and from BMVSS (Jaipur foot) camps and DDRCs.
  • Railway and state bus concessions; disability pension if eligible; income tax deduction; concessional loans for self-employment.
  • 4% job reservation, vocational training, and the RPWD Act's protection against losing a job because of disability (the employer must shift the employee to a suitable post).
  • Accident compensation from the Motor Accident Claims Tribunal; employees' compensation if injured at work; ESI benefits for insured workers.
  • Details on schemes and concessions.

6Worked long case: a teacher with a below-knee amputation in a village

From a real paper (S17)

"Following recent camp in the remote village, Mr. X 38 years old male, teacher by profession, below knee amputee was identified. Mr. X was discharged following selective amputation three weeks before. i) Give your functional diagnosis. ii) Plan your Community based rehabilitation program."

Spot the clues before you write

3 weeks after amputation: pre-prosthetic stage: wound, shaping, contracture prevention. remote village: CBR with local resources and camps. teacher: can return to work early with a prosthesis and accessible school; standing and walking in class.

One-line functional diagnosis

A 38-year-old teacher, 3 weeks after a below-knee amputation, in the pre-prosthetic stage with a healing, swollen stump and weak, deconditioned muscles, walking with crutches and unable to teach or manage the Indian toilet, in a remote village without a prosthetic centre.
