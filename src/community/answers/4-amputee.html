Psychosocial issues of being an amputee

Losing a limb affects not only walking but also the person's mind, body image, family, work and place in society. These issues decide whether the amputee uses the prosthesis and returns to normal life.

Psychosocial issues

  1. Grief and loss: shock, denial, anger, sadness, then gradual acceptance; more severe after sudden trauma.
  2. Depression and anxiety; post-traumatic stress after accidents.
  3. Body image: embarrassment, hiding the limb, low self-esteem.
  4. Phantom sensation and phantom limb pain; stump pain.
  5. Loss of role and income: unable to return to work (drivers, farmers, labourers); financial strain on the family.
  6. Dependence on others; strain on family relationships; sexual and marital concerns.
  7. Stigma and social isolation; difficulty in marriage for young people; cultural beliefs (punishment, bad luck).
  8. Practical worries: cost and repair of the prosthesis, travel to centres, inaccessible homes and toilets.

Management

  • Counselling and psychology referral.
  • Peer support: meeting a successful amputee; amputee groups.
  • Early prosthetic fitting and good training, which restore mobility and confidence (Jaipur foot allows squatting and cross-legged sitting).
  • Phantom pain: explanation, desensitisation, mirror therapy, TENS, medication by the doctor.
  • Involve the family; vocational rehabilitation and adapted work.
  • Information on concessions and schemes (UDID, free prosthesis under ADIP or from BMVSS); sport and leisure.

A confident amputee walks better: start counselling and peer contact in the pre-prosthetic stage, not after the limb is fitted.

Prosthetic check-out for a below-knee amputee

Check-out is the systematic examination of a new prosthesis on the amputee, to confirm fit, comfort, function and safety before final delivery. It has static (before donning, standing, sitting), dynamic (walking) and after-removal parts.

1. Before donning

Prosthesis as prescribed; finish and weight; socket smooth inside; suspension and foot correct; stump skin intact.

2. Standing

  • Comfortable with equal weight on both legs; pelvis level (correct length).
  • Knee in slight flexion; foot flat with slight toe-out like the other foot.
  • Socket in total contact without gaps; patellar tendon bar at the right level; suspension holds; pylon vertical in both views.

3. Sitting

Sits with the knee bent about 90° comfortably; no pinching behind the knee by the posterior wall; prosthesis stays on; shoe flat on the floor.

4. Walking

Smooth gait, equal step length, no pain, no pistoning; stairs and slopes. Look for deviations:

Excess knee flexion at heel strikeFoot too dorsiflexed or too far back; socket too flexed; heel too hard

5. After removal

Redness only over pressure-tolerant areas (patellar tendon, medial tibial flare, pretibial muscles), fading within 10–20 minutes; no abrasions or blisters; stump sock not wrinkled.

Sensitive areas to relieve in a PTB socket: tibial crest and tubercle, fibular head, distal tibia and fibula, hamstring tendons.
