Role of the physiotherapist in industrial health

Occupational health (Joint ILO/WHO Committee, 1950, revised 1995) aims at the highest degree of physical, mental and social well-being of workers, prevention of ill health caused by working conditions, protection from risks, and the adaptation of work to people and of each person to their job.

Industrial therapy is the application of physiotherapy to workers and workplaces: preventing work injury, treating work-related disorders close to the job, and returning injured workers to safe, productive work. The physiotherapist works in the industrial model: at or near the workplace, focused on the worker's function in the job and on prevention, with the employer as a partner and early return with modified duties.

Team work: with the worker, employer and supervisor, occupational health physician, occupational therapist, ergonomist and safety officer, psychologist, vocational counsellor and case manager (ESIC).

Indian context: most workers are in the informal sector (farms, construction, small units) with little protection; the Factories Act, 1948 and the ESI Act, 1948 cover health, safety and benefits.

Outcome: fewer injuries, days lost and compensation costs; better productivity.

Pre-placement in the worker care spectrum

The worker care spectrum runs from hiring to return to work: pre-placement → ability management (before injury: job analysis, ergonomic evaluation, injury prevention, employee fitness) → injury → disability management (acute care, FCE, work conditioning, work hardening) → return to work, which links back to ability management.

Pre-placement (post-offer) screening

The first step, long before any injury: after a job is offered, the worker's health and physical abilities are tested against the essential demands of that job.

Aims

  • Place the worker in a job they can do safely.
  • Find needs for training, aids or workplace changes.
  • Record a baseline for comparison if the worker is later injured.
  • Reduce injuries and compensation costs.

Components

  1. Job demand analysis of the post: weights, postures, frequency, environment.
  2. Health questionnaire and medical examination: past back or joint problems, heart and lung disease, vision and hearing.
  3. Musculoskeletal screen: posture, range, strength, flexibility.
  4. Job-matched functional tests: lifting and carrying to the job's weights and heights; pushing and pulling; positional tolerance (stooping, kneeling, climbing, overhead work); grip; aerobic fitness.
  5. Result: fit; fit with adjustments (training, tools, rotation); temporarily unfit with a plan to improve. Report to the employer in functional terms, not diagnoses.

Rules

  • Tests must be job-related, standardised and safe; the same tests for all candidates for a post.
  • Must not exclude persons with disability unfairly: the employer must consider reasonable accommodation (RPWD Act, 2016).
  • Results are confidential.

Example: a warehouse loader is tested on floor-to-waist lifts and carrying at the weights of the actual job.
