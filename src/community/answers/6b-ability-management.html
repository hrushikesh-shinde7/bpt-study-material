Importance of ergonomic evaluation at the workplace

Ergonomics (International Ergonomics Association, 2000) is the scientific discipline concerned with the understanding of interactions among humans and other elements of a system; simply, fitting the job to the person. An ergonomic evaluation studies the worker, the tasks, the workstation and the environment to find risk factors and correct them.

Importance

  • Identifies risk factors before injury occurs; prevents work-related musculoskeletal disorders, accidents and absence.
  • Improves comfort, productivity and quality; reduces fatigue, errors and compensation costs.
  • Gives objective scores to prioritise and justify changes, and to show improvement after them.
  • Helps return injured workers to work and include older workers and workers with disability.
  • Meets the employer's legal duty to provide a safe workplace.
  • Forms the basis for redesign, training, rest breaks, rotation and workstation exercises.

Steps of the evaluation

  1. Meet management and workers; review injury and absence records; symptom survey with the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire.
  2. Walk-through observation and video of the job; job and task analysis.
  3. Measure the workstation (heights, reaches, seat, screen), loads, forces, frequency and environment (light, noise, temperature).
  4. Score risk with a tool: RULA, REBA, NIOSH lifting equation, Strain Index.
  5. Recommend changes from the hierarchy of controls: redesign, tools and aids, layout, breaks and rotation, training, exercise.
  6. Re-evaluate after the changes.

Example: a REBA score of 11–15 for a loading task means very high risk, action needed now; a lower score after redesign shows the change worked.

Workplace health promotion

Workplace health promotion (Luxembourg Declaration, 1997): "the combined efforts of employers, employees and society to improve the health and well-being of people at work".

It works through:

  • Better work organisation and working environment.
  • Active participation of workers.
  • Personal development.

Examples: ergonomic workplaces; fitness and health checks; healthy canteens; tobacco-free workplace; stress management; flexible hours.

Physiotherapy role: employee fitness programmes, back care and posture education, workstation exercises; benefits are fewer injuries and sick days and better productivity.

Ergonomic physiotherapy evaluation

A systematic assessment of the worker, job and workstation by the physiotherapist to find musculoskeletal risk factors and correct them.

  1. Meet management and workers; injury and absence records; Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire.
  2. Walk-through observation and video; job and task analysis.
  3. Measure workstation heights, reaches, seat and screen; loads, forces, frequency; light, noise, temperature.
  4. Score risk: RULA, REBA, NIOSH lifting equation, Strain Index.
  5. Recommend changes by the hierarchy of controls: redesign, aids, layout, breaks and rotation, training, exercise.
  6. Re-evaluate after the changes.

Importance of task analysis

Job analysis has three parts: job description, job demand analysis and task analysis. Task analysis breaks each task into steps and records, for each step, the posture, force, repetition, duration, tools and environment, with the risk factors found.

How it is done

Walk-through observation and video of the worker; list the tasks; break each into steps; note risk factors; score with REBA, RULA or the NIOSH lifting equation.

Importance

  1. Finds the exact steps and postures that cause pain or injury, so the fix is specific.
  2. Basis for ergonomic redesign of tools, workstation and methods.
  3. Matches the job to the worker in pre-placement and return to work (compared with the functional capacity evaluation).
  4. Designs job-specific work conditioning and work hardening (job simulation).
  5. Shows what light duties are possible during recovery; guides job rotation.
  6. Basis for training safe methods and setting fair production rates.
  7. Supports compensation decisions.

Example

A labourer lifting 50 kg bags into a truck at about 3 feet: steps of bending to the floor, lifting, twisting and loading. The load is far above the NIOSH recommended limit of 23 kg, so the task must change: 25 kg bags or two-person lifts, trolleys or a ramp, loading from waist height, rotating tasks.

Task analysis tells you what to change; FCE tells you what the worker can do. Compare the two before return to work.

Employee fitness programme

A workplace programme to improve workers' health and fitness and reduce injuries and absence.

  • Plan: needs assessment and management support; a committee with workers.
  • Assess: health risk appraisal and fitness tests (BP, BMI, waist, aerobic fitness, strength, flexibility).
  • Programme: on-site exercise classes or gym, walking groups, stretching breaks, sports; education on back care, nutrition, weight, smoking, stress and sleep.
  • Support: incentives, convenient timing, family involvement.
  • Evaluate: participation, fitness changes, injuries, absence, productivity.

Benefits: fewer musculoskeletal injuries and sick days; better fitness, morale and productivity; lower health costs.

Define ergonomics; describe one ergonomic scale for musculoskeletal risk (REBA)

Ergonomics

International Ergonomics Association (2000): "the scientific discipline concerned with the understanding of interactions among humans and other elements of a system", and the profession that applies theory and methods to design in order to optimise human well-being and overall system performance. Simply, fitting the job to the person. Domains: physical, cognitive, organisational.

Rapid Entire Body Assessment (REBA)

Developed by Hignett and McAtamney (2000) to score whole-body postures in tasks that are unpredictable or varied (health care, service, industry).

  1. Group A: trunk, neck and legs, plus a load or force score → score A.
  2. Group B: upper arm, lower arm and wrist, plus a coupling (grip) score → score B.
  3. Scores A and B give score C from a table.
  4. An activity score is added (static posture held over a minute, repeated small actions, or rapid large posture changes) → REBA score 1–15.
REBA scoreRiskAction
1NegligibleNone needed
2–3LowMay be needed
4–7MediumNeeded
8–10HighNeeded soon
11–15Very highNeeded now

Use

  • Scores lifting, loading and patient-handling postures quickly by observation.
  • Ranks jobs for change; re-scoring after redesign shows improvement.
  • Related tools: RULA for upper-limb seated or standing work (score 1–7); NIOSH lifting equation (23 kg recommended limit in ideal conditions).

Viva: group A is trunk, neck and legs; a score of 11–15 means act now.

Workstation exercises

Short exercises done at the desk or workplace, 5–10 repetitions every 30–60 minutes, to break static posture in sedentary workers.

  • Neck: chin tucks; side bending and turning, holding 10 seconds.
  • Shoulders and upper back: shoulder rolls and shrugs; squeeze the shoulder blades back; overhead reaches.
  • Back: seated back extension over the chair back; seated twists; standing back bends; pelvic tilts in sitting.
  • Wrists and hands: flexor and extensor stretches; fist and spread fingers; tendon gliding.
  • Legs: ankle pumps; seated knee straightening; heel raises; marching.
  • Eyes: look into the distance; blink; palming.

Job demand analysis

A systematic record of the physical, mental and environmental demands of a job, with weights, distances and how often each occurs; one of the three parts of job analysis.

  • Physical: lifting, carrying, pushing and pulling; sitting, standing and walking time; bending, kneeling, squatting, climbing, reaching above shoulder; gripping, fine finger work, repetition; vibration.
  • Mental and sensory: attention, memory, decisions, vision, hearing, speed and deadlines.
  • Environment: heat, cold, noise, light, dust, fumes, heights, wet floors, shift work.
  • Frequency (Dictionary of Occupational Titles): occasionally up to a third of the day; frequently a third to two-thirds; constantly more than two-thirds.

Uses: physical demand level of the job; pre-placement; ergonomic change; matching with the FCE; return to work.

Cognitive ergonomics

Ergonomics (International Ergonomics Association, 2000): the scientific discipline concerned with understanding interactions among humans and other elements of a system, applied to optimise human well-being and system performance. It has three domains:

DomainConcerned with
PhysicalAnatomy, anthropometry, physiology and biomechanics: postures, manual handling, repetition, musculoskeletal disorders, workplace layout
CognitiveMental processes (perception, memory, reasoning, motor response): mental workload, decision-making, skilled performance, human error, work stress, training
OrganisationalWork systems: shift design, teamwork, communication, work pace, participation

Areas of cognitive ergonomics and solutions

  • Mental workload: overload causes errors and stress; underload causes boredom and lapses. Balance tasks and breaks.
  • Attention and alertness: monotonous monitoring, night shifts and fatigue reduce vigilance; breaks and task variety.
  • Display and control design: clear labels, logical layout, warnings that stand out, controls that move the expected way.
  • Human error: design so mistakes are hard to make and easy to correct (checklists, forcing functions).
  • Human–computer interaction: simple screens and software, readable fonts, fewer interruptions.
  • Decision-making and skilled performance: training and decision support for complex tasks.
  • Work stress: stress management.

Examples

  • Control rooms: clear displays and warnings that stand out.
  • Drivers and pilots: alertness, fatigue and breaks.
  • Surgeons and health-care workers: checklists against error; night-shift fatigue.
  • Call centres: workload, monotony, task variety.

Stress and mental overload raise muscle tension and the chance that pain persists, so combine cognitive changes with physical ergonomics, breaks and relaxation.

Purpose and principles of ergonomics

Ergonomics (International Ergonomics Association, 2000): "the scientific discipline concerned with the understanding of interactions among humans and other elements of a system", applied to design to optimise human well-being and overall system performance. Simply, fitting the job to the person. Domains: physical, cognitive, organisational.

Purpose

  • Prevent work-related musculoskeletal disorders and accidents.
  • Reduce fatigue and errors.
  • Improve comfort, productivity and quality.
  • Include older workers and persons with disability by adapting work.
  • Reduce absence and compensation costs; help injured workers return to work.

Principles

  1. Work in neutral postures: spine in its curves, elbows by the side, wrists straight, neck upright.
  2. Reduce excessive force: lighter loads, mechanical aids, good grips.
  3. Keep everything within easy reach.
  4. Work at the right height: around elbow height; lower for heavy work, higher for fine work.
  5. Reduce excessive repetition and static loading; vary tasks.
  6. Minimise fatigue with rest breaks and job rotation.
  7. Minimise contact stress and vibration.
  8. Provide clearance and space; allow change of posture (sit–stand).
  9. Keep a comfortable environment: light, temperature, noise.
  10. Make displays and controls easy to understand; involve workers in changes (participatory ergonomics).

Applied example: at a computer workstation the screen is about an arm's length away with its top at or slightly below eye level, the keyboard is at elbow height with wrists straight, and the worker takes a movement break every 30–60 minutes.

Work heights: precision work above elbow height, light work at elbow height, heavy work below elbow height.

Workstation exercises for a sedentary worker

Sedentary desk workers (clerks, bank staff, computer operators) develop neck and shoulder pain with a forward head and rounded shoulders, thoracic stiffness, low back pain from slumped sitting, wrist and hand symptoms, eye strain, weight gain and low fitness. Workstation exercises break static posture during the working day.

Dose

5–10 repetitions, every 30–60 minutes; hold stretches about 10 seconds.

RegionExercises
NeckChin tucks; side bending and turning, holding 10 seconds
Shoulders and upper backShoulder rolls and shrugs; squeeze the shoulder blades back; arm reaches overhead
Mid backSeated back extension over the chair back; seated twists; side bends
Wrists and handsWrist flexor and extensor stretches; fist and spread fingers; tendon gliding
Low back and hipsStanding back bends with hands on hips; pelvic tilts in sitting; stand up and walk
LegsAnkle pumps and circles; seated knee straightening; heel raises in standing; marching

Pair with

  • Workstation set-up: screen about an arm's length away (50–100 cm), top at or slightly below eye level; feet flat, lumbar support; keyboard and mouse at elbow height with wrists straight.
  • Outside work hours: deep neck flexor, scapular retractor and lower trapezius strengthening, thoracic mobility, pectoral stretching and general fitness; strengthening and fitness reduced neck pain in office workers (Chen et al., 2018).

Teach 2–3 exercises per micro-break so the worker actually does them.

Job analysis

Job analysis: a systematic study of a job to record what the worker does, what it demands and where the risks lie. It has three parts:

PartWhat it records
Job descriptionTitle, purpose, main duties, tools and machines, materials, hours and shifts, breaks, supervision, pay system (piece rate or salary)
Job demand analysisPhysical, mental and environmental demands and how often each occurs (occasionally, frequently, constantly)
Task analysisEach task in steps with posture, force, repetition, duration, tools and environment; risk factors found

Purpose: prevention and ergonomic redesign; pre-placement; matching with the FCE; designing work hardening; light duties; training; compensation.
