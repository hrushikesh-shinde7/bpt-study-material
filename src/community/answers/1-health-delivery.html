The primary health care setting

Primary health care (Alma-Ata, 1978): essential health care, made universally accessible to individuals and families in the community through their full participation, at a cost the community and country can afford; the first level of contact with the health system, as close as possible to where people live and work.

  • Principles: equitable distribution; community participation; intersectoral coordination; appropriate technology; focus on prevention and promotion.
  • Eight elements: health education; food and nutrition; safe water and sanitation; maternal and child health with family planning; immunisation; control of local endemic diseases; treatment of common diseases and injuries; essential drugs.
  • Where in India: village (ASHA, anganwadi worker) → sub-centre → PHC, now upgraded as Ayushman Arogya Mandirs.

Physiotherapy here means education, screening, antenatal classes, home programmes and CBR.

WHO definition of health

WHO Constitution (1948): "Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity."

  • Dimensions: physical, mental, social; often added spiritual, emotional and vocational.
  • Disease: a disturbance of the structure or function of body or mind, with signs and symptoms, that departs from health.
  • Health is a continuum: positive health → ill-health → death.
  • Determinants: biology and genes; behaviour and lifestyle; environment; socioeconomic conditions; health services.

The physiotherapist works along the whole continuum (promotion, prevention, treatment, rehabilitation), not only with disease.

How socioeconomic factors affect physiotherapy services in India

  • Cost: much care is paid out of pocket; repeated sessions and lost wages are hard for daily earners.
  • Distribution: physiotherapists concentrated in cities and private clinics; few government posts at CHCs and PHCs.
  • Distance and transport, especially for disabled and elderly villagers.
  • Awareness and literacy: rest believed to be the cure; preference for traditional healers.
  • Gender: women may lack permission or time; preference for female therapists.
  • Home environment: crowded homes, floor-level living, Indian toilets.
  • Insurance: limited cover for outpatient rehabilitation (PM-JAY mainly hospital care).

Solutions: CBR; camps; tele-rehabilitation; physiotherapists in Ayushman Arogya Mandirs and CHCs; simple home programmes; community awareness.

Health care delivery system in India

Public care in rural India is a three-tier pyramid: primary, secondary, tertiary (population per centre in plains; hilly and tribal areas in brackets).

Sub-centres and PHCs are now Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (12 service packages). Urban: urban PHCs, municipal hospitals and a large private sector.

Levels of health care delivery

  • Primary level: first contact, close to home: village (ASHA, anganwadi), sub-centre (5,000; 3,000) and PHC (30,000; 20,000), now Ayushman Arogya Mandirs. Health education, maternal and child care, immunisation, common illness, NCD screening.
  • Secondary level: CHC (1,20,000; 80,000; 30 beds; surgeon, physician, obstetrician, paediatrician) and district hospital: specialist and emergency care, X-ray, laboratory, physiotherapy department.
  • Tertiary level: medical colleges, AIIMS, national institutes: super-specialist care, teaching, research.

Referral moves up; follow-up moves down.

A pyramid: sub-centre at the base, PHC, CHC, district hospital, medical college at the top, with population norms.

Functioning of primary health centres

The primary health centre (PHC) is the first unit with a doctor in the rural three-tier system. It serves 30,000 people in plains (20,000 in hilly and tribal areas) and is the referral unit for about six sub-centres; it refers on to the CHC.

Staff and set-up

  • Medical officer, nurses, pharmacist, laboratory technician; 4–6 beds.
  • Now upgraded as an Ayushman Arogya Mandir giving comprehensive primary care in 12 service packages.

Functions

  1. Medical care: outpatient and basic inpatient treatment of common illness and injury; referral.
  2. Maternal and child health: antenatal, delivery and postnatal care; newborn care; family planning.
  3. Immunisation.
  4. Disease control: communicable (tuberculosis, malaria, leprosy); NCD screening (hypertension, diabetes, common cancers).
  5. National health programmes carried out locally.
  6. Health education and promotion; nutrition.
  7. Safe water and environmental sanitation.
  8. Collection and reporting of vital statistics and disease data.
  9. Basic laboratory services and essential drugs.
  10. Training and supervision of ANMs, health workers and ASHAs.
  11. School health; elderly, palliative and mental health care (AAM packages).

Place of physiotherapy

  • Health education (back care, ergonomics, fitness); antenatal and postnatal classes.
  • Screening of posture, development and falls risk; training ASHAs and anganwadi workers.
  • Home programmes and CBR; referral of complex cases to CHC or district hospital.

Link every function to the eight elements of primary health care (Alma-Ata, 1978).

Principles and scope of community physiotherapy

Community physiotherapy: the application of physiotherapy to the whole community, with a preventive and promotive emphasis, to optimise health and function where people live and work.

Principles

  1. Prevention and health promotion first; then treatment and rehabilitation.
  2. Equity and access: services for rural, poor, elderly, women and disabled people.
  3. Community participation and ownership.
  4. Holistic, biopsychosocial approach using the ICF.
  5. Teamwork and intersectoral coordination (health, education, social welfare, industry).
  6. Appropriate, low-cost technology and local resources.
  7. Education and empowerment for self-management.
  8. Evidence-based, measured outcomes; sustainability.

Where it is delivered

  • Primary level: villages, anganwadis, sub-centres, PHCs and Ayushman Arogya Mandirs: education, screening, training ASHAs and anganwadi workers, home programmes, camps.
  • Secondary and tertiary: CHCs and district hospitals, with referral up and follow-up back to the community; tele-rehabilitation.

Tie the scope to the levels of prevention: primordial (active schools), primary (fitness, ergonomics), secondary (screening), tertiary (rehabilitation, CBR).
