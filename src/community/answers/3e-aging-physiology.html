Visual changes due to ageing

With ageing the eye lets in less light and focuses and adapts less well; these changes add to falls risk.

  • Presbyopia: cannot focus on near objects.
  • Smaller pupil and yellower lens: less light reaches the retina; colours change.
  • Poor dark adaptation and glare tolerance.
  • Lower contrast sensitivity and depth perception; narrower visual field.
  • Cataract, glaucoma and macular degeneration more common.

Effect: misjudges steps and kerbs; falls at night; reading difficulty. Advice: good lighting without glare, contrast edges on steps, regular eye checks.

Musculoskeletal changes due to ageing

Ageing reduces the reserve of muscle, bone and connective tissue; much of the loss is secondary (inactivity) and responds to exercise.

Age-related changes in the nervous system

Ageing is the gradual decline in the body's reserve and its ability to adapt to stress. In the nervous system it shows as slower sensing, processing and responding.

Central nervous system

  • Brain volume falls, more in the frontal lobes and hippocampus; loss of neurons in some areas, of synapses and dendrites; white matter changes.
  • Fall in neurotransmitters: dopamine (slower movement), acetylcholine (memory).
  • Cognition: slower processing speed and reaction time; weaker short-term and working memory and divided attention; knowledge, vocabulary and judgement kept.
  • Motor control: slower, less smooth movement; poorer fine coordination; slower anticipatory postural adjustments.
  • Sleep: lighter, broken; earlier waking.

Peripheral nervous system

  • Loss of motor and sensory fibres; slower nerve conduction; ankle jerks reduced or absent.
  • Reduced vibration and position sense, most in the feet.

Autonomic nervous system

  • Weaker baroreflex: dizziness on standing, postural hypotension.
  • Poor temperature control; bladder urgency and nocturia.

Special senses

Presbyopia, glare and contrast problems; presbycusis; loss of vestibular hair cells (unsteady on turning); reduced taste and smell.

Effect and physiotherapy

Late, weak balance reactions, so falls rise with age. PT: balance, strength, dual-task and aerobic training; rise slowly from lying; good lighting.

Contrast primary ageing (everyone) with secondary ageing (disease, inactivity), which exercise can change.

Age-related changes in the neurological system and the effects of exercise

Ageing reduces reserve and the ability to adapt to stress; in the nervous system it makes sensing, processing and responding slower.

Changes

  • Brain: volume loss (frontal lobes, hippocampus); fewer synapses; white matter change; less dopamine and acetylcholine.
  • Cognition: slower processing and reaction time; weaker working memory and divided attention.
  • Motor control: slower, less coordinated movement; slower anticipatory postural adjustments.
  • Peripheral: fewer fibres, slower conduction, reduced ankle jerks and vibration and position sense in the feet; fewer motor units.
  • Autonomic: weaker baroreflex (postural hypotension), poor temperature control, bladder urgency.
  • Senses: presbyopia, presbycusis, vestibular loss. Result: late, weak balance reactions and falls.

Effects of exercise

  • Strength and power: resistance training works at any age; residents aged about 90 gained 174% in strength after 8 weeks (Fiatarone, 1990). Early gains are neural (better recruitment and firing), later gains add muscle size.
  • Nervous system: faster reaction time; better coordination and balance reactions.
  • Falls: exercise reduced the rate of falls by 23%; balance and functional exercise worked best (Sherrington, 2019).
  • Brain: aerobic exercise improves blood flow to the brain, memory and mood, and lowers the risk of cognitive decline.

Dose (WHO, 2020) and precautions

150–300 minutes moderate aerobic a week; strengthening on 2 or more days; balance and strength on 3 or more days. Rise slowly (postural hypotension); avoid breath-holding; supervise balance work; Borg scale with beta-blockers.

Age-related changes in the respiratory system

Ageing is the gradual loss of the body's reserve. An older person breathes normally at rest, but the loss shows on effort, in pneumonia, after surgery and with bed rest. Smoking, inactivity and disease add secondary loss.

Effect on function

Breathlessness at lower workloads, lower exercise tolerance, more pneumonia and slower recovery after surgery.

Physiotherapy implications

  • Pre-operative breathing exercises and early mobilisation: lung complications are 2–3 times more likely at 60–79.
  • Upright positioning: PaO₂ about 5 mmHg higher sitting than lying.
  • Inspiratory muscle training: maximal inspiratory pressure up about 26 cmH₂O.
  • Aerobic training: VO₂max up about 16%; posture and chest mobility work against kyphosis.
  • Airway clearance in infection; avoid long bed rest; stop smoking; vaccination advice.
  • Monitor oxygen saturation and Borg, not breathlessness alone.

Viva point: residual volume is the lung volume that rises with age.

Age-related changes in the sensorimotor system

The sensorimotor system senses the body and surroundings, processes the information and produces movement. With ageing all three steps slow: slower sensing, slower processing, slower and weaker responses.

Sensory changes

  • Touch, vibration, proprioception: fewer receptors and sensory fibres; reduced position and vibration sense, most in the feet: poor balance on uneven ground and in the dark.
  • Vision: presbyopia, less light reaches the retina, poor dark adaptation, lower contrast and depth perception: misjudges steps.
  • Vestibular: loss of hair cells and nerve fibres: unsteady on turning.
  • Hearing: presbycusis; slower nerve conduction overall.

Central processing

  • Brain volume loss, fewer synapses, less dopamine.
  • Slower processing speed and reaction time; weaker divided attention (dual tasks).

Motor changes

  • Sarcopenia with loss of type II fibres and fewer motor units: strength and power fall faster than mass.
  • Slower, less smooth movement; poorer fine coordination; slower anticipatory postural adjustments.
  • Gait: slower, shorter steps, wider base, more time on both feet.

Result

Balance reactions are late and weak: reduced ankle range and strength shift control from the ankle strategy to the hip strategy, the corrective step is too slow or too short, and walking while talking (dual-tasking) worsens gait. A slip or trip becomes a fall, so falls rise with age.

Physiotherapy

Balance training (cut falls by 23%), resistance and power training, dual-task practice, good lighting and footwear.

Physiological changes in the respiratory system with ageing

The lungs lose reserve with age; it shows in illness, after surgery and on effort.

  • Chest wall stiffer (costal cartilage calcification, kyphosis); respiratory muscles weaker.
  • Loss of elastic recoil; small airways close earlier in expiration.
  • FEV₁ and vital capacity fall; residual volume rises; total lung capacity changes little.
  • More ventilation–perfusion mismatch: resting PaO₂ falls slowly.
  • Weaker cough, slower mucociliary clearance; blunted response to low O₂ and high CO₂.

PT: breathing exercises, early mobilisation and airway clearance in illness.
