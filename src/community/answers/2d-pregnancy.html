Diastasis recti: evaluation and management

Diastasis recti: separation of the two rectus abdominis muscles along the linea alba, from stretching in pregnancy. In first-time mothers it is present in 33% at 21 weeks of pregnancy, 60% at 6 weeks after birth, 45% at 6 months and 33% at 12 months (Sperstad et al., 2016).

Risk factors and effects

  • Usually listed: multiple or large babies, many pregnancies, weak abdominals, obesity, heavy lifting (the Sperstad study found no clear risk factor).
  • Effects: abdominal doming or bulging, poor trunk support, possibly back and pelvic pain, cosmetic worry; rarely hernia.

Evaluation

  • Crook lying; she lifts head and shoulders slowly until the shoulder blades just clear the bed.
  • Fingers across the midline at three levels: at the umbilicus, 4.5 cm above and 4.5 cm below; count finger-widths between the muscle edges (or calipers, ultrasound). Two finger-widths or more at any level = diastasis.
  • Note depth and tension of the linea alba (firm, or fingers sink) and doming.
  • Record pain, back and pelvic symptoms, pelvic floor function and function (lifting, getting up).

Management in pregnancy

  • Roll to the side to get up from lying; avoid straining and breath-holding.
  • Transversus abdominis and pelvic floor activation; good posture; avoid exercises that make the abdomen dome.

Management after birth

  • Transversus drawing-in with pelvic floor; pelvic tilts; side-lying and four-point exercises.
  • Progress to head lifts and curl-ups only when she can keep the abdomen flat (pre-activate transversus first); later, loaded functional training.
  • Splinting the abdomen with crossed hands or a binder when coughing and getting up in the early days.
  • Correct lifting; constipation care; weight management.
  • Surgical repair only if a large gap with hernia or major symptoms persists after long rehabilitation.

Diastasis recti

Diastasis recti: separation of the two rectus abdominis muscles along the linea alba, from stretching in pregnancy; 60% of first-time mothers at 6 weeks after birth (Sperstad et al., 2016).

  • Effects: abdominal doming, poor trunk support, back and pelvic pain; rarely hernia.
  • Test: crook lying head lift; finger-widths at the umbilicus and 4.5 cm above and below; two finger-widths or more = diastasis.
  • Management: roll to the side to get up; avoid straining; transversus abdominis with pelvic floor; curl-ups only when the abdomen stays flat; splinting with crossed hands; surgery rarely.

Exercises contraindicated during pregnancy

  • Contact sports: boxing, hockey, kabaddi.
  • High fall risk: horse riding, skiing, gymnastics, off-road cycling.
  • Scuba diving, sky diving; hot yoga or hot Pilates; unaccustomed exercise at high altitude (ACOG, 2020).
  • Prolonged supine exercise after the first trimester (supine hypotension).
  • Double leg raises and full sit-ups (strain the abdominal wall).
  • Heavy lifting with breath-holding; ballistic and end-range stretching (lax ligaments); deep squats and lunges with pelvic girdle pain; high-impact jumping late in pregnancy.

No exercise at all with absolute contraindications, for example placenta praevia after 26 weeks or pre-eclampsia.

Six physiological changes in pregnancy

  1. Hormones: progesterone, oestrogen and relaxin rise → ligament laxity, smooth muscle relaxation.
  2. Heart and blood: blood volume up 40–50%; cardiac output up; resting HR up 10–20 beats; dilutional anaemia; clots more easily.
  3. Supine position: from about 20 weeks the uterus presses on the inferior vena cava → supine hypotension.
  4. Breathing: diaphragm rises; tidal volume and minute ventilation up; FRC falls.
  5. Musculoskeletal: weight gain; centre of gravity forward; abdominals stretch (diastasis); pelvic floor loaded; feet flatten.
  6. Gut, kidneys, metabolism: heartburn, constipation; frequency and leakage; BMR and heat production up.

Early bird classes

Early bird classes are antenatal education sessions in the first trimester, before the main antenatal classes (from about 28 weeks).

  • Content: changes of pregnancy; nutrition; posture and back care; safe exercise; pelvic floor training; discomforts of early pregnancy (nausea, fatigue); warning signs.
  • Purpose: build healthy habits early; prevent problems; reduce anxiety.
  • Later antenatal classes then prepare her for labour.

Run them where women already gather: anganwadi, PHC or the PMSMA clinic.

Guidelines for women exercising in pregnancy

Benefits

Less gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia and excessive weight gain; better mood and sleep; less back pain; better fitness for labour and recovery. Activity is not associated with miscarriage or preterm birth (Canadian guideline, 2018).

Dose

  • At least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity over at least 3 days, ideally daily; aerobic plus resistance (Canadian guideline; ACOG). Women active before pregnancy can continue their usual activity.
  • Intensity: moderate by the talk test (can talk, not sing) or Borg 12–14, because heart rate is altered in pregnancy.
  • Good choices: walking, stationary cycling, swimming and water exercise, low-impact aerobics, prenatal yoga and Pilates (not hot), light resistance, daily pelvic floor training.

Precautions

  • Warm-up and cool-down; drink water; avoid heat and humidity.
  • Avoid lying flat for long after the first trimester (side-lying or inclined).
  • Avoid breath-holding and heavy straining; avoid end-range and bouncing stretches (lax ligaments); good footwear; eat enough.

Avoid (ACOG, 2020)

  • Contact sports; high fall-risk activities; scuba and sky diving; hot yoga; unaccustomed exercise at high altitude.

Absolute contraindications

  • Significant heart disease; restrictive lung disease; incompetent cervix or cerclage; multiple pregnancy at risk of preterm labour; persistent bleeding in 2nd or 3rd trimester; placenta praevia after 26 weeks; preterm labour; ruptured membranes; pre-eclampsia or pregnancy-induced hypertension; severe anaemia.

Stop exercising if

  • Vaginal bleeding; regular painful contractions; leaking fluid; breathlessness before starting; dizziness; headache; chest pain; muscle weakness affecting balance; calf pain or swelling.

Screen first and work with the obstetrician; high-risk pregnancies need tailored or reduced activity.

Physiotherapy management of hyperemesis gravidarum

Hyperemesis gravidarum: severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy with dehydration, weight loss and electrolyte imbalance, often needing hospital care; medical treatment is fluids and anti-emetics.

  • Relaxation and slow breathing to ease nausea and anxiety.
  • Acupressure wristbands at P6 (some women find them helpful).
  • Gentle bed and chair exercises and ankle pumps to prevent deconditioning and clots while in bed.
  • Early walking as she improves; energy conservation.
  • Posture and positioning; emotional support.

Weight gain (Institute of Medicine, 2009)

  • Underweight 12.5–18 kg; normal BMI 11.5–16 kg; overweight 7–11.5 kg; obese 5–9 kg.

Posture in pregnancy: centre of gravity forward, changed lumbar curve, knees locked; side-lying rest with pillows under the abdomen and between the knees.

Musculoskeletal dysfunctions in pregnancy

Caused by relaxin (lax ligaments), weight gain, centre of gravity forward and stretched abdominals.

  • Low back pain: posture, pelvic tilts, cat–camel, side-lying with a pillow between the knees, belt.
  • Pelvic girdle pain (sacroiliac, symphysis pubis): pelvic belt; knees together when turning; sit to dress.
  • Diastasis recti: transversus with pelvic floor; roll to the side.
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome (fluid): night wrist splint in neutral, nerve gliding.
  • Leg cramps; heel, foot and knee pain; rib pain; De Quervain's, coccyx pain, meralgia paraesthetica.

Weak, loaded pelvic floor also gives urinary leakage: daily pelvic floor training.

Aims and principles of obstetric physiotherapy

Obstetric physiotherapy is the care of the woman through pregnancy, labour and the postnatal period, in antenatal and postnatal classes and individual treatment.

Aims

  1. Help her adapt to the physical changes of pregnancy; prevent or treat discomforts (back and pelvic girdle pain, cramps, swelling).
  2. Keep her fit and active safely; control weight gain.
  3. Protect the back, pelvis, abdomen and pelvic floor.
  4. Prepare for labour: knowledge, positions, relaxation, breathing, less fear.
  5. Help recovery after birth and the return to fitness.
  6. Prevent long-term problems: incontinence, prolapse, back pain, diastasis.

Principles

  1. Assess first; screen for contraindications and red flags; work with the obstetrician.
  2. Moderate, regular exercise with precautions (150 minutes a week; talk test or Borg 12–14; no long supine lying after the first trimester); progress with the trimester.
  3. Education and self-management: posture, body mechanics, rest positions.
  4. Include the partner and family; respect beliefs; group classes for support.
  5. Individualise: high-risk pregnancies need tailored or reduced activity.

Delivery

  • Early bird class (first trimester): changes, posture, safe exercise, pelvic floor, warning signs.
  • Antenatal classes from about 28 weeks: labour preparation, relaxation and breathing.
  • Postnatal: early pelvic floor and abdominal exercise, feeding posture, back care.

Know the stop signs: bleeding, painful contractions, leaking fluid, dizziness, chest pain, calf pain or swelling.

  3. Two finger-widths or more at any level = diastasis.

  5. Record pain, back and pelvic symptoms, pelvic floor function, lifting and getting up.

Antenatal programme: goals and structure of the class

Antenatal classes are group sessions usually from about 28 weeks, weekly for 6–8 sessions, in groups of 8–12, with the partner invited to some. An early bird class in the first trimester comes before them.

Goals

  • Knowledge of pregnancy, labour and the baby.
  • Fitness and posture; relief of discomforts.
  • Pelvic floor training.
  • Relaxation, breathing and positions for labour.
  • Postnatal recovery and baby care.
  • Confidence and less fear.

Topics across the course

  1. Body changes and posture.
  2. Exercise and discomforts.
  3. Pelvic floor.
  4. Signs of labour and when to go to hospital.
  5. Stages of labour and pain relief.
  6. Labour positions and breathing (partner session).
  7. Caesarean section.
  8. Postnatal care, feeding and baby handling.

Safety in class

  • Screen each woman for contraindications and red flags first; work with the obstetrician.
  • Moderate intensity by the talk test or Borg 12–14; no long supine lying; drink water; avoid heat.
  • Teach the stop signs: bleeding, painful contractions, leaking fluid, dizziness, calf pain or swelling.
