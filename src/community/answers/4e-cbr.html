Aspects (features) of community-based rehabilitation

CBR (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004): "a strategy within general community development for the rehabilitation, equalization of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities".

  • Takes place in the person's own home, village or town, using local resources and simple technology.
  • The person and family are active partners and decide goals.
  • Community workers (CBR workers, anganwadi workers, ASHAs, teachers, volunteers) do most of the work, trained and supported by professionals.
  • Covers health, education, livelihood, social life and empowerment, not only therapy.
  • Works with existing services (PHC, schools, panchayats, schemes) and refers to institutions when needed.
  • Low cost, wide coverage, sustained by the community.

Principles of CBR

CBR is rehabilitation within the community, by the community, using local resources (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004).

  • Community participation and ownership: the village plans and runs the programme (CBR committee, gram panchayat).
  • Inclusion and equal opportunity in ordinary schools, jobs and festivals.
  • Empowerment and self-advocacy through self-help groups and disabled people's organisations.
  • Local resources and appropriate technology: aids made by the village carpenter.
  • Multisectoral approach; integration with existing services (PHC, anganwadi, school).
  • Two-way referral with PHC, district hospital, DDRC.
  • Sustainability; gender equality and respect for children.

CBR teams work in the transdisciplinary way: one key worker (CBR worker or family) delivers the programme, trained and supported by the professionals.

Benefits of teamwork in CBR

In CBR, rehabilitation is carried out by persons with disability, their families and communities together with health, education, vocational and social services. No single worker can meet all needs, so CBR depends on a team.

The team

  • Village: person and family, CBR worker, anganwadi worker, ASHA, teacher, self-help group, panchayat, local craftsmen.
  • Block and district: PHC doctor and ANM, CBR supervisor, DEIC, DDRC, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, prosthetist, psychologist, social worker, NGOs.
  • National: national institutes and composite regional centres.

How the team works

Multidisciplinary (each works separately) → interdisciplinary (shared findings, common goals) → transdisciplinary (one key worker delivers the programme, trained by the others): the usual model in CBR.

Benefits

  1. Whole-person care: disability touches health, school, work and social life, so many sectors act together.
  2. Wide reach: few professionals train and support many local workers.
  3. One consistent plan: shared goals avoid repeated assessments and conflicting advice.
  4. Early identification by village workers and quick referral both ways; follow-up close to home.
  5. Lower cost: local resources and schemes are pooled, so the programme lasts.
  6. Attitude change: when many local people are involved, stigma falls.
  7. Empowerment: the person and family are team members who set goals; the community owns the programme.

Teamwork makes CBR cheaper and sustainable, with continuity of care from village to institution.

Community-based rehabilitation

Definition (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004): "a strategy within general community development for the rehabilitation, equalization of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities", carried out by people with disabilities, their families and communities together with health, education, vocational and social services.

History

The WHO promoted CBR after Alma-Ata (1978), because most disabled people in low-income countries could not reach institutions; joint position papers (1994, 2004) widened it to rights and inclusion; the WHO CBR guidelines (2010) set out the CBR matrix.

Features

  • In the person's own home and village, with local resources and simple technology.
  • Person and family are partners; community workers deliver most of the work.
  • Works through existing services; refers to institutions when needed; low cost, wide coverage.

Principles

Community participation; inclusion; empowerment; local resources; multisectoral approach; integration with existing services; referral; sustainability; gender equality.

CBR strategies in rural and urban areas

CBR is rehabilitation within the community, using local resources and existing services, with the person and family as partners (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004). Strategies differ because rural and urban areas have different resources.

Steps of a CBR programme (both settings)

  1. Situation analysis: meet leaders, map resources, household survey to identify persons with disability.
  2. Plan with the community: CBR committee; priorities from the CBR matrix.
  3. Train local workers and families in simple assessment, home programmes and referral.
  4. Implement: home visits, camps, school inclusion, aids, livelihood links, self-help groups.
  5. Monitor and evaluate; hand over to local ownership.

Urban strategies

  • Urban health centres and community centres for clinics and camps.
  • Clubs, mahila mandals and social centres for awareness, volunteers and funds.
  • Ordinary schools for inclusion and screening.
  • Industries for vocational training, jobs and workplace adaptation.
  • Sports centres for adapted sport; NGOs and corporate social responsibility funds.

Rural strategies

  • PHCs, rural hospitals and the district hospital for screening, treatment and referral.
  • Anganwadi workers and ASHAs for identification and home programmes.
  • Locomotor aids from local materials made by village craftsmen: bamboo crutches, wooden walkers, parallel bars, standing frames, CP chairs.
  • Panchayat support; self-help groups for livelihood.
  • Camps for disability certificates and aids.

End with two-way referral links (village → PHC → DDRC → national institute) and sustainability through community ownership.

Approaches to CBR

CBR (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004) is "a strategy within general community development for the rehabilitation, equalization of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities". It can be delivered in several ways, often combined in one programme.

1. Home-based approach

A trained CBR worker visits the home and teaches the family a home programme (positioning, exercise, ADL); the family carries it out daily.

2. Centre-based approach in the community

A village or ward CBR centre or anganwadi where people come for therapy, aids and group activities.

3. Outreach from institutions

Camps and mobile teams from hospitals, DDRCs and NGOs bring specialist assessment, certificates and aids to the village.

4. Health-system integrated approach

CBR delivered through primary health care, RBSK and anganwadis, rather than a separate system.

5. Community development and rights-based approach

Disabled people's organisations and self-help groups lead, linking with livelihood, education and social programmes.

Twin-track approach

Includes disabled people in mainstream programmes while also meeting their specific needs.

What all approaches share

  • Local resources and appropriate technology; the family as partner.
  • The five components of the CBR matrix: health, education, livelihood, social, empowerment.
  • Two-way referral to institutions; community ownership for sustainability.

Programme steps whatever the approach: survey → plan with a CBR committee → train → implement → evaluate.

Role of the anganwadi worker in CBR

The anganwadi worker is the village-level worker of ICDS (1975), serving children under 6, pregnant women and nursing mothers.

  • Identification: house-to-house survey, growth monitoring, milestone checks.
  • Referral: to PHC, RBSK team and DEIC; helps with certificates and UDID.
  • Prevention: immunisation, supplementary nutrition, vitamin A, iodised salt, antenatal care.
  • Home programmes taught by the physiotherapist: exercises, positioning, stimulation.
  • Pre-school inclusion; counselling mothers; records and links to schemes.

Role of the anganwadi worker in CBR

The anganwadi worker is the village-level worker of the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS, 1975). She runs an anganwadi centre for about 400–800 people (300–800 in tribal and hilly areas) with one helper, and serves children under 6, pregnant women and nursing mothers. Being in every village, she is a key member of the CBR team.

Link with the physiotherapist

The physiotherapist trains her in simple assessment, positioning, exercises and when to refer, and reviews the children she identifies; the anganwadi can also serve as a training place.

She works at all three levels of prevention: primary (immunisation, nutrition), secondary (early detection and referral) and tertiary (home programmes, inclusion).

Role of the physiotherapist in a school for physically handicapped children

Children with cerebral palsy and other locomotor disabilities need therapy, positioning and access to learn. The physiotherapist works as part of the school team with teachers, parents and the rehabilitation team.

1. Screening and assessment

Each child: GMFCS, ROM, strength, posture, gait, function; goals set with teachers and parents as part of the individual education plan.

2. Therapy

Individual and group sessions: stretching, strengthening, balance, gait training, play-based activities.

3. Positioning, seating and aids

Adapted chairs, tables, standing frames in class (standing frame 30–60 minutes a day for a child who cannot stand); orthoses such as ankle–foot orthoses, walkers and other mobility aids; wheelchair skills.

4. Training others

Teachers and attendants in safe lifting, transfers and positioning; parents in home programmes.

5. Accessible school

Ramps, rails, toilets, classroom layout; adapted physical education and sports.

6. Prevention of secondary problems

Contractures, scoliosis, obesity, pressure sores.

7. Transition and liaison

Plan the move to ordinary schools (inclusive education): advise on seating near the door and blackboard, a peer buddy, extra time and adapted physical education; later, vocational training. Liaise with doctors and the rehabilitation team; refer for orthopaedic review or surgery when needed.

The goal is not only therapy but full participation in learning and school life.
