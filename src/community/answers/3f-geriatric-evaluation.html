Geriatric assessment scales

Comprehensive geriatric assessment is a multidimensional, team-based assessment of an older person's medical, functional, psychological and social needs. Standard scales make it objective, show risk (falls, frailty), set goals and measure change.

Group the scales by domain (ADL, IADL, cognition, mood, balance and falls, frailty) and give one cut-off for each.

Principles of geriatric rehabilitation

Geriatric rehabilitation aims to restore and keep the older person's function and independence in the tasks that matter to them, despite multiple illnesses and reduced reserve.

Principles

  1. Comprehensive assessment: include cognition, mood, home and carers; treat the person, not the age.
  2. Function is the goal: independence in meaningful tasks, goals set with the person and family.
  3. Teamwork: doctor, nurse, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, social worker, family.
  4. Mobilise early; avoid unnecessary bed rest (loss of strength, postural hypotension, pressure sores, delirium).
  5. Start low, go slow, but progress: older muscle still adapts to overload; allow longer recovery.
  6. Prevent the next problem: falls, pressure sores, contractures, deconditioning, incontinence.
  7. Adapt the environment and use aids: walking aids, raised toilet seat, rails, lighting.
  8. Consider multiple illnesses and drugs: BP, sugar, heart rate response, pain, sensory loss.
  9. Simple instructions, repetition, written or pictorial home programmes; involve carers.
  10. Dignity and choice; motivation through meaningful goals.
  11. Continuity: link to community programmes, senior citizens' groups and follow-up.

Role of the physiotherapist

Health promotion (exercise groups, walking clubs, fall-prevention classes); screening; treatment of OA, back pain, stroke, Parkinson's, fractures, COPD, incontinence; home visits, aids and carer training; palliative care.

Remember the order: assess → set functional goals → treat early and progressively → prevent → adapt → follow up.

Functional assessment of the elderly

Functional assessment measures what an older person can do in daily life. It is the core of comprehensive geriatric assessment, because function predicts independence, care needs and outcome better than diagnosis alone.

Use of the findings

  • Set functional goals with the person and family.
  • Identify risk: falls (TUG 13.5 s or more), frailty, cognitive impairment (MMSE 23 or less).
  • Decide on aids, home changes, referral and level of care; re-test to measure progress.

ADL are basic self-care tasks; IADL are the more complex tasks needed to live alone.

Adverse effects of immobility in the elderly

Older people lose function quickly in bed and regain strength more slowly than the young.

  • Muscle and function: rapid loss of strength and walking ability (hospital-associated disability); contractures.
  • Heart and lungs: postural hypotension and falls on first standing; DVT; pneumonia.
  • Bone and skin: bone loss; pressure sores.
  • Bladder and bowel: incontinence, retention, constipation, faecal impaction.
  • Mind: delirium, depression, fear of falling.
  • Metabolic: insulin resistance, poor appetite, weight loss.

Prevent: sit out and walk early, exercise in bed, turning, toileting schedule.

Components of fitness assessment in the geriatric population

Fitness assessment in older people measures the physical capacities needed for independent daily life: strength, endurance, flexibility, balance and agility, and body composition. It gives a baseline, guides the exercise prescription and shows progress.

Screen first

BP, heart disease, recent falls, pain, drugs; stop if chest pain, dizziness or unusual breathlessness.

Senior Fitness Test (Rikli and Jones)

ComponentTest
Lower-body strength30-second chair stand: number of full stands, arms crossed
Upper-body strength30-second arm curl: 2 kg for women, 3.6 kg for men
Aerobic endurance6-minute walk, or 2-minute step in place
Lower-body flexibilityChair sit-and-reach
Upper-body flexibilityBack scratch
Agility and dynamic balance8-foot up-and-go
Body compositionHeight, weight, BMI (and waist)

Add

  • Grip strength with a dynamometer.
  • Static balance: single-leg stance.
  • Gait speed: below about 0.8 m/s suggests poor function and frailty.
  • Physical performance: SPPB (0–12); falls risk: TUG (13.5 s or more).

Use

  • Find the weak components and the person's risk of falls and frailty.
  • Set goals and prescribe aerobic, strength, flexibility and balance training to the weak components (WHO: balance and strength on 3 or more days a week).
  • Re-test to show change and motivate the person with results.

Precautions during testing

Longer warm-up; avoid breath-holding; supervise balance items; good footwear and a safe floor; use the Borg scale in people on beta-blockers.

The chair stand tests lower-body strength, not endurance: a common MCQ.
