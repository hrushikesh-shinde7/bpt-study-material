Define obesity; list its types

Obesity (WHO): abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that may impair health. Screened by BMI (kg/m²) and waist; confirmed by body fat where possible.

Methods of measuring body composition

Body composition divides body weight into fat mass (essential and storage fat) and fat-free mass (muscle, bone, organs, water). Body fat % = fat mass ÷ body weight × 100. It shows what BMI cannot: whether excess weight is fat or muscle.

Choosing a method

  • Community and clinic: BMI, waist, skinfolds, BIA (cheap, portable).
  • Laboratory: hydrostatic weighing, Bod Pod, DEXA, CT and MRI (accurate, costly).
  • Repeat with the same method and time of day to track change.

Obesity by body fat (2025 Indian definition): over 25.5% in men and over 38% in women. Weight loss should cut fat mass and keep fat-free mass.

Set-point theory; three causes of childhood obesity

Set-point theory

  • The brain (hypothalamus, using signals such as leptin from fat) defends a "set" body weight, like a thermostat.
  • When a person diets, hunger rises and metabolism falls, so weight returns towards the old level.
  • This explains regain after crash diets ("yo-yo" dieting): rapid loss is mostly water and muscle, and weight returns, often with more fat.
  • Application: slow weight loss (about 0.5 kg a week) with regular exercise and lasting habits is thought to help the body settle at a lower weight; target 5–10% of body weight over 3–6 months.

Childhood obesity

Measured by BMI-for-age on IAP 2015 charts (5–18 years): overweight from the adult-equivalent 23 line, obesity from the 27 line.

Three causes

  1. Diet: fast food, sugary drinks and snacks, large portions. Prevention: no junk food in canteens; fruit and home food in tiffin; water bottles.
  2. Inactivity and screen time: too little play and sport; short sleep. Prevention: at least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity (WHO); limit recreational screen time; regular sleep.
  3. Family and environment: parents with obesity and family eating habits; urban living without safe play space. Prevention: the whole family changes habits together; active travel to school.

Other causes: academic pressure and emotional eating; early-life factors (birth weight, mother's diabetes in pregnancy, formula feeding); rarely endocrine or genetic disease.

Obesity in childhood continues into adult life; praise effort, not weight.

Obesity and its effects on adult health

Obesity (WHO): abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that may impair health. In Asian Indians any BMI over 23 kg/m² counts as obesity (2025); abdominal obesity is a waist of ≥90 cm in men, ≥80 cm in women.

Causes: energy intake more than use (high-calorie food, sugary drinks); sedentary work and screen time; poor sleep and stress; genetics; pregnancy without return to activity; menopause; drugs (steroids); hypothyroidism, PCOS; ageing.

Role of physiotherapy

  • Assess BMI, waist, body fat and fitness; screen BP, sugar and joints.
  • Aerobic at least 5 days a week, 40–59% HRR, building to more than 250 minutes a week; resistance 2–3 days to keep fat-free mass; low impact for painful knees.
  • Lifestyle: dietitian-led 500 kcal a day deficit; steps, sleep, food diary; no crash diets.

Target 5–10% of body weight over 3–6 months; losing more than 5% within about 20 weeks improved disability in knee osteoarthritis.

Causes of childhood obesity

Childhood obesity is judged by BMI-for-age on the IAP 2015 charts (5–18 years): overweight from the line equal to an adult BMI of 23, obesity from the line equal to 27. Under 5 years, WHO standards are used.

Causes (with prevention)

  1. Unhealthy food: fast food, sugary drinks and snacks; large portions. → No junk food or sugary drinks in canteens; fruit and home food in tiffin; water bottles.
  2. Physical inactivity: too little play and sport. → At least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity, vigorous and muscle- and bone-strengthening on 3 days a week (WHO); daily PE periods.
  3. Screen time: TV, mobile and games replace play. → Limit recreational screen time.
  4. Short sleep. → Regular sleep routine.
  5. Family factors: parents with obesity, family eating habits. → Whole family changes habits together; family meals.
  6. Urban environment: no safe play space. → Active travel to school; school sport.
  7. Academic pressure and emotional eating. → Fun, non-competitive games; no blame or teasing.
  8. Early-life factors: low or high birth weight, mother's diabetes in pregnancy, formula feeding.
  9. Rarely endocrine or genetic disease (secondary obesity).

Why it matters

  • Early type 2 diabetes, high BP, fatty liver, sleep apnoea; knee pain, flat feet, knock knees, slipped capital femoral epiphysis; teasing, low self-esteem; obesity continues into adult life.

For a 5-cause question, pick diet, inactivity, screen time, sleep and family habits, and add one line of prevention for each.

Body mass index and its uses

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². Example: 68 kg, 1.60 m → 68 ÷ 2.56 = 26.6 kg/m².

  • WHO: normal 18.5–24.9; overweight 25–29.9; obese ≥30.
  • Asian Indian (2009): normal up to 22.9; overweight 23–24.9; obese ≥25 (2025: over 23 is obesity).
  • Uses: simple, cheap screening of individuals and populations; grading obesity; tracking change; deciding treatment.
  • Limits: does not separate fat from muscle (a muscular athlete scores high); says nothing about fat distribution; needs age charts in children.

Add waist circumference: a normal BMI with a large waist is still at risk.

Fat mass and fat-free mass

Body weight = fat mass + fat-free mass; body fat % = fat mass ÷ body weight × 100.

  • Fat mass: all body fat: essential fat (nerves, bone marrow, organs; more in women) and storage fat under the skin and around organs.
  • Fat-free mass: muscle, bone, organs and water (about three-quarters water); it sets the resting metabolic rate.
  • Measured by skinfolds, BIA, DEXA, underwater weighing.

Weight loss should reduce fat mass and keep fat-free mass: resistance training, enough protein, no crash diets.

Obesity is graded by BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)², with waist and body fat added.

Revised Indian definition (2025)

  • Any BMI over 23 is obesity; no separate "overweight" step.
  • Stage 1: extra fat without effects on organ function or daily activities.
  • Stage 2: BMI over 23 plus a large waist (≥90 cm men, ≥80 cm women) or waist-to-height over 0.5, plus limits in daily activities (knee pain, breathlessness) or an obesity-related disease (type 2 diabetes).
  • BMI grades: I 23–24.9; II 25–27.5; III 27.6–32.4; IV ≥32.5. Body fat over 25.5% (men), over 38% (women) is obesity.

Clinical significance

  • Risk of diabetes, hypertension, heart disease and death rises with each grade.
  • At the same BMI, Asian Indians carry more body and abdominal fat, so cut-offs are lower.
  • Grade guides treatment: lifestyle for all; drugs and closer medical care in higher grades or with disease; bariatric surgery for severe obesity with disease.
  • Waist adds what BMI misses: normal BMI with a large waist is still at risk.
  • Physiotherapy: higher grades need screening for disease first; low-impact exercise if joints hurt; target 5–10% loss over 3–6 months.

Always show the BMI working in a case, then grade it by both WHO and Indian cut-offs.

Anthropometric assessment in obesity

Anthropometry is the measurement of body size and proportions with simple tools (scale, stadiometer, tape, calipers). It is the cheap field method of judging obesity and fat distribution.

Measurements

  1. Weight in light clothes, no shoes, on a calibrated scale; height with a stadiometer.
  2. BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²; graded by WHO and Asian Indian cut-offs (obesity over 23 in Indians, 2025).
  3. Waist circumference: midway between the lowest rib and the top of the iliac crest, at the end of a normal breath out; abdominal obesity ≥90 cm men, ≥80 cm women.
  4. Hip circumference: widest part of the buttocks.
  5. Waist-to-hip ratio: risk at ≥0.90 men, ≥0.85 women (WHO). Waist-to-height ratio: risk over 0.5.
  6. Skinfolds with calipers at biceps, triceps, subscapular and suprailiac sites → body density and % fat.
  7. Mid-upper-arm and neck circumference (neck for sleep apnoea risk).
  8. Children: BMI-for-age on IAP charts (adult-equivalent 23 and 27 lines).

Standard technique

  • Same time of day, same side, mean of two readings; repeat monthly.

Interpretation

  • Apple (android) shape with a large waist carries more risk than pear (gynoid).
  • BMI cannot separate fat from muscle, so add waist and skinfolds.

Apple and pear outlines with the waist line (between lowest rib and iliac crest) and the hip line (widest part of buttocks).
