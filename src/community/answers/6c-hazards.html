Byssinosis

Byssinosis: occupational lung disease from inhaling cotton, flax and hemp dust in textile mills (carding and blowing rooms).

  • Feature: chest tightness and breathlessness on the first day back at work after a break ("Monday chest tightness"); later chronic airflow obstruction.
  • Schilling grades: ½ occasional symptoms on the first day of the week; 1 on every first day; 2 on the first and other days; 3 grade 2 with permanent incapacity.
  • Prevention: dust control, ventilation, respirators, periodic spirometry, job transfer.
  • Physiotherapy: breathing exercises, airway clearance, exercise training.

Workplace hazards and risk assessment tools

Workplace hazards

A hazard is anything at work that can cause harm; the risk is how likely the harm is and how severe it would be.

TypeExamples
PhysicalHeat, cold, poor lighting, noise, vibration, radiation, air pressure
ChemicalDusts (silica, coal, asbestos, cotton), gases, fumes, solvents, lead, mercury, pesticides
BiologicalTuberculosis and hepatitis B in health workers; anthrax, brucellosis, tetanus, leptospirosis
Mechanical and ergonomicMachinery accidents, falls, lifting, repetitive forceful work, awkward postures
PsychosocialJob stress, shift work, monotony, harassment

Risk assessment: steps

  1. Identify hazards: walk-through, job analysis, injury records, worker interviews.
  2. Decide who might be harmed and how.
  3. Evaluate the risk (likelihood × severity on a risk matrix) and decide controls.
  4. Record findings and act.
  5. Review regularly and after any change or injury.

Control

Hierarchy of controls, most to least effective: elimination → substitution → engineering → administrative → PPE.

PPE is the last line, not the first.

Asbestosis

Asbestosis: a pneumoconiosis; lung fibrosis from inhaling asbestos fibres.

  • Jobs: asbestos mining, cement sheets, insulation, brake linings, ship-breaking.
  • Features: lower-lobe fibrosis, pleural plaques and thickening, clubbing; restrictive lung disease.
  • Sequelae: lung cancer (more with smoking) and mesothelioma, decades later; cor pulmonale, respiratory failure.
  • Prevention: substitution (asbestos bans), wet methods, enclosure, exhaust ventilation, respirators; periodic chest X-ray and spirometry; notifiable under the Factories Act.
  • Physiotherapy: breathing control, pursed-lip breathing, thoracic mobility, graded exercise training, energy conservation, smoking cessation.

Three hazards of exposure to chemical agents

Chemical agents (dusts, gases, fumes, solvents, metals, pesticides) harm workers by three routes:

  1. Local action on skin and eyes: dermatitis, burns, ulcers, skin cancer.
  2. Inhalation of dusts, gases, fumes and vapours: pneumoconiosis (silicosis, asbestosis, byssinosis), asthma, poisoning by carbon monoxide, lead and solvents.
  3. Ingestion from contaminated hands, food and smoking at work: lead and mercury poisoning.

Prevention: substitute safer materials; wet methods, enclosure, exhaust ventilation; correctly fitted respirators; pre-placement and periodic examination.

Sequelae of pneumoconiosis

Pneumoconiosis: lung disease from inhaling mineral or organic dust, causing fibrosis (silicosis, coal workers' pneumoconiosis, asbestosis, byssinosis).

  • Progressive fibrosis and restrictive lung disease; progressive massive fibrosis.
  • Tuberculosis, especially with silica (silico-tuberculosis); chronic bronchitis and airflow obstruction.
  • Pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale; respiratory failure.
  • Lung cancer (asbestos, silica) and mesothelioma (asbestos); pneumothorax.
  • Disability, loss of work and income, early death.

Physiotherapy: breathing control, airway clearance, graded exercise training as pulmonary rehabilitation, energy conservation.

Contributing factors for industrial injury

Industrial injuries arise from sudden accidents (machinery, falls) or from cumulative microtrauma (repeated small strains). They rarely have one cause: worker, machine, environment, organisation and ergonomic factors act together. For example, a tired worker on a long night shift, hurrying to meet a production target on a wet floor beside an unguarded machine.

FactorExamples
WorkerFatigue, lack of training or experience, haste, alcohol, illness, poor vision or hearing, stress, not using protection
Machine and toolsUnguarded moving parts, faulty or poorly maintained equipment, unsuitable tools
EnvironmentPoor lighting, noise, heat, slippery or cluttered floors, poor ventilation, work at height
OrganisationLong hours, night shifts, production pressure, poor supervision, no safety rules or PPE, poor maintenance
ErgonomicHeavy lifting, awkward postures, repetition, badly designed workstations

Prevention

  • Risk assessment: identify hazards, decide who may be harmed, rate likelihood × severity, act, review after any change or injury.
  • Hierarchy of controls: elimination and substitution; engineering (guards on moving parts, hoists, good lighting and ventilation, clean non-slip floors); administrative (safety rules, supervision, maintenance, limits on hours and night shifts, rotation, breaks, training); PPE last.
  • Ergonomic evaluation of jobs (REBA, RULA, NIOSH) and redesign.
  • Physiotherapy: safe lifting and posture education, warm-up before heavy shifts, micro-breaks, fitness programmes, early reporting and early care of aches.

Injury records and worker interviews show where the factors cluster; correct the job before blaming the worker.

Cumulative trauma disorder; explain one common CTD

Cumulative trauma disorders (work-related musculoskeletal disorders): injuries of muscles, tendons, nerves and joints from repeated small strains without enough recovery.

Causes

  • Work: repetition, force, awkward or static posture, vibration, contact pressure, cold, long hours without breaks, piece-rate pressure.
  • Personal: age, female sex, pregnancy, diabetes, hypothyroidism, rheumatoid arthritis, obesity, previous injury, low fitness, smoking; stress.

Stages

StageFeatures
1Ache during work, settles overnight; work not affected; reversible
2Pain early in the shift, lasts overnight; sleep disturbed; work capacity reduced
3Pain at rest and with light tasks; cannot do even light duties; months to years

Examples: carpal tunnel syndrome, de Quervain's, trigger finger, lateral epicondylitis, rotator cuff tendinopathy, tension neck syndrome, hand-arm vibration syndrome.

Carpal tunnel syndrome

  • What: compression of the median nerve under the flexor retinaculum at the wrist.
  • Features: tingling and numbness of thumb, index, middle and half the ring finger, worse at night and with gripping; later weakness and thenar wasting (abductor pollicis brevis).
  • Tests: Phalen's (wrist flexion 60 seconds), Tinel's, carpal compression; nerve conduction studies confirm.
  • Treatment: night splint in neutral; nerve and tendon gliding; ergonomic changes and breaks; steroid injection; carpal tunnel release for thenar wasting, constant numbness or failed conservative care.

Prevention

Ergonomic redesign, breaks, job rotation, early reporting while still at stage 1.

Carpal tunnel in cross-section: flexor retinaculum, median nerve, flexor tendons.
