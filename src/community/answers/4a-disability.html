Define impairment, disability and handicap

Defined by the WHO in the ICIDH (International Classification of Impairments, Disabilities and Handicaps, 1980).

  • Impairment (organ level): any loss or abnormality of psychological, physiological or anatomical structure or function.
  • Disability (person level): any restriction or lack, resulting from an impairment, of ability to perform an activity in the manner or within the range considered normal for a human being.
  • Handicap (society level): a disadvantage for a given individual, resulting from an impairment or a disability, that limits or prevents the fulfilment of a role that is normal for that individual, depending on age, sex, and social and cultural factors.

Example: polio → paralysed leg (impairment) → cannot walk far (disability) → cannot farm or go to school (handicap).

The ICIDH was replaced by the ICF (2001), in which disability is the umbrella term for impairments, activity limitations and participation restrictions.

Full form of ICIDH (asked as "ICFDH")

ICIDH: International Classification of Impairments, Disabilities and Handicaps, published by the WHO in 1980.

  • Describes the consequences of disease as a one-way chain: disease → impairment → disability → handicap.
  • Impairment: loss or abnormality of structure or function (organ level).
  • Disability: restriction or lack of ability to perform an activity in the normal manner (person level).
  • Handicap: a disadvantage that limits or prevents a role normal for that individual (society level).
  • Replaced by the ICF (2001): body functions and structures, activities and participation, with environmental and personal factors, all interacting both ways.

Define disability and classify its types

Disability (ICIDH, WHO 1980): any restriction or lack, resulting from an impairment, of ability to perform an activity in the manner or within the range considered normal. In the ICF it is the umbrella term for impairments, activity limitations and participation restrictions.

21 specified disabilities (RPWD Act 2016), in groups:

  • Locomotor: locomotor disability, leprosy-cured, cerebral palsy, dwarfism, muscular dystrophy, acid attack victims.
  • Sensory: blindness, low vision, deaf and hard of hearing, speech and language disability.
  • Intellectual: intellectual disability, specific learning disabilities, autism spectrum disorder; mental illness.
  • Chronic neurological: multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease; blood disorders: haemophilia, thalassaemia, sickle cell disease; multiple disabilities including deaf-blindness.

Other ways: congenital or acquired; temporary or permanent; visible or invisible; mild, moderate, severe, profound. Benchmark disability: 40% or more.

Levels of disability prevention

The WHO report on disability prevention and rehabilitation (1981) describes three levels of prevention based on the ICIDH chain: disease → impairment (organ level) → disability (person level) → handicap (society level). Each level breaks one link of the chain.

1. Primary prevention (first level)

Aim: prevent the impairment from occurring.

  • Immunisation: polio, measles, rubella.
  • Nutrition: iodine, vitamin A, folic acid, iron.
  • Antenatal care and safe delivery; avoiding consanguineous marriage; genetic counselling.
  • Road and workplace safety: helmets, seat belts.
  • Control of leprosy and diabetes; healthy lifestyle and exercise; health education.

2. Secondary prevention (second level)

Aim: stop an impairment becoming a disability by early detection and treatment.

  • Newborn and developmental screening (RBSK); hearing and vision screening; screening for diabetic foot.
  • Early treatment of infections and injuries.
  • Early physiotherapy, positioning and splinting.
  • Early surgery (clubfoot, cataract).

3. Tertiary prevention (third level)

Aim: stop a disability becoming a handicap.

  • Rehabilitation: medical, vocational, social; aids and appliances.
  • Prevention of secondary conditions.
  • Inclusive education; accessible buildings and transport; employment.
  • Legal rights and schemes; community awareness; CBR.

Types of disability; evaluation of permanent physical impairment of the upper extremity

Types of disability

  • RPWD Act 2016: 21 specified disabilities: locomotor (locomotor disability, leprosy-cured, cerebral palsy, dwarfism, muscular dystrophy, acid attack victims); sensory (blindness, low vision, deaf and hard of hearing, speech and language); intellectual (intellectual disability, specific learning disabilities, autism); mental illness; chronic neurological (multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease); blood disorders (haemophilia, thalassaemia, sickle cell disease); multiple disabilities including deaf-blindness.
  • Other ways: congenital or acquired; temporary or permanent; visible or invisible; mild, moderate, severe, profound; primary or secondary.
  • Benchmark disability: 40% or more, certified by a medical authority.

Evaluation of permanent impairment of the upper extremity

Done when the condition has reached maximum improvement from treatment; based on measured function, not opinion. The upper limb has an arm component and a hand component, each worth 90%.

Combining formula: a + b(90 − a) ÷ 90 (a higher, b lower value). Arm values are combined by the formula; hand values are added; then arm and hand are combined by the formula. Example: arm 27% and hand 64% → 64 + 27 × 26 ÷ 90 = 71.8%.

Extra weightage up to 10% for persistent pain, infection, deformity, contracture, dominant limb (4%) or shortening (2% for each inch beyond the first); the total never exceeds 100%.

A result of 40% or more is a benchmark disability, needed for most reservations and benefits.
