Define rehabilitation

Rehabilitation (WHO): "a set of interventions designed to optimize functioning and reduce disability in individuals with health conditions in interaction with their environment".

Older WHO wording (still quoted in Indian textbooks): all measures that reduce the impact of disabling and handicapping conditions and enable disabled people to achieve social integration.

  • Aim: the highest possible independence in daily life, school, work and community, with the least restriction.
  • Kinds: medical, vocational, social, educational, psychological.
  • By place: institution-based (IBR); outreach; community-based (CBR).

Differences between institution-based (IBR) and community-based rehabilitation (CBR)

IBR: rehabilitation in hospitals, rehabilitation centres and special schools with specialist staff and equipment; the person comes to the centre.

CBR (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004): "a strategy within general community development for the rehabilitation, equalization of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities", carried out by people with disabilities, their families and communities with government and non-government services.

They are not rivals: CBR handles most needs locally and refers complex cases to institutions; institutions train CBR workers and give specialist back-up; outreach programmes link the two.

IBR, outreach and CBR as three circles from the institution to the village.

Role of the occupational therapist in the rehabilitation team

The occupational therapist helps the person do the daily activities of life, school and work as independently as possible.

  • Assesses and trains ADL: feeding, dressing, bathing, toileting, cooking, using money and transport.
  • Hand function: fine motor training, hand splints, sensory re-education.
  • Adaptive devices (built-up spoons, button hooks, reachers, sock aids) and home, school and workplace modification.
  • Cognitive and perceptual retraining (memory, attention, neglect) after brain injury and stroke.
  • Pre-vocational and work skills; wheelchair seating; energy conservation; play and sensory integration in children.

Role of the medical social worker in rehabilitation

The medical social worker deals with the social and economic side of disability.

  • Psychosocial assessment: family, income, housing, education, work, community support.
  • Counselling the person and family to accept the disability and take part in rehabilitation.
  • Linking to benefits: disability certificate and UDID card, pensions, travel concessions, free aids (ADIP), scholarships, loans for self-employment.
  • Arranging financial help through hospital funds, government schemes and NGOs.
  • Discharge planning, return to school or work, follow-up; advocacy against discrimination and abuse.

Role of the medical social worker in the rehabilitation team

Rehabilitation is a team effort around the person and family. The medical social worker is the member who handles the social, family and economic effects of disability and links the person to resources outside the hospital.

1. Psychosocial assessment

Family structure, income, housing, education, work and community support; identifies problems that block rehabilitation.

2. Counselling

Helps the person and family accept the disability and take part in rehabilitation; supports the family through the change in roles.

3. Linking to benefits

Disability certificate and UDID card; pensions; travel concessions; free aids and appliances (ADIP); scholarships; loans for self-employment.

4. Financial help

Through hospital funds, government schemes and NGOs.

5. Discharge planning and return to the community

Home and community resources; return to school or work; follow-up after discharge.

6. Advocacy

Against discrimination and abuse; for the person's rights.

7. In community-based rehabilitation

Community survey to identify persons with disability; awareness meetings; forming self-help groups and CBR committees; linking families to schemes, livelihood and education; mobilising local resources.

8. Teamwork

Shares findings in team meetings so the team sets common goals (interdisciplinary model) and avoids conflicting advice.

The social worker turns medical recovery into social integration, which is the final aim of rehabilitation.

Role of the social worker in CBR

In CBR the social worker works in the village with the person, family and community.

  • Psychosocial assessment of the family: income, housing, education, work, support.
  • Community survey to identify persons with disability.
  • Awareness meetings to reduce stigma.
  • Forming self-help groups and CBR committees.
  • Linking families to schemes (certificate, UDID, pensions, ADIP aids), livelihood and education.
  • Mobilising local resources; counselling the family; advocacy against discrimination.

Vocational rehabilitation

Purpose (ILO Convention 159, 1983): "to enable a disabled person to secure, retain and advance in suitable employment and thereby to further such person's integration or reintegration into society."

  1. Vocational evaluation: physical capacity (functional capacity evaluation), education, skills, interests, aptitude, work history, local job market.
  2. Guidance and counselling: a realistic goal matching ability and interest.
  3. Training: industrial training institutes, Vocational Rehabilitation Centres for Handicapped, NGOs; work conditioning and hardening.
  4. Placement: old job with changes, open employment (4% reservation, RPWD Act 2016), sheltered workshop, supported or self-employment (NDFDC loans).
  5. Follow-up to keep the job.

Significance of vocational guidance and counselling in rehabilitation

Vocational rehabilitation (ILO Convention 159, 1983) aims "to enable a disabled person to secure, retain and advance in suitable employment" and so to integrate into society. Vocational guidance and counselling helps the person choose a realistic work goal that matches ability and interest.

Steps of vocational rehabilitation

  1. Evaluation: physical capacity (functional capacity evaluation), education, skills, interests, aptitude, work history, local job market.
  2. Guidance and counselling.
  3. Training: industrial training institutes, Vocational Rehabilitation Centres for Handicapped, NGOs; work conditioning and work hardening.
  4. Placement: old job with changes, open employment, sheltered workshop, supported employment, self-employment.
  5. Follow-up to solve problems at work and keep the job.

Significance

  • Matches the job to remaining abilities, so training is not wasted and failure is avoided.
  • Builds motivation, self-esteem and acceptance of disability; reduces depression.
  • Leads to earning and economic independence; less dependence on family and state.
  • Gives a social role and dignity, the final aim of rehabilitation (participation).
  • Guides the family and employer; identifies adaptations, aids and job modifications.
  • Informs about schemes: 4% reservation in government posts (RPWD Act 2016), training centres, concessional loans (NDFDC).

Physiotherapist's part

Functional capacity evaluation of physical demands; work conditioning and work hardening; advice on workplace adaptations.

Work is the step that turns rehabilitation into full participation in society.

Training for activities of daily living, with examples

Activities of daily living (ADL) are the everyday tasks a person needs for self-care and independence.

  • Basic ADL: feeding, grooming, bathing, dressing, toileting, transfers, walking or wheelchair mobility.
  • Instrumental ADL: cooking, shopping, housework, money, phone, transport.

Steps of ADL training

  1. Assess with the Barthel Index or FIM.
  2. Set goals with the person.
  3. Analyse the task and break it into steps.
  4. Teach step by step (forward or backward chaining), in the real setting and at the usual time.
  5. Use the easiest method first and fade help.
  6. Add aids and change the environment.
  7. Train the family; review progress.

  • Nurse: skin, bladder and bowel care; teaching the family.

Management of the disabled hand

The hand is the main tool for self-care and work and a sense organ. Causes: nerve injury, leprosy, burns, crush injury, stroke, cerebral palsy, rheumatoid arthritis, amputation. Plan: restore, protect, adapt.

Assessment

  • Deformity (claw hand, wrist drop), wasting, scars, oedema (volumeter).
  • Range by goniometry; grip dynamometer (sitting, elbow 90°, mean of 3 trials) and pinch gauge; muscle test of little finger abduction, thumb abduction, wrist extension.
  • Sensation: monofilaments, two-point discrimination.
  • Function: Nine-Hole Peg Test, Jebsen–Taylor test, DASH, real tasks.

Functional grips (Napier)

Power: cylindrical (glass), spherical (ball), hook (bag). Precision: tip pinch (pin), three-jaw (pen), lateral key pinch (key). Train each with graded objects: large to small, light to heavy.

Treatment

  • Oedema and pain: elevation, retrograde massage; mobilise, stretch, strengthen; dexterity on pegboards.
  • Splints (static or dynamic; bamboo or tubing in villages): cock-up for radial palsy; knuckle-bender for mobile claw hand; thumb web spacer for median palsy; safe position for burns (wrist 20–30° extension, knuckles 70–90° flexion, fingers straight).
  • Sensory re-education: localisation, then textures and objects, eyes closed then checked with eyes open.
  • Anaesthetic hand (NLEP self-care): inspect daily, soak about half an hour, scrape hard skin, oil while wet, protect from heat and friction.

Adaptive devices and ADL

Built-up padded handles, universal cuff, lever taps, button hook, Velcro, one-handed methods; practise eating, cooking, washing and dressing at home.

Work and vocational aspects

Job analysis, graded work simulation, modified or insulated tools, vocational guidance or job change; tendon transfer for a mobile claw hand at an NLEP centre; certify the hand component at maximum improvement.

Restore, protect, then adapt: if the hand cannot be restored, change the tool or the task.

What is a non-government organisation (NGO)? Give examples

NGO: a voluntary, non-profit organisation run independently of the government, registered as a society or trust.

Examples:

  • ADAPT (formerly the Spastics Society of India): cerebral palsy.
  • Indian Red Cross Society.
  • HelpAge India: older people.
  • Bhagwan Mahaveer Viklang Sahayata Samiti (Jaipur foot).
  • Amar Seva Sangam, Mobility India, CBM.

NGOs give services where government services do not reach, train CBR workers, and advocate for rights.

Role of NGOs in community welfare, CBR and the rehabilitation team

NGO: a voluntary, non-profit organisation independent of the government (for example ADAPT, Indian Red Cross, BMVSS).

  • Surveys and early identification; awareness to reduce stigma.
  • Services where government does not reach: therapy, camps, aids and appliances, special and inclusive schools.
  • Training CBR workers, parents and volunteers.
  • Vocational training, self-help groups, livelihood.
  • Advocacy for rights and accessibility; linking people to government schemes.
  • Fund-raising, research, disaster relief; piloting programmes that government later adopts.

Role of NGOs in the rehabilitation team

An NGO is a voluntary, non-profit organisation run independently of the government, registered as a society or trust. NGOs work alongside government services and are important members of the wider rehabilitation team, especially in CBR.

Roles

  1. Identification and awareness: surveys and early identification of persons with disability; awareness to reduce stigma.
  2. Direct services where government does not reach: therapy, camps, aids and appliances, special and inclusive schools.
  3. Training: CBR workers, parents and volunteers.
  4. Livelihood: vocational training, self-help groups.
  5. Advocacy: rights, accessibility and policy; linking people to government schemes.
  6. Resources: raising funds; research; disaster relief.
  7. Innovation: flexible and close to the community, so they often pilot programmes that government later adopts.
  8. Partnership with government: the Deendayal Disabled Rehabilitation Scheme gives grants to NGOs to run rehabilitation services.

Spastics Society of India and its functions

Started on 2 October 1972 in Mumbai by Dr Mithu Alur to provide education and treatment for children with cerebral palsy; now called ADAPT (Able Disabled All People Together).

Functions:

  • Special and inclusive education; early intervention.
  • Therapy: physiotherapy, occupational and speech therapy.
  • Training courses for teachers and therapists.
  • Vocational training and placement of young adults.
  • Advocacy for inclusive education; outreach in slums and villages; research and publications.

Functions of the Red Cross Society

Founded on Henry Dunant's idea after the battle of Solferino (International Committee, Geneva, 1863). The Indian Red Cross Society was set up by an Act of 1920; the President of India is its president.

Functions:

  • Disaster preparedness and relief.
  • Blood donation and blood banks; first aid training; ambulance and hospital services.
  • Health education; maternal and child welfare; care of the sick and disabled.
  • Help for war victims; tracing missing family members.
  • Junior and Youth Red Cross in schools and colleges.

Seven principles: humanity, impartiality, neutrality, independence, voluntary service, unity, universality.
