Ergonomics for low back ache in a housemaid

A housemaid's low back ache builds up from many small strains (cumulative microtrauma) rather than one injury: hours of stooping, floor-level work and lifting with little rest.

Task analysis: what strains the back

TaskStress on the back
Sweeping and moppingSustained stooping with short-handled brooms
Floor-level washingLong squatting or stooping; static posture
Carrying bucketsHeavy load held far from the body, lifting from the floor, bending and twisting
PersonalLow fitness, obesity, previous injury

Ergonomic advice

  • Long-handled brooms and mops so she works upright.
  • Sit on a low stool instead of squatting for long at floor-level washing; keep the work close.
  • Carry half-filled buckets close to the body; make two trips rather than one heavy one.
  • Avoid bending and twisting together; turn with the feet.
  • Raise work towards elbow height where possible (washing on a raised surface).
  • Change posture often; short breaks; alternate stooped and upright tasks.

Physiotherapy

  • Assess: pain, posture, back mobility, core endurance, hamstring flexibility; ask her to show each chore.
  • Back and core strengthening; strengthen quadriceps and hips; stretch calves and hamstrings.
  • Pain relief in the acute phase (heat, TENS) only as a bridge to exercise.
  • Regular physical activity and weight control; a home programme.

Change the task first; training in lifting technique alone does not prevent back pain (Verbeek et al., 2011).

Job stress and psychological hazards at work: causes, prevention and management

Psychological (psychosocial) hazards are features of work that cause strain: job stress, shift work, monotony, harassment. Job stress arises when demands are high and control, reward or support are low.

Causes (aetiology)

  • Demand–control model: high demands with little control over the work.
  • Effort–reward imbalance: great effort with little reward.
  • Monotony and job dissatisfaction.
  • Anxiety about finishing work on time and to quality; tight deadlines.
  • Long hours and shift work.
  • Poor relationships, harassment, poor support, job insecurity.

Effects

Muscle tension and pain, headache, fatigue, poor sleep, raised BP, anxiety, depression, more errors and accidents, absence. Psychosocial factors raise muscle tension and the chance that pain persists. In long-route drivers: irritability, anxiety, poor sleep and loneliness.

Prevention at work

  • Reasonable workload and deadlines; more control and variety.
  • Breaks; support from supervisors; clear roles; safe workplaces.
  • Shift planning: forward-rotating shifts, no long runs of night shifts.

Management

  • Relaxation: Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation; Mitchell's physiological relaxation; diaphragmatic breathing.
  • Exercise: regular aerobic exercise, stretching programmes at work, yoga.
  • Ergonomic and task changes that remove physical strain; education on posture, sleep and pacing.
  • Referral to a counsellor or doctor for anxiety or depression.
  • Evaluate with pain scores, absenteeism and stress questionnaires.

Example: a long-route driver with stress from traffic and deadlines needs planned rest, relaxation and breathing, fitness and family time.

Ergonomic advice to an air hostess

Main stresses: prolonged standing, pushing trolleys, overhead lifting of bags, shift work.

  • Push trolleys with both hands, close to the body.
  • Avoid lifting bags overhead alone; get help.
  • Supportive cushioned shoes; change position and walk when possible.
  • Calf raises and ankle pumps during the flight; compression stockings if the legs swell.
  • Sleep planning for shifts: protected sleep in a dark quiet room, regular meals.

Back, core and calf strengthening and general fitness off duty.

Ergonomic care for physiotherapists

Physiotherapists face the same work-related musculoskeletal disorders they treat: low back, neck, wrist and hand pain from manual therapy, patient handling and bending over low plinths.

Risk factors

TaskRisk
Manual therapyRepeated force through the wrists and hands; static posture
Patient handling and transfersHeavy, awkward loads, often above the NIOSH limit of 23 kg; bending and twisting
Low plinthsSustained stooping; neck flexion
Long, busy daysToo little rest, fatigue, stress

Ergonomic measures

  • Height-adjustable plinths: work near elbow height for light work, lower for heavy work.
  • Hoists and slide sheets for transfers; team lifts instead of lifting alone.
  • Body mechanics: keep the patient close, neutral spine, avoid bending and twisting together, step and turn with the feet.
  • Rotate tasks (manual work with exercise supervision); micro-breaks every 30–60 minutes.
  • Use mechanical aids and good grips to reduce force through the hands.
  • Own fitness and strengthening: back, core, neck and grip; workstation exercises.
  • Early reporting of aches (stage 1 of a cumulative trauma disorder is reversible).
  • Stress management: relaxation, breathing, sleep.
  • Hands: grip strengthening; nerve and tendon gliding if the hands tingle.
  • Desk and documentation work: screen at an arm's length with the top at or below eye level; keyboard at elbow height.

Self-check: score your own transfer and treatment postures with REBA, and use the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire to find early symptoms in the team.

Follow the hierarchy of controls: equipment and layout first, technique and fitness next.

Role of the physiotherapist in stress management in industry

Work stress arises when job demands are high and control or reward is low (demand–control and effort–reward models), with monotony, deadlines, long hours, shift work, poor relationships, harassment and job insecurity. It causes muscle tension and pain, headache, fatigue, poor sleep, raised BP, anxiety, depression, errors, accidents and absence.

Role of the physiotherapist

  1. Screening and education: identify workers with stress-related pain and fatigue; teach posture, sleep and pacing.
  2. Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation: tense, then release, each muscle group in turn.
  3. Mitchell's physiological relaxation: move into the opposite of the stress posture.
  4. Diaphragmatic breathing practised daily and during stressful moments.
  5. Exercise: regular aerobic exercise, stretching programmes at work, yoga; employee fitness programmes with group classes and walking groups.
  6. Ergonomic and task changes that remove physical strain; workstation exercises and micro-breaks.
  7. Shift-work advice: protected sleep, regular meals, exercise.
  8. Referral to a counsellor or doctor for anxiety or depression.

At the organisation level

Advise management on reasonable workload and deadlines, more control and variety, breaks, supervisor support, clear roles and forward-rotating shifts; build stress management into workplace health promotion.

Health promotion

Walking or yoga, sleep routine, weight and posture awareness.

Evaluation

Pain, absenteeism and stress questionnaires before and after the programme.

Stress raises muscle tension and the chance that pain persists, so relaxation and exercise belong in every industrial back and neck programme.

Musculoskeletal hazards of prolonged standing

Many workers stand for most of the shift: watchmen, traffic police, surgeons, teachers, air hostesses. Standing still is static work: the same muscles stay contracted, and the legs get little movement to pump blood back to the heart.

Hazards and their causes

Effects reported consistently (Waters and Dick, 2015):

HazardWhy it happens
Low back painStatic loading of the back muscles and joints; no change of posture
Leg pain and swellingLittle calf-muscle pumping, so fluid collects in the legs
Varicose veinsPooling of blood in the leg veins over years
Fatigue and muscle painSustained contraction of back and leg muscles without rest
Foot pain, plantar heel painHard floors and poor footwear

Prevention and physiotherapy

  • Change position often and walk when possible: the most effective measure.
  • Sit–stand options or a leaning stool; rest breaks.
  • Anti-fatigue mats.
  • Supportive, cushioned footwear and insoles.
  • A footrest to alternate the feet and shift weight.
  • Compression stockings for those with swelling.
  • Calf raises and ankle pumps during the shift.
  • Back and leg strengthening and general fitness outside work.

Job examples: a teacher alternates sitting and standing; a surgeon uses an anti-fatigue mat and supportive shoes; an air hostess does calf exercises and wears supportive shoes.

Viva: movement beats every other measure; mats, sit–stand options, footwear and stockings also help.

Effects of shift work on health

Shift work, especially at night, disturbs the body clock (circadian rhythm).

  • Poor sleep and fatigue; reduced vigilance in monotonous night work; more errors and accidents.
  • Digestive problems, weight gain, metabolic and heart risk.
  • Night shift work is classed as "probably carcinogenic" (IARC, 2019).
  • Strain on family and social life; stress.

Advice: forward-rotating shifts; protected sleep in a dark, quiet room; regular meals; exercise; avoid long runs of night shifts.

Factors causing low back pain in industry and its prevention

Industrial low back pain usually builds up from cumulative microtrauma rather than one injury.

Factors

GroupFactors
Manual handlingHeavy, frequent or awkward lifting (bending and twisting); lifting far from the body or from the floor
PostureProlonged sitting or standing; stooping and squatting work
VibrationWhole-body vibration (drivers)
AccidentsSlips and falls
PersonalLow fitness, smoking, obesity
PsychosocialJob dissatisfaction and stress

Squatting and stooping work (automobile workers, farm labour) adds back strain and knee and ankle pain.

Revised NIOSH lifting equation: 23 kg is the recommended limit under ideal conditions, reduced for distance from the body, height, travel, twisting, frequency and poor grip. A 50 kg bag lifted to 3 feet is well beyond it, so the task itself must change.

Prevention

  • Redesign first: lighter bags, trolleys, hoists, conveyors, loads at waist height, team lifts. For a labourer loading 50 kg bags: 25 kg bags or two-person lifts, a trolley or ramp, loading from waist height.
  • Squatting work: low stools or kneeling pads; rotate tasks.
  • Ergonomic evaluation and task analysis (NIOSH, REBA).
  • Administrative: job rotation, rest breaks.
  • Vibration: suspended, well-maintained seats; limit driving hours.
  • Posture: change position often; sit–stand options.
  • Fitness: core and hip strengthening, hamstring flexibility, aerobic fitness, weight control, smoking cessation.
  • Early reporting and early physiotherapy: education and reassurance, graded activity.

Training in lifting technique alone did not prevent back pain (Verbeek et al., 2011): change the task, then train.

Mechanical stresses faced by truck drivers

  • Prolonged sitting in a fixed posture: low back and neck pain, stiffness, poor leg circulation.
  • Whole-body vibration through the seat: low back pain and disc degeneration.
  • Poor seat and reach: twisting, reaching, neck strain on mirrors and turning.
  • Loading, unloading, climbing in and out of the cab: back and knee strain, falls.
  • Long hours and night driving: fatigue, sleep loss, obesity, accidents.

Advice: break every 2 hours to walk and stretch; suspended seat with lumbar support; mirrors set to avoid neck twisting; use handholds and steps; planned sleep.

Ergonomic evaluation and modifications for a surgeon

Surgeons stand for long operations with the neck bent over the field and the arms held still, so neck, shoulder and back pain and leg fatigue are common. Ergonomic evaluation finds these risk factors and the modifications remove them, following the hierarchy of controls: change the equipment and layout first, then breaks and fitness.

Ergonomic evaluation

  1. Symptom survey: Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (neck, shoulders, back, legs).
  2. Observe and video operations; task analysis of posture, duration and breaks.
  3. Measure: table height against elbow height, monitor position, loupe and headlight angle, floor and footwear.
  4. Score posture with RULA (neck, trunk, upper limbs) or REBA.
  5. Recommend changes and re-evaluate after them.

Risk factors found

  • Prolonged standing: low back pain, leg swelling, varicose veins, foot pain.
  • Sustained neck flexion (loupes, looking down).
  • Static shoulder and arm postures; long cases without breaks.
  • Table too high or too low; monitors placed to the side, so the neck twists.

Modifications

  • Table height so the hands work near elbow height with elbows about 90° and shoulders relaxed; lower for laparoscopic surgery.
  • Monitors in front with the top at eye level.
  • Loupes and headlights set for a neutral neck.
  • Anti-fatigue mat and supportive shoes; footrest to alternate feet; compression stockings if legs swell.
  • Short stretching micro-breaks every 30–60 minutes during long operations (American College of Surgeons).
  • Fitness and back and neck strengthening outside theatre.

A lower RULA or REBA score after the changes shows they worked.
