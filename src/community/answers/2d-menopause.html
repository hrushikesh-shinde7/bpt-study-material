Importance of physiotherapy in postmenopausal women

Menopause is the permanent end of menstruation from loss of ovarian follicle activity, diagnosed after 12 months without a period; Indian women reach it at about 46 years. Postmenopause is the years after it.

Changes that make physiotherapy necessary

  • Oestrogen and progesterone fall; inhibin falls, so FSH rises markedly and LH rises.
  • Rapid bone loss in the first years (osteoporosis, fractures of wrist, spine, hip); loss of muscle mass and strength.
  • Weaker pelvic floor and tissues (stress incontinence, urgency, prolapse); worse cholesterol, abdominal fat, higher BP and heart risk; hot flushes, poor sleep, mood swings.

Precautions

  • Screen for osteoporosis and fracture: red flags are sudden severe back pain, height loss, night pain; refer for DEXA and X-ray.
  • Spine-sparing movement: avoid repeated or loaded forward bending and twisting (sit-ups, toe touches); teach the hip hinge and log-roll out of bed. Impact work only when fracture is excluded and joints allow.

Aerobic exercise alone is not enough for bone: the programme must include resistance and balance training (Too Fit to Fracture, 2014).

Prevention of osteoporosis with ageing

Osteoporosis (WHO, 1994): a systemic skeletal disease with low bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration of bone, so bones are fragile and fracture easily.

  • Build peak bone mass in childhood and youth: sport, jumping, running, enough calcium and protein.
  • Exercise throughout life: weight-bearing and impact activity, progressive resistance training, balance training.
  • Nutrition: calcium from milk, curd, ragi, sesame, green leafy vegetables; vitamin D from sunlight (supplements if deficient); enough protein.
  • Lifestyle: stop smoking; limit alcohol and caffeine; keep a healthy weight.
  • Prevent falls: balance training, home safety, vision and drug review.
  • Screen and treat: DEXA for women at risk; medicines (bisphosphonates) when fracture risk is high.

Risk (predisposing) factors for osteoporosis

Osteoporosis: low bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration of bone with increased fragility and fracture risk (WHO).

GroupFactors
Cannot changeFemale sex, older age, early menopause, family history of hip fracture, small thin build, previous fragility fracture, Asian or white ethnicity
Can changePhysical inactivity, low body weight, low calcium and vitamin D (little sunlight), smoking, alcohol, excess caffeine and salt
Diseases and drugsLong-term steroids, rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid or parathyroid excess, diabetes, malabsorption, kidney disease, immobility

Physiotherapy targets the changeable factors: resistance, impact and balance exercise, sunlight and diet advice.

Types of osteoporosis

Osteoporosis: low bone mass with microarchitectural deterioration; diagnosed by DEXA T-score −2.5 or below.

TypeFeatures
Primary type I (postmenopausal)Women about 50–70; oestrogen loss; mainly trabecular bone; vertebral crush and distal radius (Colles') fractures

Physiological variations associated with menopause

Menopause: the permanent end of menstruation from loss of ovarian follicle activity, diagnosed after 12 months without a period. Average age in Indian studies is about 46 years (about 51 in Western women).

Stages

  • Perimenopause: years of irregular cycles and symptoms before, and the first year after, the last period.
  • Postmenopause: the years after. Premature menopause: before 40. Surgical menopause: after removal of both ovaries (sudden symptoms).

Hormonal changes

Oestrogen (oestradiol) and progesterone fall; inhibin falls, so FSH rises markedly and LH rises; androgens become relatively higher.

Medical treatment (hormone therapy, vaginal oestrogen, bone-protecting drugs) is the doctor's decision; exercise and education are the physiotherapist's part.
