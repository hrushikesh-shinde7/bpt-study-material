Complications of caesarean section

Caesarean section is delivery of the baby through an abdominal and uterine incision, done as a planned (elective) operation, for example for cephalopelvic disproportion, or as an emergency.

  • Early: bleeding; infection of the wound or uterus; deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism; chest infection and atelectasis after anaesthesia; urinary retention or bladder injury; paralytic ileus and wind pain; spinal headache.
  • Late: wound breakdown; incisional hernia; adhesions and chronic pain; delayed mobility and breastfeeding.
  • Later pregnancies: scar rupture and abnormal placenta.
  • Baby: fast breathing for a short time after birth.

Physiotherapy prevents many of these: deep breathing, supported huffing with a pillow over the wound, ankle pumps and early walking.

Physiotherapy management of pain during labour

Labour pain comes with contractions that open the cervix in the first stage and with stretching of the pelvic floor and perineum in the second. Pain and anxiety cause fast breathing and over-breathing and raise cardiac output and BP.

Aims: reduce fear and pain; help her stay in control and mobile; help labour progress; save energy; keep the baby well oxygenated.

Methods

  1. Antenatal education: what happens in labour; positions, breathing and relaxation practised beforehand; partner's role. Less fear means less pain.
  2. Positions and mobility: walking, leaning forward on the partner or bed, birthing ball, kneeling forward, all-fours (eases backache when the baby faces forward), side-lying for rest.
  3. Breathing: slow, relaxed breathing in early labour; lighter, slightly faster at the peak; "pant-pant-blow" in transition; avoid over-breathing.
  4. Relaxation: Mitchell's physiological relaxation or progressive relaxation; music.
  5. Massage and warmth: firm sacral pressure or massage during contractions; warm packs on the back or lower abdomen.
  6. TENS: two pairs of electrodes on the back, one either side of the spine at T10–L1 and one at S2–S4; low background current with a boost button during contractions; start early; not in water.
  7. Warm water: shower or bath in the first stage.
  8. Continuous support by a partner or companion: reduces fear and the need for drugs.
  9. With medical analgesia (epidural, Entonox, opioids given by doctors): adjust positions to side-lying or supported sitting.

Evidence: WHO (2018) recommends relaxation and massage for women who want them. TENS changes pain scores little, but most women would use it again and no harms were reported.

Role of breathing exercises in labour

In labour oxygen use rises, and pain causes fast breathing and over-breathing (tingling, dizziness). Breathing patterns are taught in antenatal classes with relaxation, so the woman can use them in labour.

Why breathing helps

  • Focuses attention away from pain and keeps her in control.
  • Aids relaxation and saves energy.
  • Keeps the oxygen supply to mother and baby.
  • Prevents exhausting breath-holding and stops her pushing too early.

Breathing by stage

StageBreathing
Early first stageSlow, relaxed breathing: a sigh out at the start, slow breaths in through the nose and out through the mouth, a sigh at the end

Precautions

  • Avoid over-breathing: if she feels tingling or dizziness, slow down and breathe into cupped hands.
  • Practise before labour with the partner, who coaches during contractions.

Combine breathing with upright positions, relaxation and sacral massage for the best effect.

Physiotherapy during labour

Labour is the process by which contractions open the cervix and the baby is born, followed by the placenta. Most of the physiotherapy is taught in antenatal classes (what happens, positions, breathing, relaxation, partner's role) and used on the day.

Aims: reduce fear and pain; keep her in control and mobile; help labour progress; save energy; protect the perineum; keep the baby well oxygenated.

First stage (cervix opens to 10 cm; active phase from 5 cm)

  • Upright, mobile positions: walking, leaning forward, birthing ball, kneeling forward, all-fours; side-lying for rest or with an epidural. They shortened the first stage by about 1 hour 22 minutes.
  • Breathing: slow and relaxed, lighter at the peak; avoid over-breathing (cupped hands if tingling).
  • Relaxation (Mitchell's), sacral massage, warm packs, warm shower.
  • TENS: electrodes at T10–L1 and S2–S4, boost button during contractions; not in water.

Transition

Urge to push before full dilatation: "pant-pant-blow".

Second stage (full dilatation to birth)

  • Position of her choice (kneeling, supported squatting, all-fours, side-lying); avoid lying flat on the back.
  • Push with the natural urge, breathing out or short breath-holds; pant as the head crowns so the perineum stretches slowly.

Fourth stage and puerperium

Deep breathing, ankle pumps, gentle pelvic floor contractions, feeding posture.

Continuous support by a partner or companion reduces fear and the need for drugs; with an epidural, adjust positions to side-lying or supported sitting.

Positions in labour

Upright and mobile positions help labour progress, ease pain and reduce fear.

  • First stage: walking; standing and leaning forward on the partner or bed; sitting on a birthing ball; kneeling forward over a pillow or ball; all-fours (eases backache when the baby faces forward); side-lying for rest or with an epidural.
  • Upright and mobile positions shortened the first stage by about 1 hour 22 minutes and reduced caesarean and epidural use (Lawrence et al., 2013).
  • Second stage: the position she chooses: kneeling, squatting with support, all-fours, side-lying.
  • Avoid lying flat on the back: it presses on the great vessels and narrows the pelvic outlet (WHO, 2018).

Ergonomic advice for a lactating mother

Feeding takes hours a day, so a slouched posture loads the neck and back.

  • Bring the baby to the breast, not the breast to the baby: a pillow on the lap.
  • Chair with back support (cushion behind the low back), feet flat or on a low stool, shoulders relaxed, arm supported.
  • Avoid long neck bending; stretch neck and upper back after feeds.
  • Change holds: cradle, cross-cradle, football hold (after caesarean), side-lying (night, sore perineum).
  • Keep wrists straight; support the head with the forearm.
  • Change nappies at waist height; lift the baby close with knees bent; do not carry on one hip for long.

Postpartum blues

Postpartum (baby) blues: short-lived tearfulness, mood swings, irritability, anxiety and poor sleep in about half or more of new mothers, starting 2–3 days after birth and settling within 2 weeks.

  • Cause: sudden fall in hormones, sleep loss and adjustment to the baby.
  • Management: reassurance that it is common; rest and sleep; family help with the baby; talking about feelings; gentle walks and relaxation.
  • Refer: symptoms beyond 2 weeks or severe suggest postnatal depression: screen with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale. Confusion or hallucinations mean postpartum psychosis, an emergency.

Breast engorgement and its management

Breast engorgement: painful, swollen, hard breasts, usually 2–5 days after birth as milk comes in, from milk stasis plus swelling of the breast tissue.

  • Frequent, effective feeding on demand (8–12 times a day) with a correct latch.
  • Hand-express a little to soften the areola before latching.
  • Cold packs between feeds for swelling.
  • Gentle lymphatic drainage strokes towards the armpit; avoid deep or forceful massage.
  • Supportive, non-tight bra; pain relief as prescribed.
  • Refer if fever or a red, hot area appears (mastitis).

Three goals of postnatal care

Puerperium: the 6 weeks after delivery while the body returns towards its non-pregnant state.

  1. Prevent complications: chest problems after anaesthesia, deep vein thrombosis, urinary retention, constipation (deep breathing, ankle pumps, early walking).
  2. Restore the pelvic floor and abdominal wall: pelvic floor contractions from the first day; transversus drawing-in; check for diastasis recti.
  3. Teach posture and body mechanics for feeding, lifting and carrying the baby, and relieve pain of the perineum, back, wound and breasts.

Also: support emotional well-being and rest; gradual return to fitness and work with a lifelong pelvic floor habit.
