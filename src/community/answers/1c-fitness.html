Field tests used in athlete training

Field tests measure fitness components outside the laboratory, in the athlete's own setting. They are cheap, quick and sport-specific, can test many athletes at once, and are used to track training and decide return to play.

Rules for good testing

  • Standard warm-up before every session.
  • Same surface, time of day and order of tests each time: least tiring first, endurance last.
  • Trained tester and clear instructions.
  • Record conditions of each test.
  • Repeat at set intervals to show change with training.

Uses

  • Find strengths and weaknesses; set training loads; compare with the athlete's earlier scores; guide return to play after injury.

Pick tests that match the sport; the beep test and Yo-Yo test can test a whole team at once.

General principles of exercise and its phases

Exercise is planned, structured, repeated activity done to improve or keep physical fitness. It works only when it follows the principles of training.

Principles of training

  1. Overload: the body adapts only when stressed beyond its usual level.
  2. Progression: increase the load gradually: duration first, then frequency, then intensity; about 10% a week.
  3. Specificity: the body adapts to the exact type of training (aerobic training builds endurance, not strength).
  4. Reversibility: gains are lost when training stops ("use it or lose it").
  5. Individuality: prescribe for the person's age, fitness, disease and goals.
  6. Recovery: adaptation happens during rest; too little rest → overtraining.

Dose is written as FITT-VP: frequency, intensity, time, type, volume, progression.

Phases of a session

PhaseWhat it involves
Warm-up (5–10 min)Light aerobic activity, dynamic movements; raises heart rate, muscle temperature and joint mobility; reduces injury and heart strain
Conditioning (20–60 min)Main aerobic, resistance or sport work at the prescribed intensity
Cool-down (5–10 min)Light activity so HR and BP fall gradually; prevents blood pooling and dizziness
Stretching (≥10 min)Static stretches when muscles are warm

Stages of a training programme

  • Initial (first few weeks): low intensity, building the habit.
  • Improvement (next few months): steady progression.
  • Maintenance: keeping the gains long term.

Phases as a heart-rate curve against time: rise (warm-up), plateau (conditioning), fall (cool-down).

Deconditioning effects of bed rest

Deconditioning is the loss of function in many body systems caused by prolonged bed rest and inactivity, added to the effects of the illness itself.

  • Avoid unnecessary bed rest; sit out and walk early.
  • Breathing exercises and positioning for the chest.

Deconditioning effects of bed rest

Prolonged bed rest causes deconditioning, a loss of function in many systems:

  • Heart: higher resting HR, smaller stroke volume, lower VO₂max, postural hypotension, DVT.
  • Lungs: basal collapse, retained secretions, pneumonia.
  • Muscles: antigravity wasting, about 1–1.5% strength loss a day.
  • Bones and joints: bone loss, high urine calcium, contractures.
  • Skin: pressure sores; gut and bladder: constipation, urinary stasis and stones; mind: confusion, low mood, poor balance.

Prevent with early sitting and walking, breathing exercises, ankle pumps, turning and early weight-bearing.

Energy systems

ATP is the only direct source of energy for muscle contraction, but it is stored for only seconds. Three systems rebuild it. All three work all the time; the length and intensity of the effort decide which one leads.

1. ATP–PC (phosphagen) system

  • Creatine phosphate rebuilds ATP without oxygen.
  • Maximal efforts up to about 10 s: 100 m sprint, jump.

2. Anaerobic glycolysis

  • Glucose → lactate without enough oxygen.
  • Leads from about 10 s to 2 min: 400 m run.

3. Aerobic (oxidative) system

  • Full oxidation of carbohydrate and fat in the mitochondria → carbon dioxide and water.
  • Leads beyond about 2 min and at rest: walking, marathon.

Training implications

  • Interval training for the anaerobic systems (speed, power, lactate tolerance).
  • Continuous training for the aerobic system (more capillaries, mitochondria, higher VO₂max).

Precautions when prescribing fitness exercise for a diabetic patient

  • Hypoglycaemia: check glucose before and after if on insulin or sulphonylureas; carry fast sugar; do not inject insulin into the exercising limb; lows can come 6–15 hours later, risk up to 48 hours.
  • High sugar: no exercise with high glucose and ketones.
  • Feet: daily inspection, good footwear; cycling or swimming if there is an ulcer.
  • Retinopathy (severe): avoid vigorous and heavy resistance work, jarring, head-down positions and breath-holding.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: use RPE, not HR; avoid quick position changes and heat; hydrate.

Exercise at a regular time, often 1–2 hours after a meal, with a partner who knows the signs of hypoglycaemia.

Hazards of a sedentary lifestyle

A sedentary lifestyle means too little physical activity and long periods of sitting. People who are not active enough have a 20–30% higher risk of death than those who are sufficiently active (WHO).

Heart and metabolism

  • Coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension.
  • Type 2 diabetes, obesity, high cholesterol, metabolic syndrome.

Cancer

  • Colon, breast and endometrial cancer.

Musculoskeletal

  • Low back and neck pain; stiffness.
  • Osteoporosis; weak muscles (sarcopenia); falls in later life.

Mind

  • Depression, anxiety, poor sleep, lower cognitive function.

Sedentary desk work

  • Neck and shoulder pain with a forward head and rounded shoulders; low back pain from slumped sitting; weight gain and low fitness.

Fitness

  • Loss of cardiorespiratory endurance, strength and flexibility (reversibility of training: "use it or lose it").

Prevention (WHO, 2020)

  • 150–300 minutes a week of moderate activity.
  • Strength work twice a week.
  • Break up long sitting with movement: desk workers take a short movement break every 30–60 minutes.

Role of the physiotherapist

  • Fitness screening and FITT-VP prescription; workplace and school activity programmes; ergonomic advice; community awareness.

Link to levels of prevention: physical activity is primordial and primary prevention of most non-communicable diseases.

Principles of aerobic training

  • Overload: work above the usual level (moderate 40–59% HRR or vigorous 60–89%).
  • Progression: increase duration, then frequency, then intensity, about 10% a week.
  • Specificity: rhythmic large-muscle activity builds endurance, not strength.
  • Reversibility: gains are lost when training stops.
  • Individuality and recovery: dose for age, fitness and disease; rest days avoid overtraining.

Dose as FITT-VP: 5 or more days moderate, 30–60 minutes (150 a week); walking, cycling, swimming; with warm-up and cool-down.

Target HR (Karvonen) = resting HR + (maximum HR − resting HR) × intensity.

What is aerobic exercise?

Aerobic exercise is rhythmic, continuous exercise of large muscle groups in which energy comes mainly from oxygen-using pathways.

  • Intensity and length: low to moderate, minutes to hours.
  • Fuel: fat and glucose → carbon dioxide and water; type I (slow-twitch) fibres.
  • Examples: brisk walking, jogging, cycling, swimming, dancing.
  • Adaptations: more capillaries, mitochondria and enzymes; bigger stroke volume; higher VO₂max; lower resting HR and BP.

Dose: moderate 40–59% HRR, 30–60 minutes, 5 or more days a week (150 minutes).

Definitions

  • Aerobic exercise: rhythmic, continuous exercise of large muscle groups in which energy comes mainly from oxygen-using pathways.
  • Anaerobic exercise: short, intense exercise in which energy comes mainly from ATP–PC and glycolysis without enough oxygen.

MET (metabolic equivalent)

  • The energy used sitting quietly at rest: about 3.5 ml O₂/kg/min (about 1 kcal/kg/hour).
  • Activity is expressed as multiples of rest: light <3 METs; moderate 3–5.9; vigorous ≥6.
  • Examples: brisk walking about 4 METs; running about 8–10.
  • Uses: prescribing and grading intensity; weekly volume as MET-minutes (healthy adults about 500–1000 a week); comparing activities.

Most daily activity mixes both: all energy systems work together, and the length and intensity of effort decide which leads.

Define aerobic exercise; explain circuit training

Aerobic exercise

Rhythmic, continuous exercise of large muscle groups in which energy comes mainly from oxygen-using pathways; low to moderate intensity for minutes to hours (brisk walking, cycling, swimming). It raises VO₂max and lowers resting HR and BP.

Circuit training: definition

A series of exercises (stations) done one after another with little rest, working different muscle groups and adding aerobic stations; the whole set is repeated.

Method

  • 8–12 stations; 30–60 s or 10–15 repetitions at each, light to moderate load.
  • Short rest (15–30 s) while moving to the next station.
  • 2–3 rounds; warm-up before and cool-down after.
  • Alternate upper body, lower body and trunk so each group rests while another works.

Types

  • Resistance circuit; aerobic circuit (aerobic bouts between resistance stations); sport-specific circuit.

Benefits

  • Muscular endurance and strength with some aerobic fitness in one short session.
  • Suits groups: schools, workplaces, cardiac rehabilitation phase III, older adults with chair stations; varied and enjoyable.

Precautions

  • Correct technique first; avoid breath-holding; adapt stations for joint problems; monitor HR or RPE in patients.

Effects of exercise in a person with hypertension

Exercise lowers blood pressure in hypertension, both immediately after a session and long-term.

Acute effect

  • After one session of aerobic exercise BP stays lower for up to 22 hours: post-exercise hypotension (Pescatello et al., 2004).

Chronic effect

  • Regular aerobic exercise lowers resting BP, most in people with hypertension (about 5–7 mmHg).
  • Dynamic resistance and isometric training also help.

Mechanisms

  • Lower sympathetic activity; lower peripheral resistance.
  • Better endothelial function and arterial stiffness.
  • Weight loss; better insulin sensitivity.

Other gains

  • Fewer cardiovascular events; better fitness and mood; less need for drugs.

Prescription

  • Moderate aerobic exercise 30 minutes or more on most days: walking, cycling, swimming; intensity by RPE or talk test.
  • Resistance training 2–3 days a week at moderate load.

Precautions

  • Do not start if resting BP is very high (about 200/110 or more) until controlled.
  • Avoid breath-holding (Valsalva) and heavy isometric straining.
  • Long warm-up and cool-down: vasodilators and alpha-blockers cause post-exercise dizziness.
  • Use RPE with beta-blockers; watch dehydration and low potassium with diuretics.

Post-exercise hypotension is why a long cool-down matters, especially with vasodilators and alpha-blockers.

Principles of exercise prescription

Exercise prescription is a planned, individual dose of exercise (FITT-VP) written to improve or maintain health and fitness safely and to meet the person's goals.

Principles of training

  • Overload; progression (duration → frequency → intensity, about 10% a week); specificity; reversibility; individuality; recovery.

Steps and safety

  1. Screen: history, risk factors, medicines, red flags; medical clearance when needed.
  2. Test fitness and set goals with the person.
  3. Write the FITT-VP for aerobic, resistance, flexibility and balance work.
  4. Teach warm-up, cool-down, self-monitoring (talk test, RPE, pulse) and warning signs.
  5. Review and retest every 4–6 weeks; adjust for adherence and enjoyment.

Adapt for disease: glucose checks in diabetes, no Valsalva and long cool-down in hypertension.
