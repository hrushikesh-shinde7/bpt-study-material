Field tests used in athlete training

A field test is a test done away from the laboratory that needs neither extensive training nor expensive equipment (NSCA). A good test is valid (measures what it claims) and reliable (repeatable). Uses: pre-season screening, setting training loads, tracking progress and deciding return to play.

Tests by component

Test (component)How it is done; what it shows
20 m shuttle run, beep test (aerobic)Run between lines 20 m apart to beeps; starts at 8.5 km/h, +0.5 km/h each minute; stop when pace is lost (line missed twice). Last stage predicts VO₂max
Yo-Yo intermittent recovery (repeated intense running)2 × 20 m shuttles at rising speed, 10 s jog between; level 1 starts at 10 km/h; score = total distance. Suits football and team sports
Cooper 12-minute run (aerobic)Distance covered in 12 min; VO₂max = 22.351 × km − 11.288; correlation 0.897 with treadmill VO₂max
10–40 m sprint (speed)Timing gates; 0–10 m split = acceleration, later splits = maximal speed
RAST (anaerobic power)6 × 35 m sprints, 10 s rest; power = mass × distance² ÷ time³; fatigue index
Vertical and broad jump (power)Jump reach minus standing reach, best of 3; broad jump measured to the heels
T-test, Illinois, 505 (change of direction)T-test: 10 yards forward, shuffle 5 left, 10 right, 5 back, run backwards; reliability 0.98. Illinois (16–19 years) excellent: under 15.2 s boys, 17.0 s girls
1RM, handgrip (strength)Rising single lifts, maximum within 3–5 attempts; hand dynamometer
Push-ups, curl-ups (endurance)Maximum without rest; curl-ups to a metronome
Sit-and-reach (flexibility)Moderately valid for hamstrings, poor for the low back

Order of testing (NSCA)

Non-fatiguing (height, weight, flexibility, skinfolds, vertical jump) → agility → maximal strength and power → sprints → muscular endurance → anaerobic capacity → aerobic capacity last or on another day.

Keep the warm-up, surface, footwear, time of day and order the same; full rest between maximal attempts; retest at set points.

Advantages and limits

  • Advantages: cheap, quick, many athletes at once, sport-specific and realistic.
  • Limits: less precise than laboratory tests; weather, surface, motivation and pacing affect scores; most give predicted, not measured, values; hand-timing error.

T-test and Illinois test change-of-direction speed; true agility adds a reaction to a signal.

General principles of exercise and its phases

Exercise is planned, structured, repeated activity done to improve or keep physical fitness. It works only when it follows the principles of training.

Principles of training

  1. Overload: the body adapts only when stressed beyond its usual level.
  2. Progression: increase the load gradually: duration first, then frequency, then intensity; about 10% a week.
  3. Specificity: the body adapts to the exact type of training (aerobic training builds endurance, not strength).
  4. Reversibility: gains are lost when training stops ("use it or lose it").
  5. Individuality: prescribe for the person's age, fitness, disease and goals.
  6. Recovery: adaptation happens during rest; too little rest → overtraining.

Dose is written as FITT-VP: frequency, intensity, time, type, volume, progression.

Phases of a session

PhaseWhat it involves
Warm-up (5–10 min)Light aerobic activity, dynamic movements; raises heart rate, muscle temperature and joint mobility; reduces injury and heart strain
Conditioning (20–60 min)Main aerobic, resistance or sport work at the prescribed intensity
Cool-down (5–10 min)Light activity so HR and BP fall gradually; prevents blood pooling and dizziness
Stretching (≥10 min)Static stretches when muscles are warm

Stages of a training programme

  • Initial (first few weeks): low intensity, building the habit.
  • Improvement (next few months): steady progression.
  • Maintenance: keeping the gains long term.

Phases as a heart-rate curve against time: rise (warm-up), plateau (conditioning), fall (cool-down).

Deconditioning is the loss of function in many body systems caused by prolonged bed rest and inactivity, added to the effects of the illness itself.

Deconditioning effects of bed rest

Prolonged bed rest causes deconditioning, a loss of function in many systems:

  • Heart: higher resting HR, smaller stroke volume, lower VO₂max, postural hypotension, DVT.
  • Lungs: basal collapse, retained secretions, pneumonia.
  • Muscles: antigravity wasting, about 1–1.5% strength loss a day.
  • Bones and joints: bone loss, high urine calcium, contractures.
  • Skin: pressure sores; gut and bladder: constipation, urinary stasis and stones; mind: confusion, low mood, poor balance.

Prevent with early sitting and walking, breathing exercises, ankle pumps, turning and early weight-bearing.

Energy systems

ATP is the only direct fuel for contraction, but stored ATP lasts only a few seconds of maximal work. Three systems rebuild it. All three work all the time; the length and intensity of the effort decide which one leads.

SystemHow ATP is madeRate and use
Phosphagen (ATP–PC)Creatine phosphate + ADP → ATP (creatine kinase); no oxygenFastest, smallest store; all-out efforts of seconds: sprint start, jump, lift
Fast glycolysisGlucose or glycogen → pyruvate → lactate; net 2 ATP (3 from glycogen)Fast, medium store; about 30 s to 2 min: 400 m; acid adds to fatigue
OxidativeCarbohydrate and fat burnt in mitochondria (Krebs cycle, electron transport) → CO₂ + water; about 30–32 ATP per glucoseSlowest, largest store; rest and efforts over 3 min: walking, marathon

Duration and main system (NSCA)

0–6 s phosphagen; 6–30 s phosphagen and fast glycolysis; 30 s–2 min fast glycolysis; 2–3 min fast glycolysis and oxidative; over 3 min oxidative.

Key terms

  • Rate against capacity: the faster a system makes ATP, the less it can make in total.
  • Respiratory exchange ratio: about 0.7 on fat, 1.0 on carbohydrate.
  • Lactate threshold: about 50–60% VO₂max untrained, 70–80% trained.
  • Oxygen deficit at the start of exercise; EPOC afterwards rebuilds creatine phosphate, clears lactate and returns temperature and heart rate to rest.

Training implications (NSCA work : rest)

  • Phosphagen: 5–10 s near-maximal bouts, 1:12 to 1:20.
  • Fast glycolysis: 15–30 s bouts, 1:3 to 1:5.
  • Oxidative: bouts over 3 min at lower intensity, 1:1 to 1:3, or continuous training (more capillaries and mitochondria, higher VO₂max).

Precautions when prescribing fitness exercise for a diabetic patient

  • Hypoglycaemia: check glucose before and after if on insulin or sulphonylureas; carry fast sugar; do not inject insulin into the exercising limb; lows can come 6–15 hours later, risk up to 48 hours.
  • High sugar: no exercise with high glucose and ketones.
  • Feet: daily inspection, good footwear; cycling or swimming if there is an ulcer.
  • Retinopathy (severe): avoid vigorous and heavy resistance work, jarring, head-down positions and breath-holding.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: use RPE, not HR; avoid quick position changes and heat; hydrate.

Exercise at a regular time, often 1–2 hours after a meal, with a partner who knows the signs of hypoglycaemia.

Hazards of a sedentary lifestyle

A sedentary lifestyle joins two separate risks. Sedentary behaviour is any waking behaviour at 1.5 METs or less while sitting, reclining or lying. Physical inactivity is not meeting the physical activity recommendations (Tremblay et al., 2017). A person can meet the guidelines and still sit all day.

Size of the problem

  • World: 31% of adults insufficiently active (2022); India about 49% (women 57%, men 42%).
  • ICMR-INDIAB: 54.4% inactive; towns 65% against villages 50%.
  • Insufficient activity: 20–30% higher risk of death (WHO); 9% of premature deaths worldwide (Lee et al., 2012).

High sitting raises all-cause death (hazard ratio 1.24; Biswas et al., 2015). Risk climbs above 6–8 h a day of sitting or 3–4 h of television. About 60–75 min a day of moderate activity offsets long sitting (Ekelund et al., 2016).

Physiotherapy prescription

  • 150–300 min a week of moderate activity plus strengthening on 2 days (WHO, 2020); sit less and replace sitting with any activity, even light.
  • Break up sitting: a 2-minute walk every 20 minutes cut the glucose rise after a drink by 24–30% (Dunstan et al., 2012).
  • Desk workers: build to 2 h, then 4 h a day of standing and light walking at work; sit-stand desk; avoid static standing (Buckley et al., 2015).
  • Habits: stand for calls, take the stairs, walking meetings, active commuting; step counter and reminders; workplace and school programmes; ergonomic advice.

Physical activity is primordial and primary prevention of most non-communicable diseases.

Principles of aerobic training

  • Overload: work above the usual level (moderate 40–59% HRR or vigorous 60–89%).
  • Progression: increase duration, then frequency, then intensity, about 10% a week.
  • Specificity: rhythmic large-muscle activity builds endurance, not strength.
  • Reversibility: gains are lost when training stops.
  • Individuality and recovery: dose for age, fitness and disease; rest days avoid overtraining.

Dose as FITT-VP: 5 or more days moderate, 30–60 minutes (150 a week); walking, cycling, swimming; with warm-up and cool-down.

Target HR (Karvonen) = resting HR + (maximum HR − resting HR) × intensity.

What is aerobic exercise?

Aerobic exercise is rhythmic, continuous exercise of large muscle groups in which energy comes mainly from oxygen-using pathways.

  • Intensity and length: low to moderate, minutes to hours.
  • Fuel: fat and glucose → carbon dioxide and water; type I (slow-twitch) fibres.
  • Examples: brisk walking, jogging, cycling, swimming, dancing.
  • Adaptations: more capillaries, mitochondria and enzymes; bigger stroke volume; higher VO₂max; lower resting HR and BP.

Dose: moderate 40–59% HRR, 30–60 minutes, 5 or more days a week (150 minutes).

Definitions

  • Anaerobic exercise: short, intense exercise in which energy comes mainly from ATP–PC and glycolysis without enough oxygen.

MET (metabolic equivalent)

  • The energy used sitting quietly at rest: 3.5 ml O₂/kg/min (about 1 kcal/kg/hour).
  • Bands: sedentary 1.0–1.5; light 1.6–2.9; moderate 3.0–5.9; vigorous 6.0 or more.
  • Values (Compendium, 2024): yoga 2.3; slow walking 3.0; brisk walking 4.8; cycling 6.8; running at about 10 km/h 9.3.
  • Energy: kcal/min = METs × 3.5 × kg ÷ 200; a 60 kg person walking briskly uses about 5 kcal a minute.
  • Volume: MET-minutes = METs × minutes; ACSM advises 500–1000 a week.
  • Uses: grading and prescribing intensity, weekly volume, comparing activities, weight control, choosing activities in cardiac rehabilitation.
  • Limit: measured rest averages about 2.6 ml/kg/min, so METs are estimates; check with HR or RPE.

Most daily activity mixes both: all energy systems work together, and the length and intensity of effort decide which leads.

Define aerobic exercise; explain circuit training

Aerobic exercise

Rhythmic, continuous exercise of large muscle groups in which energy comes mainly from oxygen-using pathways; low to moderate intensity for minutes to hours (brisk walking, cycling, swimming). It raises VO₂max and lowers resting HR and BP.

Circuit training: definition

A series of exercises (stations) done one after another with little rest, working different muscle groups and adding aerobic stations; the whole set is repeated. Developed by Morgan and Adamson at Leeds in 1953.

Method

  • About 10–15 stations, alternating upper body, lower body, trunk and aerobic work.
  • 30–40 s or 12–15 repetitions at each, at 40–60% 1RM for a weight circuit.
  • 15–30 s rest while moving on; 1–3 circuits, about 30 minutes; warm-up and cool-down.
  • Progress by adding a round, more work time, less rest or more load.

Types

  • Circuit weight training: light weights, many repetitions.
  • Aerobic circuit: aerobic bouts between stations, or heart rate kept at 70–80% of maximum.
  • High-intensity circuit: 12 body-weight exercises, 30 s each, 10 s change.
  • Circuit class therapy for patients; sport-specific circuits.

Effects

  • Muscular endurance and strength; VO₂max up about 5% (men) and 8% (women) in early studies, and 11% when heart rate was kept at 70–80% of maximum.
  • After stroke, circuit class therapy improved six-minute walk distance by about 61 m (Cochrane, 2017).

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: several fitness components in one short session; suits groups (schools, workplaces, cardiac rehabilitation phase III, older adults with chair stations); little equipment; enjoyable.
  • Disadvantages: smaller strength gains than heavy training; needs space and supervision; technique slips with fatigue.

Precautions: technique first; avoid breath-holding; adapt stations for joint problems; monitor HR or RPE in patients.

Effects of exercise in a person with hypertension

Hypertension affects 35.5% of Indian adults (ICMR-INDIAB, 2023). Exercise lowers blood pressure both after a single session and with training, and is a first-line lifestyle treatment.

Acute effects

  • During aerobic exercise systolic BP rises with workload while diastolic stays level or falls slightly; heavy isometric holds and breath-holding raise both sharply.
  • After one session BP stays lower for up to 22 hours: post-exercise hypotension (Pescatello et al., 2004).

Chronic effects

  • Resting BP falls most in hypertension: about 5–8 mmHg (2–4 in prehypertension, 1–2 in normal BP).
  • Network meta-analysis (Edwards et al., 2023): aerobic 4.5/2.5, dynamic resistance 4.6/3.0, combined 6.0/2.5, interval training 4.1/2.5, isometric 8.2/4.0 mmHg.
  • Fewer cardiovascular events; better fitness and mood; less need for drugs.

Mechanisms

  • Lower sympathetic activity: plasma noradrenaline down 29%, renin activity down 20%.
  • Lower vascular resistance (7%); better endothelial function; less arterial stiffness with aerobic training.
  • Weight loss; better insulin sensitivity.

Prescription (ACSM)

  • Aerobic: 5–7 days a week, 40–59% heart rate reserve (RPE 12–13), 30 min or more a day; walking, cycling, swimming.
  • Resistance: 2–3 days a week, 60–70% 1RM (40–50% in older people and beginners), 2–4 sets of 8–12.
  • Flexibility 2–3 days a week.

Precautions

  • Do not start if resting BP is above 200/110; stop a test above 250/115.
  • Avoid breath-holding (Valsalva) and maximal straining.
  • Long warm-up and cool-down: vasodilators and alpha-blockers cause dizziness after exercise.
  • Use RPE with beta-blockers; watch dehydration and low potassium with diuretics.

Classic answers banned weights; current evidence shows resistance and isometric training also lower BP, provided there is no breath-holding.

Principles of exercise prescription

Exercise prescription is a planned, individual dose of exercise (FITT-VP) written to improve or maintain health and fitness safely and to meet the person's goals.

Principles of training

  • Overload; progression (duration → frequency → intensity, about 10% a week); specificity; reversibility; individuality; recovery.

Steps and safety

  1. Screen: history, risk factors, medicines, red flags; medical clearance when needed.
  2. Test fitness and set goals with the person.
  3. Write the FITT-VP for aerobic, resistance, flexibility and balance work.
  4. Teach warm-up, cool-down, self-monitoring (talk test, RPE, pulse) and warning signs.
  5. Review and retest every 4–6 weeks; adjust for adherence and enjoyment.

Adapt for disease: glucose checks in diabetes, no Valsalva and long cool-down in hypertension.
