Define ageing; mention types of ageing; enumerate genetic theories of ageing

Definition

Ageing is the progressive, time-related decline in the structure and function of the body that reduces the ability to adapt to stress and raises the risk of disease and death. It is universal, progressive, intrinsic (from within) and harmful.

Types of ageing

TypeMeaning
ChronologicalAge in years since birth

Primary and secondaryPrimary: changes due to age itself. Secondary: changes added by disease, inactivity, lifestyle

Genetic (programmed) theories

  1. Genetic clock: lifespan is set by genes that switch on and off in sequence; each species has a typical maximum lifespan; progeria shows early ageing from one gene.
  2. Hayflick limit (cellular senescence): normal cells divide a limited number of times, then stop; human fibroblasts stop after about 40–60 divisions (Hayflick and Moorhead, 1961).
  3. Telomere theory: telomeres shorten at each division; when too short, the cell stops dividing; telomerase restores them in germ and cancer cells.
  4. Endocrine (neuroendocrine): a clock in the hypothalamus and pituitary drives ageing through hormones (fall in growth hormone, sex hormones, melatonin).
  5. Immunological: programmed decline of immunity (thymus shrinks), so infections, cancer and autoimmunity rise.

Physiotherapy aims at successful ageing (Rowe and Kahn): low disease and disability, high physical and cognitive function, active engagement with life. Much decline is secondary and can be changed.

Theories of ageing: enumerate and explain any three

Ageing is the progressive, time-related decline in body structure and function that reduces the ability to adapt to stress; it is universal, progressive, intrinsic and harmful.

Classification

  • Programmed (genetic): genetic clock, Hayflick limit, telomere, endocrine, immunological.
  • Damage or error (stochastic): free radical, wear and tear, cross-linking, DNA damage, error catastrophe, rate of living.
  • Psychosocial: disengagement, activity, continuity, Erikson's eighth stage.

1. Telomere theory and Hayflick limit (programmed)

Normal cells divide a limited number of times and stop: human fibroblasts after about 40–60 divisions (Hayflick, 1961). Telomeres, the protective ends of chromosomes, shorten at each division; when too short, the cell stops dividing. Senescent cells build up in old tissues.

2. Free radical theory (damage; Harman, 1956)

Reactive oxygen species made during normal metabolism damage DNA, proteins and membranes; damaged mitochondria make more radicals. Evidence: oxidative damage and lipofuscin rise with age. PT: regular moderate exercise increases antioxidant defences.

3. Activity theory (psychosocial; Havighurst, 1961)

Staying physically and socially active and replacing lost roles leads to successful ageing and life satisfaction. Basis of exercise groups, day centres and volunteering. The opposite view, disengagement theory, is criticised because withdrawal harms health.

Meaning for physiotherapy

No single theory explains ageing; much decline is secondary (inactivity, disease), which exercise can change: stretching counters cross-linking stiffness, strengthening and weight control reduce joint wear, and group exercise supports activity and continuity.

Theories of ageing: enumerate and describe any one

Ageing: the progressive, time-related decline in body structure and function that reduces adaptation to stress and raises the risk of disease and death.

Theories

  • Programmed: genetic clock; Hayflick limit; telomere; endocrine; immunological.
  • Damage: free radical; wear and tear; cross-linking; DNA damage; error catastrophe; rate of living.
  • Psychosocial: disengagement; activity; continuity; Erikson's eighth stage.

Free radical theory (Harman, 1956)

  • Free radical: a molecule with an unpaired electron, so highly reactive. Main reactive oxygen species: superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical.
  • Source: normal metabolism: electrons leak from the respiratory chain in the mitochondria (mitochondrial theory, Harman 1972).
  • Damage: lipid peroxidation of membranes; oxidised, non-working proteins; DNA mutations.
  • Vicious circle: damaged mitochondria leak more radicals.
  • Defences: superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase; vitamins C and E; repair. Ageing follows when damage outruns defence.
  • Evidence for: oxidative damage rises with age; lipofuscin (age pigment) accumulates.
  • Evidence against: antioxidant pills do not prolong life (beta-carotene, vitamin A and E slightly raised mortality in 68 trials); naked mole-rats live over 28 years despite high oxidative damage.

Meaning for physiotherapy

Regular moderate exercise briefly raises radicals but trains the body to make more of its own antioxidant enzymes (hormesis); high-dose antioxidant pills can blunt training gains. Advise exercise, fruit and vegetables and no smoking. Much decline in old age is secondary (inactivity, disease), which physiotherapy can change.

Enumerate (three) theories of ageing

Theories fall into three groups: programmed (genetic clock, Hayflick limit, telomere, endocrine, immunological), damage (free radical, wear and tear, cross-linking, DNA damage, error catastrophe, rate of living) and psychosocial (disengagement, activity, continuity, Erikson).

  1. Hayflick limit / telomere (programmed): cells divide about 40–60 times and stop as telomeres shorten.
  2. Free radical (damage; Harman, 1956): reactive oxygen species damage DNA, proteins and membranes.
  3. Activity (psychosocial; Havighurst): staying active and involved brings successful ageing.
