Muscles of the pelvic floor

The pelvic floor is a muscular sling from the pubis to the coccyx, pierced by the urethra, vagina and anal canal.

LayerMuscles
Pelvic diaphragm (deep)Levator ani: pubococcygeus (with puborectalis), iliococcygeus; and coccygeus
Deep perineal pouchDeep transverse perineal, external urethral sphincter
Superficial perineal pouchBulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus, superficial transverse perineal
OthersExternal anal sphincter; the perineal body is their central tendon

Nerve: pudendal (S2–S4) and direct S3–S4 branches to levator ani. Mostly slow-twitch fibres for support, with fast-twitch fibres for coughs and lifts.

Functions: support pelvic organs; continence; relaxation for voiding; sexual function; spinal and pelvic stability with the diaphragm, transversus and multifidus.

Continence-promoting advice

Simple lifestyle advice that reduces load on the bladder and pelvic floor; given with pelvic floor training.

  • Drink normally, about 1.5–2 litres a day; cutting fluids concentrates urine and irritates the bladder.
  • Reduce caffeine, fizzy drinks and alcohol.
  • Avoid constipation and straining: fibre, fluids, a footstool on the toilet.
  • Lose weight if overweight (BMI over 30); stop smoking; treat chronic cough.
  • Sit fully on the toilet, relax and empty completely; do not hover; no "just in case" visits.
  • Do the knack before lifting, coughing or sneezing; lift with the load close.
  • Keep up pelvic floor exercises for life, especially in pregnancy, after birth and around menopause.

Interferential therapy in stress urinary incontinence

Principle: two medium-frequency currents cross in the pelvis and produce a low-frequency beat deep in the pelvic floor, with little skin discomfort. It contracts the muscle without a vaginal probe, aiming to restore reflex activity and urethral closure.

  • Electrodes: bipolar, under the ischial tuberosities and over the anterior perineum (Laycock and Green); or four electrodes, two suprapubic and two by the ischial tuberosities, crosswise.
  • Trial settings: carrier about 4000 Hz; beat 0–100 Hz; 15 minutes, 3 times a week for 15 sessions.
  • Evidence: small trials only; NICE advises it only if she cannot contract the pelvic floor, always with supervised training.
  • Avoid in: pregnancy, infection, bleeding, pelvic cancer, pacemaker.

Types of stress incontinence and their physiotherapy

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) (ICS): involuntary leakage on effort or exertion, or on sneezing or coughing. Causes: weak pelvic floor and urethral support after childbirth, menopause, chronic cough, obesity, heavy lifting.

Types by severity (Stamey grades)

  • Grade 1: leakage with coughing, sneezing or lifting.
  • Grade 2: with smaller rises in pressure: walking, standing up, climbing stairs.
  • Grade 3: leakage regardless of activity or position.

Types by mechanism

  • Urethral hypermobility: bladder neck and urethra drop on effort because support is weak (Q-tip swings more than 30°).
  • Intrinsic sphincter deficiency: urethral closure itself is weak (after surgery, severe atrophy).

Physiotherapy

  • Assess: bladder diary, pad test, cough stress test, PERFECT.
  • Pelvic floor muscle training, supervised for at least 3 months: first-line for all types. Slow holds up to 10 seconds × 10, up to 10 fast contractions; at least 8 contractions 3 times a day; lying → sitting → standing → during lifting and stairs.
  • The knack: strong contraction just before and during a cough, sneeze or lift (key for grade 1).
  • Biofeedback (perineometer, EMG) or electrical stimulation (about 50 Hz) for a very weak or unaware muscle; vaginal cones.
  • Lifestyle: weight loss, treat cough and constipation, safe lifting.
  • Grade 3 or sphincter deficiency: refer to the urogynaecologist (surgery, bulking agents), with PT before and after.

Trained women were about eight times more likely to report cure (Dumoulin et al., 2018). Review with diary, pad test and PERFECT every 4–6 weeks.

Deferment techniques for urge incontinence

Urgency incontinence: leakage with or just after a sudden urge (overactive detrusor). Deferment helps the woman hold on until she reaches the toilet.

  • Stop and stand still or sit down; pressure on the perineum helps.
  • Do several quick strong pelvic floor contractions.
  • Breathe slowly and relax.
  • Distract the mind (count backwards); curl the toes or cross the legs.
  • When the urge fades, walk calmly to the toilet.

Used within bladder training (at least 6 weeks): lengthen the gap between voids by about 15 minutes at a time towards 3–4 hours; cut caffeine.

PERFECT method of pelvic floor muscle grading

Scheme by Laycock and Jerwood (2001), assessed by digital vaginal examination with consent.

LetterWhat is measured
P: PowerMaximal contraction on the Modified Oxford scale 0–5 (0 none, 1 flicker, 2 weak, 3 moderate with lift, 4 good, 5 strong)
E: EnduranceSeconds a maximal contraction is held (up to 10)
R: RepetitionsNumber of those holds repeated (up to 10)
F: FastNumber of quick 1-second contractions (up to 10)
ECTEvery Contraction Timed: results become her exercise programme

P3 E5 R4 F6: start with 5-second holds × 4, then 6 quick contractions.

Define uterine prolapse and its types

Pelvic organ prolapse: descent of the front wall of the vagina, the back wall, the uterus (cervix) or the vault after hysterectomy. Uterine prolapse is descent of the uterus.

CompartmentType
AnteriorCystocele, urethrocele, cystourethrocele
MiddleUterine (uterovaginal); vault prolapse
PosteriorRectocele, enterocele

Degrees: first, cervix descends within the vagina; second, cervix comes out through the vaginal opening on straining; third (procidentia), whole uterus outside.

Physiotherapy management of uterine prolapse

Uterine prolapse: descent of the uterus (cervix) into or out of the vagina. Causes: childbirth (many births, long labour, big baby, instruments, pushing before full dilatation), loss of oestrogen after menopause, chronic rise in abdominal pressure (cough, constipation, heavy lifting, obesity), ageing.

Symptoms: something coming down; dragging or heaviness worse by evening; low backache; leakage or incomplete emptying; difficulty emptying the bowel.

Assessment

Symptoms, obstetric and bowel history, menopause; inspection on straining; PERFECT; bladder diary.

Physiotherapy

  1. Pelvic floor muscle training, individually taught and supervised, for at least 16 weeks: first option for symptomatic prolapse (NICE); reduced symptoms in the POPPY trial.
  2. Dose from PERFECT: slow holds up to 10 seconds × 10, up to 10 fast; at least 8 contractions 3 times a day; lying → sitting → standing → functional tasks.
  3. The knack before coughing, sneezing, lifting.
  4. Constipation: fibre, fluids; footstool with knees above hips, lean forward, breathe out, do not strain.
  5. Lifestyle: weight loss; treat chronic cough; safe lifting with the load close.
  6. Modify straining activities: heavy lifting, breath-held sit-ups, high-impact jumping.
  7. Pessary (fitted by a doctor) for women unfit for or not wanting surgery; PT continues.
  8. Surgery (vaginal hysterectomy with repair, colporrhaphy) for large prolapse: before and after, breathing and supported coughing, leg exercises, early walking, gentle pelvic floor and deep abdominal work, roll to the side, no heavy lifting for about 6 weeks.

Training improved prolapse by one POP-Q stage in 19% of women against 8% of controls (Brækken et al., 2010).

Types and degrees of urogenital (uterine) prolapse and their management

Pelvic organ prolapse: descent of one or more of the front wall of the vagina, the back wall, the uterus (cervix) or the vault after hysterectomy.

Types (by compartment)

  • Anterior: cystocele (bladder), urethrocele, cystourethrocele.
  • Middle (apical): uterine (uterovaginal); vault prolapse after hysterectomy.
  • Posterior: rectocele (rectum), enterocele (small bowel in the pouch of Douglas).

Degrees (grades) of uterine prolapse

DegreeWhat is seenPOP-Q
FirstCervix descends but stays inside the vaginaStage I: more than 1 cm above the hymen
SecondCervix comes out through the vaginal opening on strainingStage II: within 1 cm of the hymen
Third (procidentia)Whole uterus outside the vaginal openingStage III: more than 1 cm below; IV: complete eversion

Causes: childbirth, menopause, chronic raised abdominal pressure, ageing. Symptoms: dragging, heaviness by evening, backache, bladder and bowel problems.

Management

  • First and second degree (stage I–II): supervised pelvic floor muscle training for at least 16 weeks (NICE; POPPY trial); at least 8 contractions 3 times a day from the PERFECT result; the knack.
  • Lifestyle: weight loss, treat constipation and cough, safe lifting; modify straining (heavy lifting, breath-held sit-ups, jumping).
  • Pessary by the gynaecologist if surgery is unsuitable or not wanted.
  • Large prolapse: surgery (vaginal hysterectomy with repair, colporrhaphy) with PT before and after: breathing, leg exercises, early walking, gentle pelvic floor work, no heavy lifting for about 6 weeks.

Degrees of uterine prolapse: three small side views with the cervix inside the vagina, at the opening, and the uterus outside.

Indications for hysterectomy

Hysterectomy: surgical removal of the uterus; total, subtotal (cervix left) or radical (Wertheim's); abdominal, vaginal or laparoscopic.

  • Fibroids with heavy bleeding or pressure (the commonest).
  • Abnormal uterine bleeding not controlled by drugs.
  • Adenomyosis and endometriosis.
  • Uterovaginal prolapse.
  • Cancer of the uterus, cervix or ovary.
  • Chronic pelvic pain from pelvic inflammatory disease.
  • Life-threatening bleeding after childbirth.

PT after surgery: breathing and supported coughing, leg exercises, early walking, gentle pelvic floor work, no heavy lifting for about 6 weeks.

Kegel's exercises

Pelvic floor muscle training described by the gynaecologist Arnold Kegel (1948); supervised training for at least 3 months is first-line for stress and mixed incontinence (NICE).

  • Teach: "squeeze and lift as if stopping wind and the flow of urine", without holding the breath or squeezing the buttocks. Stopping the urine stream is only for finding the muscle once.
  • Dose: slow holds up to 10 seconds × 10 and up to 10 quick 1-second contractions; at least 8 contractions 3 times a day, set from PERFECT.
  • Progress: lying → sitting → standing → lifting, stairs, jumping.
  • The knack: contract just before and during a cough, sneeze or lift.

Uses: stress, urge and mixed incontinence; prolapse; pregnancy and after birth.

Tests for assessing incontinence in women

After a history of when, how much and how often she leaks, the physiotherapist uses:

  • Bladder diary (frequency–volume chart) for at least 3 days.
  • ICIQ-UI short form: severity and bother.
  • Pad test: in the 1-hour test a gain of 1 g or more is positive.
  • Cough stress test: leakage at the moment of a cough with a full bladder.
  • Q-tip test: swab swings more than 30° on straining in urethral hypermobility.
  • Digital vaginal examination: PERFECT and Modified Oxford scale; perineometer or EMG.
  • Medical: urine test, post-void residual by ultrasound, urodynamics before surgery.

Types of uterine prolapse and the effects of exercise

Pelvic organ prolapse: descent of the vaginal walls, the uterus (cervix) or the vault after hysterectomy, usually after childbirth, menopause or a chronic rise in abdominal pressure.

Types

  • By compartment: anterior (cystocele, urethrocele); middle (uterovaginal, vault); posterior (rectocele, enterocele).
  • By degree: first, cervix descends within the vagina; second, cervix comes out through the opening on straining; third (procidentia), whole uterus outside (POP-Q stages I–IV).

How exercise helps (mechanisms)

  1. Timing (the knack): contracting before a cough, lift or strain narrows the levator hiatus and lifts the floor, so the organs are not pushed down.
  2. Strength training: months of near-maximal contractions make the muscle thicker and stiffer, so the floor sits higher at rest. On ultrasound, trained women had a smaller hiatus and a higher bladder and rectum (Brækken 2010).

Effects shown in trials

  • Stage improves: one POP-Q stage better in 19% of trained women vs 8% of controls (Brækken 2010).
  • Fewer symptoms: less dragging and heaviness at 12 months in the POPPY trial (447 women, 5 physiotherapy visits over 16 weeks).
  • Indian trial: one-to-one training beat a self-help manual for symptoms and quality of life (Kashyap 2013, Chandigarh).
  • Reviews: first-line for stage I–III, with no serious side effects; needs supervision (Bø 2022). Long-term benefit is uncertain, and training added around surgery did not improve the result.
  • Also: less leakage and better support of the spine and pelvis.

Prescription

Supervised training for at least 16 weeks for stage 1–2 (NICE): 3 sets of 8–12 near-maximal contractions 3–4 times a week, then maintenance; the knack; weight loss, treat constipation and cough. Modify, do not stop, straining activities (heavy lifting, breath-held sit-ups, jumping).

Large prolapse needs a pessary or surgery; exercise continues alongside.
