viiiPleuritis (pleurisy)

Definition

Pleurisy (pleuritis): inflammation of the pleura, causing sharp pain on breathing.

Clinical features

  • Sharp pain on breathing in and coughing; pleural rub (a creaking sound).
  • Inflamed pleura hurts when it moves, so breathing becomes shallow on that side.

Medical management

Treat the cause; pain relief.

Physiotherapy

  • Pain relief first; lying on the painful side may splint it and ease pain.
  • Supported breathing: hand pressure over the painful area during deep breaths and coughing.
  • Gentle thoracic expansion as pain allows, to prevent basal collapse.
  • Early mobilisation.

ixPleural effusion

Definition and types

Pleural effusion: abnormal fluid in the pleural space. It is a transudate or an exudate (Light's criteria). The fluid is an exudate if any one of these is present:

  1. Pleural fluid protein ÷ serum protein more than 0.5
  2. Pleural fluid LDH ÷ serum LDH more than 0.6
  3. Pleural fluid LDH more than two-thirds of the upper normal limit for serum LDH
TypeMechanismCommon causes
TransudatePressure imbalance; pleura normalHeart failure, cirrhosis, kidney disease, low albumin
ExudateInflamed or diseased pleura leaks proteinPneumonia (parapneumonic), TB (common in India), cancer, pulmonary embolism

Clinical features

Gradual breathlessness. On the affected side: reduced expansion, stony dull percussion, reduced or absent breath sounds with bronchial breathing above the fluid; a large effusion pushes the trachea away (compare with pneumothorax in v above).

Radiological findings (3 marks in S16)

  • Blunting of the costophrenic angle (needs about 200 mL or more on a PA film; less on a lateral film).
  • A homogeneous white (opaque) area at the lung base with a curved upper border rising towards the axilla (meniscus sign).
  • Loss of the diaphragm outline on that side.
  • Large effusion: mediastinum and trachea pushed to the opposite side; whole hemithorax white ("white-out").
  • Lying films: diffuse haziness rather than a level.

Other tests: ultrasound to find and mark fluid; CT for complex cases; pleural tap (aspiration) for protein, LDH, glucose, pH, cells, culture, TB tests and cancer cells.

Medical management

Treat the cause; therapeutic aspiration or an intercostal drain for large or symptomatic effusions; pleurodesis or an indwelling catheter for recurrent malignant effusion. Rapid drainage of a large effusion can cause re-expansion pulmonary oedema.

Physiotherapy (also for empyema)

  • Positioning: lie on the unaffected side, so the affected lung is uppermost and can expand, and the healthy lung below has better ventilation and blood flow.
  • Localised thoracic expansion exercises to the affected side (hands on the lower ribs for feedback), with a 3-second hold.
  • Supported coughing; ACBT if secretions are present.
  • Early mobilisation; walking with the drain.
  • Trunk side-bending away from the affected side and posture correction; shoulder exercises.
  • After resolution: exercise training to restore fitness.

xEmpyema and other pleural disorders

Definitions

  • Empyema: pus in the pleural space.
  • Haemothorax: blood in the pleural space (usually trauma).
  • Fibrothorax: the thick peel left by untreated empyema or haemothorax, which traps the lung, reduces chest wall movement and can cause scoliosis.

Clinical features

Empyema: swinging fever, sweats, chest pain, not improving on antibiotics.

Medical and surgical management

Antibiotics, chest drain, sometimes drugs to break down the fluid; surgery (VATS or decortication) if it does not drain.

Physiotherapy

As for pleural effusion (ix above), with the drain care and precautions of v above. Early expansion exercises and posture work help prevent pleural thickening and a trapped lung. The S25 long case of empyema with a drain is worked in full below.
