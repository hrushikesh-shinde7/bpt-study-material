1Ergonomic advice for patients

  • Posture: sit with the pelvis upright, feet supported, back supported; change position often; avoid long sustained postures.
  • Workstation: screen at eye level, elbows at about 90°, forearms supported, chair and desk heights adjusted; for wheelchair users, knee clearance and items within easy reach.
  • Energy conservation (MS, MND, myasthenia, post-polio, Parkinson's): plan, prioritise, pace, sit to work, rest before fatigue, do heavy tasks when strongest.
  • Joint protection: use large joints and both hands, avoid sustained grips, use aids (built-up handles, trolleys), protect the hemiplegic shoulder.
  • Home layout: frequently used items between hip and shoulder height, clear walkways, good lighting, rails and non-slip surfaces (see adaptive devices).
  • Return to work: graded hours, task changes, rest breaks, travel plan, a workplace visit when possible.

2Educating parents and caregivers

  • Handling and carrying: positions matched to the child's tone (spastic: break up the stiff pattern with hips bent and apart; athetoid: limbs together and bent, close to the carer); see cerebral palsy.
  • Positioning through the day: side lying, prone on a wedge, supported sitting; avoid W-sitting and long periods lying on the back.
  • Feeding: upright, head slightly forward, trunk supported; refer for swallowing assessment when there is coughing or chest infection.
  • Dressing: dress the stiffer or weaker limb first and undress it last.
  • Transfers and turning in adults: slide sheets, transfer boards, hoists; never lift under the arms (see lifting).
  • Home programme: short, written, illustrated, built into daily routines (bath time, play, meals); demonstrate it and ask the carer to show it back.
  • Warning signs to report (for example shunt problems, autonomic dysreflexia, breathing trouble, skin redness).

3Protecting the carer

  • Use equipment rather than lifting; set the bed and chairs at working height; keep loads close; avoid twisting.
  • Training in lifting technique alone does not prevent back pain (Cochrane, 2011), so equipment and planning matter more.
  • Watch for carer strain: tiredness, low mood, back pain; involve other family members, respite and support groups.

3 mEnergy conservation techniques.

Plan the day; prioritise important tasks; pace with rests before fatigue; sit to work; organise items within reach; use aids and trolleys; do demanding tasks when strongest; avoid heat (MS).

7 mHome-care advice to the parents of a child with cerebral palsy.

Positioning (side lying, prone on a wedge, supported sitting; no W-sitting), carrying matched to tone, feeding upright with head slightly forward, dressing the stiffer limb first, daily play practising head control, sitting and reaching, standing frame time, splint and skin care, no baby walkers, a short written home programme shown back by the parents, and warning signs and follow-up.
