1The vestibular system

  • Parts: three semicircular canals on each side (anterior, posterior, horizontal) sense head rotation; two otolith organs (utricle and saccule) sense linear movement and gravity; the vestibular nerve; the vestibular nuclei; and the cerebellum.
  • Vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR): moves the eyes opposite to the head so vision stays clear during head movement.
  • Vestibulospinal reflexes: keep the body upright and steady.
  • Balance comes from three inputs, vision, somatosensation and the vestibular system, integrated by the brain.

2Vestibular lesions: signs, symptoms and types

Signs and symptoms of a vestibular lesion (7 marks with adaptation exercises in S17)

  • Vertigo: a false sense of spinning or movement; dizziness and light-headedness.
  • Nystagmus: in peripheral lesions, horizontal-torsional, beating away from the damaged side, reduced by fixing the gaze.
  • Imbalance: unsteady standing and walking, veering or falling towards the damaged side; falls.
  • Oscillopsia: the world seems to jump during head movement (VOR failure), worst in bilateral loss.
  • Nausea and vomiting, sweating, motion sensitivity.
  • Hearing loss, tinnitus, ear fullness if the cochlea is involved (Ménière's disease, labyrinthitis).
  • Anxiety and avoidance of head movement, which delays recovery.

Peripheral vs central vertigo

Red flag: HINTS in acute vertigo

In a person with continuous vertigo and nystagmus, a normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus or skew deviation (vertical eye misalignment on the cover test) points to a stroke. Refer as an emergency.

4Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)

Asked for 7 marks in S20 and W21 (physiotherapy for BPPV), S24 (two manoeuvres) and S26 (diagnostic test for posterior canal BPPV and one treatment).

  • What it is: otoconia (calcium crystals) come loose from the utricle and move into a semicircular canal, most often the posterior canal. Head movement shifts the crystals and stimulates the canal, causing brief vertigo.
  • Features: brief spinning (seconds to under a minute) on lying down, turning in bed, looking up or bending; worse in the morning; no hearing loss.
  • Common causes: idiopathic in older people; after head injury; after inner-ear disease; prolonged bed rest.

Diagnosis (S26, 3 marks)

Posterior canal BPPV is diagnosed when the Dix-Hallpike test provokes vertigo with torsional, upbeating nystagmus: the person is brought from sitting to lying with the head turned 45° to one side and the neck extended 20°, affected ear down. If it is negative, test the other side. If the history fits but the test shows horizontal or no nystagmus, do a supine roll test for horizontal canal BPPV (BPPV guideline, 2017).

Canalith repositioning: the Epley manoeuvre (right posterior canal)

  1. Sit the person on the couch with the head turned 45° to the right.
  2. Lie them back quickly with the neck extended about 20° (the right Dix-Hallpike position); wait until the vertigo and nystagmus stop, about 30 seconds or more.
  3. Turn the head 90° to the left, keeping the neck extended; wait about 30 seconds.
  4. Roll the person onto the left side while turning the head a further 90°, so the nose points down towards the floor; wait about 30 seconds.
  5. Bring them up to sitting with the head still turned and the chin slightly down.
  6. Repeat the Dix-Hallpike test; repeat the manoeuvre if it is still positive.

Semont (liberatory) manoeuvre (right posterior canal)

  1. Sit the person on the side of the couch with the head turned 45° to the left (away from the affected ear).
  2. Move them quickly onto the right side, keeping the head turned, so the back of the head rests on the couch; wait about 1 minute.
  3. Swing them quickly in one movement through sitting onto the left side, head still turned, so the nose points down; wait about 1 minute.
  4. Return slowly to sitting.
  • Horizontal canal BPPV: barbecue (Lempert) roll or the Gufoni manoeuvre.
  • Brandt–Daroff exercises: a home habituation option (repeated lying to each side from sitting); less effective than repositioning, useful for residual symptoms.

What the BPPV guideline (2017) says

  • Treat posterior canal BPPV with a canalith repositioning procedure; observation with follow-up is also an option.
  • Do not order imaging or vestibular testing if the diagnosis is clear, and do not routinely give vestibular suppressant medicines.
  • Vestibular rehabilitation may be offered; reassess within 1 month; teach about falls and recurrence.

Classic answer vs current guidance: after the Epley manoeuvre

Classic

Sleep propped upright for 48 hours, wear a soft collar and avoid bending or turning the head after the manoeuvre.

Current

The 2017 guideline advises against post-procedure postural restrictions: they add nothing to the success of the manoeuvre.

Write in the exam

"Epley manoeuvre, then repeat the Dix-Hallpike test; no postural restrictions are needed; reassess within a month."

5Vestibular rehabilitation

Asked for 7 marks in S22 (vestibular rehabilitation), S24 (principles and two manoeuvres), S17 (adaptation exercises) and W25 (substitution and habituation exercises).

Principles

  • Offer exercise-based vestibular rehabilitation to adults with one-sided or two-sided vestibular hypofunction; start early, within the first 2 weeks of an acute loss (Hall et al., 2022).
  • The brain recovers by adaptation (recalibrating the VOR), habituation (reducing the response to provoking movements) and substitution (using other senses and strategies).
  • Movement provokes mild symptoms during exercise; that is expected and helps recovery. Vestibular suppressant medicines slow compensation and should be stopped as soon as possible.
  • Supervised sessions plus a home programme; progress difficulty step by step; combine with balance and gait training.

The three exercise types

TypeHow it works and examplesUsed for
Adaptation (gaze stabilisation)Image movement on the retina during head movement (retinal slip) drives the cerebellum to increase VOR gain. VOR ×1: keep the eyes on a fixed target while moving the head side to side, then up and down. VOR ×2: the target moves opposite to the head. Progress speed, duration, background and position (sitting, standing, walking)One-sided loss (vestibular neuritis, after surgery)
HabituationRepeated, controlled exposure to the movements or visual scenes that provoke mild dizziness lessens the response over time. Choose movements from the Motion Sensitivity Quotient test; repeat each a few times, twice a day, letting symptoms settle between repetitions; Brandt–Daroff exercises are one exampleMotion-provoked dizziness; visual motion sensitivity; persistent dizziness
SubstitutionReplaces lost vestibular function with other systems: vision and touch for balance; planned eye–head movements (look at a target, then turn the head); a stick, night lights, avoiding dark or uneven groundBilateral or complete loss

Doses (Hall et al., 2022)

ConditionGaze stabilisationBalance and gait
Acute or subacute one-sided loss3 times a day, at least 12 minutes in total dailyStatic and dynamic balance and walking, progressed
Chronic one-sided loss3–5 times a day, at least 20 minutes daily, for 4–6 weeksAt least 20 minutes daily for 4–6 weeks
Bilateral loss3–5 times a day, 20–40 minutes daily, for about 5–7 weeksAt least 20 minutes daily for 6–9 weeks

Weekly clinic visits plus the home programme. Stop when goals are met, symptoms resolve, or progress plateaus.

Classic answer vs current guidance: eye exercises

Classic

Cawthorne–Cooksey exercises begin with eye movements alone (looking up and down, side to side) with the head still.

Current

Voluntary saccades or smooth pursuit without head movement should not be used to improve gaze stability: the evidence shows harm outweighs benefit (Hall et al., 2022). Use gaze stabilisation with head movement.

Write in the exam

"Gaze stabilisation exercises with head movement (VOR ×1, VOR ×2), habituation, balance and gait training, as a supervised and home programme."

Balance and gait training

  • Standing with a narrowing base, on firm then soft surfaces, eyes open then closed.
  • Weight shifts, reaching, stepping, tandem walking; walking with head turns and pivots; dual tasks; varying speed.
  • Community walking: crowds, stairs, uneven ground; graded return to driving and work.

7Precautions and red flags

Take care
  • Refer urgently if vertigo comes with a new headache, double vision, slurred speech, weakness, numbness, inability to walk, or a central HINTS finding.
  • Before the Dix-Hallpike test, check for neck problems, carotid or vertebral artery disease and recent neck injury; use a modified side-lying test if neck extension is unsafe.
  • Guard closely during manoeuvres and balance work; keep a vomit bowl ready.
  • Advise the person not to drive until symptoms settle.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mDescribe the Dix-Hallpike test.

Sitting, head turned 45° to one side; lie back quickly with the neck extended 20° below horizontal (that ear down). Positive for posterior canal BPPV: vertigo with torsional, upbeating nystagmus after a short delay, lasting under a minute. Repeat on the other side if negative.

3 mWhat are substitution exercises?

Exercises that replace lost vestibular function with other senses and strategies: balance training using vision and touch, planned eye–head movements (look at the target first, then turn the head), assistive devices and environmental changes (night lights). Used mainly for bilateral loss.

7 mDescribe the principles of vestibular rehabilitation and any two manoeuvres.

Principles: early, exercise-based, supervised plus home programme; adaptation (VOR ×1, ×2), habituation (repeated exposure to provoking movements) and substitution (vision, touch, strategies); balance and gait training; progress step by step; mild symptoms during exercise are expected; stop suppressant medicines early. Doses: gaze exercises 3 times a day for at least 12 minutes in acute loss, 3–5 times a day for 20 minutes in chronic loss for 4–6 weeks.

Manoeuvres: Epley (sitting with the head turned 45° to the affected side, lie back with the neck extended, turn the head 90° to the other side, roll onto that side nose down, sit up) and Semont (head turned away, lie quickly onto the affected side, swing through to the other side nose down, sit up).

LAQA 58-year-old woman has had brief spinning attacks for a week whenever she lies down or turns in bed. Plan the assessment and physiotherapy. (Practice case)

Assessment: history (brief positional vertigo, no hearing loss, no neurological symptoms); red flags; neck and vascular screen; Dix-Hallpike both sides (torsional upbeating nystagmus = posterior canal BPPV); supine roll test if needed; balance (Romberg, mCTSIB), falls risk, Dizziness Handicap Inventory.

Physiotherapy: Epley manoeuvre on the affected side, repeat the test, repeat the manoeuvre if still positive; no postural restrictions; teach about recurrence and falls; Brandt–Daroff or habituation exercises if symptoms remain; balance training if unsteady; review within a month.
