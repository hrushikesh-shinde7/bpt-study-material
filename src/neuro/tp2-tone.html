1Muscle tone: definition and classification

Asked for 3 marks in S20 (define and classify) and with hypotonia for 7 marks in W17.

  • Muscle tone is the resistance of a relaxed muscle to passive stretch. It has a neural part (the stretch reflex and descending control) and a non-neural part (the stiffness of muscle and connective tissue).
  • Normal tone is high enough to hold posture against gravity and low enough to allow easy, selective movement.
TypeFeaturesTypical lesion
SpasticityVelocity-dependent: more resistance with faster stretch; clasp-knife; brisk reflexes, clonusUpper motor neuron: stroke, SCI, MS, CP
RigiditySame resistance at any speed, in flexors and extensors (lead-pipe; cogwheel with tremor)Basal ganglia: Parkinson's disease
DystoniaSustained or intermittent contractions causing twisting posturesBasal ganglia: dyskinetic CP, focal dystonias
Hypotonia (flaccidity)Low resistance, floppy, hypermobile joints, reduced reflexesLMN, cerebellum, early stroke or spinal shock, Down's syndrome
Fluctuating toneTone swings between low and highDyskinetic (athetoid) CP

Spasticity (Lance, 1980): a motor disorder with a velocity-dependent increase in tonic stretch reflexes and exaggerated tendon jerks, from hyperexcitability of the stretch reflex, as one part of the upper motor neuron syndrome. The syndrome also has negative features (weakness, loss of dexterity, fatigue) that limit function more than the spasticity itself.

2Measuring tone

Modified Ashworth Scale (3 marks in S19 and W21)

GradeMeaning
0No increase in tone
1Slight increase: a catch and release, or minimal resistance at the end of the range
1+Slight increase: a catch followed by minimal resistance through less than half of the remaining range
2More marked increase through most of the range, but the part moves easily
3Considerable increase; passive movement is difficult
4The part is rigid in flexion or extension

How to test: the patient lies relaxed; move the part through its full range in about one second, at most three times; grade the first movement.

  • Modified Tardieu Scale: move the joint slowly (R2, full passive range) and as fast as possible (R1, the angle of the "catch"). A large gap between R2 and R1 means a strong dynamic (reflex) component that may respond to stretch, casting or botulinum toxin; a small gap means fixed contracture.
  • Also: spasm frequency scores, the pendulum test, clonus count; and always the effect of tone on function.

3Spasticity: when and why to treat

  • Treat when it causes a problem: pain, spasms, poor hygiene or dressing, contracture, poor seating, or interference with movement.
  • Sometimes it helps: leg extensor tone can help standing and transfers; do not remove it without checking function.
  • Look for triggers first: pain, full bladder or urine infection, constipation, pressure sores, ingrown toenails, tight clothing, anxiety, fatigue.
  • Team options: oral drugs (baclofen, tizanidine, dantrolene); botulinum toxin for focal spasticity followed by stretching, splinting or casting and task practice (a strong recommendation after stroke, AHA/ASA 2026); intrathecal baclofen for severe generalised spasticity; nerve blocks; surgery.

4Techniques to reduce tone

Asked for 7 marks in S19 (tone-inhibiting techniques) and W20 (physiotherapy of spasticity), and for 3 marks in S23 (three methods to reduce tone) and W18 (slow icing).

TechniqueHowWhy it works
PositioningPositions opposite to the spastic pattern in lying, sitting and standing; avoid long periods in the spastic patternKeeps muscles at length and reduces reflex activity
Prolonged stretchSlow, sustained stretch held for minutes, by positioning, a splint, a standing frame or a castReduces stretch reflex excitability for a short time and keeps length
Weight-bearingThrough the extended arm in sitting; standing on a plantigrade footProlonged stretch plus joint compression
Slow icing (prolonged cold)An ice pack wrapped in a damp towel, or cold immersion, over the spastic muscle for about 15–20 minutesCooling slows nerve conduction and calms the muscle spindle, so tone drops for a short time: use the window for stretching and active practice
Neutral warmth and slow strokingWrap the limb in a blanket; slow strokes down the backCalming sensory input (Rood)
Rhythmic rotationSlow, rhythmic rotation of the trunk or limb through a gradually larger rangeRelaxes the muscle before movement
Splints and serial castingResting hand splint; AFO; casts changed every few days to gain rangeLow-load, long-duration stretch
Electrical stimulationStimulate the antagonist (for example wrist extensors in a flexed hand) during task practiceReciprocal inhibition and active movement
Active task practiceStrengthen the weak antagonists; practise reaching and walkingFunction improves when weakness and control are treated, not tone alone

Three methods to reduce tone (S23): prolonged stretch with positioning or a splint; prolonged cold (slow icing); weight-bearing. Others: neutral warmth, rhythmic rotation, reflex-inhibiting patterns, electrical stimulation of antagonists.

Classic answer vs current evidence: stretching for spasticity

Classic

Daily passive stretching reduces spasticity and prevents contractures.

Current

Regular stretch for under 7 months has no clinically important effect on joint mobility, and little or no effect on spasticity, pain or activity (Cochrane). Combine positioning, splints or casts with active task practice, and use botulinum toxin with therapy for problem focal spasticity.

Write in the exam

"Treat triggers; positioning, prolonged stretch with splints or casts, weight-bearing and cold to reduce tone for practice; active task training; botulinum toxin with therapy for focal spasticity."

5Hypotonia and facilitation techniques

Asked for 3 marks in W16 (three facilitatory techniques) and 7 marks in W17 (physiotherapy of hypotonia) and W24 (facilitatory techniques to improve tone).

Quick stretch and tappingA brisk stretch or tap over the muscle belly or tendon just before asking for movementExcites the muscle spindle and stretch reflex

Physiotherapy of hypotonia (W17)

  • Support and protect hypermobile joints (shoulder support in early stroke; avoid overstretching).
  • Positioning against gravity with support: prone over a wedge, supported sitting, standing frame.
  • Facilitation (table above) just before asking for active movement.
  • Co-contraction and holding: weight-bearing in kneeling and standing, rhythmic stabilisation.
  • Strengthening and functional training: head and trunk control, sit-to-stand, walking.
  • Teach carers handling and safe positions.

6Precautions

Take care
  • Cold: check skin sensation and circulation; avoid in cold sensitivity, Raynaud's phenomenon and over numb skin without close checks.
  • Never force a spastic joint; it causes pain and more spasticity.
  • Facilitation: quick, intense stimuli can increase spasticity in another part of the body; stop if tone rises unwanted.
  • Fast vestibular input: avoid in seizures, raised intracranial pressure and with nausea.
  • Splints and casts: check the skin daily for red marks.

3 mDescribe the Modified Ashworth Scale.

0 no increase; 1 slight increase with a catch and release or minimal resistance at the end of range; 1+ catch then minimal resistance through less than half the range; 2 marked increase through most of the range but easily moved; 3 considerable increase, passive movement difficult; 4 rigid in flexion or extension.

3 mWhat is slow icing?

Prolonged cold over a spastic muscle (an ice pack or cold immersion for about 15–20 minutes). It slows nerve conduction and calms the muscle spindle, so tone falls for a short time; the window is used for stretching and active practice. Quick icing, by contrast, facilitates.

7 mWhat is muscle tone? Describe physiotherapy for hypotonia. (3 + 4)

Tone (3): resistance of a relaxed muscle to passive stretch, with neural and non-neural parts; types: normal, hypertonia (spasticity, rigidity, dystonia), hypotonia, fluctuating.

Hypotonia (4): protect joints; positioning against gravity with support; facilitation (quick stretch, tapping, approximation, resistance, fast brushing, quick icing, vibration, bouncing); co-contraction in weight-bearing; rhythmic stabilisation; electrical stimulation; strengthening and functional training; carer education.

LAQA man 4 months after stroke has Modified Ashworth grade 2 elbow and wrist flexors that make dressing difficult. Plan the management of his spasticity. (Practice case)

Assess: MAS, Tardieu (R1 and R2), range, pain, triggers, function (dressing). Physiotherapy: treat triggers; positioning of the arm out of the flexor pattern; weight-bearing through the extended arm; prolonged stretch with a resting hand splint at night; slow icing or rhythmic rotation before practice; electrical stimulation of wrist and finger extensors during reaching and grasping; task practice for dressing; strengthening of extensors. Refer for botulinum toxin to the elbow and wrist flexors, followed by stretching, splinting and task practice; review range and function.
