1The ICF model

  • ICF (International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health; WHO, 2001) describes health and disability as an interaction between a health condition and contextual factors.
  • It is biopsychosocial: it joins the medical model (disability caused by disease, fixed by treatment) and the social model (disability created by society and the environment).
  • Functioning is the positive side (body functions, activities, participation); disability is the negative side (impairments, activity limitations, participation restrictions).
  • Principles: universality (it applies to everyone, not only disabled people), parity (no split by cause into "physical" and "mental"), neutrality (neutral wording), and environmental influence.

Qualifiers

  • A code becomes a classification when a qualifier is added after the point: 0 no problem, 1 mild, 2 moderate, 3 severe, 4 complete problem (8 not specified, 9 not applicable).
  • For example, b730.3 is a severe impairment of muscle power.
  • Environmental factors use a minus scale for barriers and a plus for facilitators: e120.2 is a moderate barrier, e120+2 a moderate facilitator.

3Bidirectional relationships and their clinical use

  • Impairment to activity: spasticity and weak dorsiflexors limit walking.
  • Activity back to impairment: not walking causes weakness, contracture and low fitness (secondary impairments).
  • Participation to body functions: losing a job or friends lowers mood and activity, which worsens fatigue and fitness.
  • Environment to activity: a ramp or walking aid improves mobility; stairs and an Indian-style toilet make it harder.
  • Personal factors: motivation and coping change how much practice happens and how much is recovered.

Clinical use: treat at more than one level at once (for example, strengthen, practise walking, and change the home), and predict secondary problems before they happen.

5Capacity and performance

  • Capacity: the highest level a person can achieve in a standard environment, without help or devices (what the person can do in the clinic).
  • Performance: what the person actually does in their usual environment, with whatever help and devices they really use (what the person does at home).
  • The gap: if capacity is greater than performance, something in the environment is a barrier; if performance is better than capacity, the environment is helping.
  • Example: a man after stroke walks 50 m with a stick in the clinic (capacity) but never leaves his first-floor flat because of the stairs (performance). Treatment: stair training and a rail, not only more gait training.

6Patient-specific goals with clinical reasoning

  • Set goals with the patient and family, from what matters to them (work, toilet independence, walking to the temple or market).
  • Make them SMART: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.
  • Short-term goals (2–6 weeks): impairments and basic activities. Long-term goals (months): activities and participation.
  • Give the reason for each goal from the ICF (the clinical reasoning) and the expected outcome from the prognosis.

7Documentation and red flags

Documentation of disability and functioning

  • Record each ICF level with measures (see scales), the functional diagnosis, the goals and the dates.
  • ICF codes with qualifiers can summarise problems; ICF Core Sets (short lists of the most relevant categories) exist for conditions such as stroke, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, brain injury and cerebral palsy.
  • Written records support the disability certificate and access to schemes.

8Writing ICF in a long case

  1. Spot the clues in the question: age, job, family, home, the timing, and each symptom.
  2. Draw the model and fill two or three points into each box.
  3. Make a table with three columns: component, findings from the case, and clinical reasoning (why it happens or why it matters).
  4. Write a one-line functional diagnosis that links the health condition, the main impairments, the activity limitations and the participation restriction.
  5. Go on to goals that come straight from the table.

Every condition page in these notes has a worked example in section 10 (for example stroke, cerebral palsy, GBS).

3 mDifferentiate capacity and performance.

Capacity is the highest level a person can do in a standard environment without help or devices; performance is what the person actually does in the usual environment with the help and devices actually used. If capacity is higher, the environment is a barrier; if performance is higher, the environment is a facilitator.

3 mGive examples of environmental facilitators and barriers in a patient with paraplegia.

Facilitators: a light wheelchair, ground-floor home with ramps, western toilet with rails, supportive family and employer, disability schemes. Barriers: stairs, Indian-style toilet, uneven roads, crowded transport, cost of equipment, negative attitudes.

Body functions (mental; sensory and pain; voice and speech; cardiovascular, haematological, immunological and respiratory; digestive, metabolic and endocrine; genitourinary and reproductive; neuromusculoskeletal and movement-related; skin). LAQPractice: using a 65-year-old woman with Parkinson's disease (Hoehn and Yahr III) who has stopped going to the temple after two falls, explain the ICF with its bidirectional relationships and write two SMART goals. (7 + 5 + 3)

ICF (7): health condition PD; body functions: bradykinesia, rigidity, postural instability, fear of falling; activities: turning, walking outdoors, steps; participation: temple visits, social life; environment: crowded temple steps (barrier), supportive daughter (facilitator); personal: older, anxious. Relationships (5): postural instability causes falls; falls cause fear and less walking; less walking causes weakness and lower fitness; isolation lowers mood and motivation; a handrail and a companion restore participation. Goals (3): "TUG under 13.5 s in 8 weeks"; "walks to the temple with her daughter once a week by 3 months".
