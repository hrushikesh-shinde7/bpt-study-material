1The paper and a time plan

The syllabus prints a scheme with MCQs and 5-mark questions, but it was never used in 2016–2026. The real paper:

  • About 2 minutes a mark; never spend 20 minutes on a 3-mark answer.
  • If you write slowly, do the two long cases first so 30 marks are safe.
  • Choose the case whose condition you know best, not the shorter-looking one. The two options usually mix an upper and a lower motor neuron condition, or adult and paediatric.

3Short answers: 3 and 7 marks

3 marks (about half a page)

  1. One-line definition.
  2. Three to five points, a small table or a labelled sketch.
  3. One line of physiotherapy relevance.
Example: "Autonomic dysreflexia" (3 marks)

Definition: a sudden dangerous rise in blood pressure in spinal cord injury at or above T6, triggered by a stimulus below the lesion. Features: pounding headache, flushing and sweating above the lesion, slow pulse, blocked nose. Management: sit up and lower the legs, loosen clothing, check the bladder then the bowel, monitor blood pressure; the commonest cause is a full bladder.

7 marks (1.5–2 pages)

  1. Definition or introduction (2 lines).
  2. Classification, cause or the "why" (a flowchart or table is ideal).
  3. The main body as 6–8 headed points: assessment points, aims, techniques with doses, by stage or by problem.
  4. A labelled diagram where one exists.
  5. Precautions.
  6. One closing line: an outcome measure or the evidence.

Each condition page has an answer skeleton with marks for the questions actually asked.

4Long answers: the 15-mark case

  1. Read the split (for example 5 + 7 + 3 or 7 + 8) and give each part space in proportion.
  2. Underline the clues: age, job and earning role, family, home (stairs, rural), time since onset, the main complaint, what the patient wants.
  3. Draw the ICF model, then the ICF table with a "clinical reasoning" column, and a one-line functional diagnosis (see ICF).
  4. Problem list in order of priority.
  5. Goals: short- and long-term, SMART, with time frames, linked to the problems.
  6. Management: by phase or stage, then by impairment with technique, dose and reason; precautions; education and home programme.
  7. Outcome measures and discharge criteria in one or two lines.

Upper vs lower motor neuron cases

  • UMN (stroke, SCI, MS, CP, brain injury): tone, motor control and task practice, balance and gait, cognition and perception, prevention of contracture.
  • LMN (GBS, nerve injury, polio, neuropathy): protection of denervated muscle and numb skin, strengthening by grade, splints, breathing in GBS, re-education as reinnervation comes.

5Diagrams that earn marks

  • The ICF model (every long case).
  • The motor pathway with UMN and LMN marked; the circle of Willis; incomplete cord syndromes.
  • The autonomic dysreflexia flowchart; types of neurogenic bladder.
  • Brachial plexus; nerve injury classification; the strength-duration curve.
  • The gait cycle; the iliotibial band chain in polio; types of spina bifida; the neuromuscular junction.

Keep diagrams simple, labelled and large enough to read; a diagram never replaces the text.

6Common mistakes and a final checklist

  • Writing everything known about the condition instead of answering the parts asked.
  • Goals without time frames; treatment without doses; no precautions.
  • Ignoring the patient's job, family and home in the case.
  • Old answers the guidelines have moved on from (for example passive stretching alone, bed rest, avoiding exercise in MS or myasthenia): check the "classic vs current" boxes.

Checklist before you hand in

  • Question numbers and sub-letters correct; choices clear.
  • Keywords underlined; headings visible.
  • Every long case has the ICF model, functional diagnosis, goals with time frames, and doses.
