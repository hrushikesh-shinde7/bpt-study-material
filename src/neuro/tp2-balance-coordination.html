1What balance needs

  • Balance (postural control) is keeping the centre of mass over the base of support, in stillness (static), during movement (dynamic) and when disturbed (reactive).
  • Sensory systems: vision, the vestibular system and somatosensation (pressure under the feet and joint position). The brain reweights them by situation: for example, more vestibular and vision on foam.
  • Central processing: cerebellum, basal ganglia, brainstem and cortex integrate the information and choose a response.
  • Motor systems: strength, range of motion (especially ankle), and coordinated postural strategies; anticipatory adjustments before a voluntary movement and reactive ones after a disturbance.
  • Cognition and attention: balance worsens when attention is divided (dual tasks).

2Motor strategies of balance

Asked for 3 marks in S18 (three motor strategies).

StrategyWhen it is usedHow it works
Ankle strategySmall, slow disturbances on a firm, wide surfaceThe body sways as one piece about the ankles; muscles fire from distal to proximal
Hip strategyLarger, faster disturbances, or a narrow or soft surfaceQuick flexion or extension at the hips moves the centre of mass; muscles fire from proximal to distal
Stepping (or reaching) strategyThe centre of mass moves outside the base of supportA rapid step or a reach to grab support creates a new base

A suspensory strategy (lowering the centre of mass by bending the knees) is sometimes added.

3Evaluating sensory and motor strategies

Asked for 7 marks in W18.

Sensory strategies

  • Modified Clinical Test of Sensory Interaction in Balance (mCTSIB): stand 30 seconds in four conditions: firm surface eyes open, firm eyes closed, foam eyes open, foam eyes closed. Falls with eyes closed on a firm surface suggest somatosensory loss; falls on foam with eyes closed suggest vestibular loss or poor sensory reweighting.
  • Romberg and sharpened Romberg: loss of balance when the eyes close points to sensory (proprioceptive) ataxia.
  • Sensory Organisation Test on computerised posturography (six conditions) where available.

Motor strategies

  • Reactive responses: push and release test or a nudge in each direction; observe the ankle, hip or stepping response and whether it is quick and effective. The pull (retropulsion) test in Parkinson's disease.
  • Limits of stability and anticipatory control: functional reach test (reach forward along a ruler at shoulder height; less than about 15 cm suggests a high fall risk); multidirectional reach; rising on toes.
  • Systems tools: BESTest or Mini-BESTest (biomechanical constraints, stability limits, anticipatory adjustments, reactive responses, sensory orientation, dynamic gait).
  • Function and fall risk: Berg Balance Scale (0–56), Timed Up and Go, Dynamic Gait Index or Functional Gait Assessment, dual-task walking.

4Training static and dynamic standing balance

Asked for 7 marks in S16 and for 3 marks in S17 (three strategies to improve dynamic balance).

Ways to make standing balance harder (progression)

ChangeEasierHarder
Base of supportFeet apartFeet together, semi-tandem, tandem, single leg
SurfaceFirm floorFoam, wobble board, uneven ground
VisionEyes openEyes closed, moving visual scenes
Upper limb supportHands on parallel barsNo hands, then holding an object
HeadStillTurning and nodding
AttentionSingle taskTalking, counting backwards, carrying a tray

Static balance

  • Hold positions in the progression above, increasing the time (for example 30 seconds) before making the task harder.
  • Weight evenly on both legs with a mirror or scale feedback (after stroke).

Dynamic balance (S17: three strategies)

  • Reaching beyond arm's length in all directions, to the floor and overhead.
  • Weight shifts and stepping: forward, backward, sideways, onto a step, over obstacles; turning.
  • Reactive (perturbation) training: expected, then unexpected pushes or pulls to train ankle, hip and stepping responses.
  • Walking variations: changing speed and direction, narrow paths, head turns, carrying objects, dual tasks.
  • Functional and group activities: tai chi, dance, ball games (catching and throwing).

Dose

For preventing falls in older people, exercise programmes that challenge balance and total more than 3 hours a week have the largest effect (a 39% fall reduction; Sherrington et al., 2017). Balance must be challenged near the limit of ability, safely.

Recall check. Make a five-step progression of standing balance for a patient who can stand only with feet apart and eyes open.

5Coordination

  • Tests: finger-nose, finger-finger, heel-shin, rapid alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia), rebound test, drawing a circle with the foot, tapping; each with eyes open and closed to separate cerebellar from sensory problems.
  • Frenkel's exercises: slow, precise, repeated movements of increasing difficulty, first in lying, then sitting, standing and walking, watching the limb and moving to counts or floor markings. Vision replaces the lost position sense, so they suit sensory ataxia best.
  • Other methods: PNF rhythmic stabilisation and stabilising reversals for proximal stability; slow reversals for smooth control; approximation; light weights on the wrist or ankle for some patients; task practice of real activities.

6Precautions

Take care
  • Use a gait belt, parallel bars or a harness; stand on the weaker side; clear the area of obstacles.
  • Check blood pressure in people with orthostatic hypotension before standing work.
  • Progress one variable at a time; the task should be challenging but safe.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite three motor strategies of balance.

Ankle strategy (small, slow sway about the ankles on a firm surface); hip strategy (larger, faster disturbances or narrow surface, movement at the hips); stepping strategy (a step or reach when the centre of mass leaves the base).

3 mGive three strategies to improve dynamic balance.

Reaching beyond the base in all directions; stepping and walking with changes of speed, direction and surface; perturbation (reactive) training; also dual-task walking and tai chi.

7 mHow do you evaluate the sensory and motor strategies of balance?

Sensory: mCTSIB in four conditions (firm and foam, eyes open and closed); Romberg and sharpened Romberg; posturography if available. Motor: push and release or nudge tests in each direction (observe ankle, hip, stepping); functional and multidirectional reach for limits of stability; anticipatory control (rising on toes, reaching); BESTest or Mini-BESTest; function and fall risk (Berg, TUG, DGI).

LAQA 70-year-old man with peripheral neuropathy has fallen twice in a month, mostly at night. Plan the assessment and balance training. (Practice case)

Assessment: history of falls; sensation; mCTSIB (expect falls with eyes closed); Romberg; Berg, TUG, functional reach; strength and ankle range; vision; home hazards and lighting. Training: static progression with vision first, then reduced vision; stepping and reactive training; walking on varied surfaces; ankle strength; more than 3 hours a week of challenging balance exercise; night lights, a stick, footwear; foot care.
