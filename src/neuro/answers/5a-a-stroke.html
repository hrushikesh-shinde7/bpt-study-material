Types of stroke

Stroke (WHO): rapidly developing signs of focal disturbance of brain function lasting more than 24 hours or leading to death, with no cause other than vascular.

Anterior cerebral artery syndrome

The ACA supplies the medial surface of the frontal and parietal lobes, including the leg area of the motor and sensory cortex. Occlusion gives, on the opposite side:

  • Weakness and sensory loss of the leg more than the arm (face spared).
  • Urinary incontinence.
  • Abulia (apathy, slowed thinking), personality change; grasp reflex.
  • Difficulty starting gait (gait apraxia).

Contrast: MCA gives face and arm more than leg, with aphasia or neglect.

Circle of Willis with a diagram

The circle of Willis is a ring of arteries at the base of the brain, around the optic chiasma and pituitary, joining the two internal carotid systems and the vertebrobasilar system.

Vessels

  • Front: right and left anterior cerebral arteries (ACA) joined by the anterior communicating artery (ACoA).
  • Sides: each internal carotid (ICA) divides into the ACA and the middle cerebral artery (MCA); the posterior communicating artery (PCoA) joins the ICA to the PCA.
  • Back: two vertebral arteries form the basilar artery, which divides into the two posterior cerebral arteries (PCA).

Supply

  • Carotid (anterior) circulation: most of the cerebral hemispheres (ACA medial surface and leg area; MCA lateral surface, face and arm area, speech areas).
  • Vertebrobasilar (posterior) circulation: brainstem, cerebellum, occipital lobes, thalamus.

Function and clinical importance

  • A safety connection (collateral) between right and left and between front and back: if one artery narrows slowly, blood crosses over.
  • Often incomplete or variable, so protection differs between people.
  • Most berry aneurysms causing subarachnoid haemorrhage arise at its junctions, especially the ACoA.
  • Occlusion of each branch gives a typical syndrome: MCA (face and arm, aphasia or neglect), ACA (leg, incontinence), PCA (hemianopia, memory).

Flexion synergy of the spastic upper limb

A synergy is a fixed, stereotyped pattern in which muscles contract together and cannot act alone, seen in Brunnstrom stages 2–3 after stroke.

PartComponent
ScapulaRetraction and elevation
ShoulderAbduction and external rotation
ElbowFlexion (the strongest component)
ForearmSupination
Wrist and fingersFlexion

The typical posture mixes the strongest parts of both synergies: elbow flexion (flexion synergy) with shoulder adduction and internal rotation (extension synergy).

Pusher syndrome

  • Definition: the patient pushes with the non-paretic arm and leg towards the paretic side and resists correction, so falls towards the weak side in sitting and standing.
  • Cause: a disturbed sense of upright body posture, often after right-hemisphere or thalamic lesions; graded with the Scale for Contraversive Pushing.
  • Treatment: use vision to show what is vertical (mirror, door frame, therapist's arm); reach towards the strong side; sit or stand with the strong side against a wall or high table to remove the pushing surface. Do not force the patient upright.

Physiotherapy in the acute stage of CVA

Aims: prevent complications (chest infection, pressure injury, DVT, shoulder pain, contracture), start early functional movement, educate the family.

Timing: begin once medically stable, with short, frequent sessions. AVERT (2015): very early, high-dose out-of-bed activity in the first 24 hours worsened outcome.

Physiotherapy of the hemiplegic shoulder

Problems

  • Inferior subluxation in the flaccid stage: the weight of the arm stretches the capsule (gap below the acromion, measured in finger-breadths).
  • Pain (impingement, poor handling, spasticity).
  • Spasticity of internal rotators and adductors; loss of external rotation.
  • Shoulder-hand syndrome (CRPS): painful shoulder with a swollen, discoloured hand.

Prevention

  • Support the arm on a pillow, arm trough or lap tray in sitting; correct positioning in bed.
  • Handle with the scapula moved forward; never pull on the arm in transfers; teach staff and family.

Treatment

  • ROM within the pain-free range, scapula mobilised first (protraction, upward rotation) before elevating the arm.
  • Weight-bearing through the arm in sitting; active-assisted then task practice as movement returns.
  • Electrical stimulation of supraspinatus and posterior deltoid for subluxation.
  • Botulinum toxin for a spastic, painful shoulder, followed by stretching.
  • Shoulder-hand syndrome: elevation, gentle active movement, desensitisation; refer early.

Avoid overhead pulleys: the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline rates them harmful because they increase shoulder pain.

Hemiplegic (spastic) gait: features and physiotherapy

PhaseWhat goes wrongMain reason
StanceShort stance on the weak leg; knee hyperextends or stays bent; trunk leans; little hip extension at the end of stance; weak push-offWeak hip extensors and quadriceps; spastic or tight calf; poor balance
SwingStiff knee; foot drop and inversion; circumduction or hip hikingWeak hip flexors and dorsiflexors; spastic quadriceps and calf (extensor synergy)
OverallSlow, short, asymmetric steps; arm held flexed; high energy costWeakness, spasticity, poor selective control, fear

Physiotherapy

  1. Treat the cause: strengthen hip extensors, quadriceps and dorsiflexors; stretch the calf; reduce tone before practice.
  2. Practise the parts: weight shift onto the weak leg, step-ups, stepping forwards and backwards, knee control in small ranges, hip extension in late stance.
  3. Practise the whole: repetitive overground walking and treadmill training (with or without body-weight support), faster as able.
  4. Foot drop: AFO or peroneal nerve stimulation; walking aid only as needed.
  5. Progress: outdoors, slopes, obstacles, dual tasks; measure with the 10-metre walk and 6-minute walk.

Through the phases of recovery

  • Flaccid: standing and stepping in parallel bars with knee support.
  • Synergy: walking dominated by extensor synergy, so break it: knee bending with the hip extended, dorsiflexion with the knee straight.
  • Selective control: faster, more symmetrical gait; training moves to the community.

Physiotherapy of the spastic hand

After stroke the wrist and finger flexors become spastic, so the hand stays fisted; grasp is possible but release is not, and contracture and hygiene problems follow.

Assess: MAS of wrist and finger flexors, passive range, active extension, sensation, Brunnstrom hand stage.

  • Stretch and position: slow, sustained stretch of wrist and finger flexors; weight-bearing through the open hand in sitting.
  • Reduce tone before practice: slow rhythmic rotation of trunk and shoulder; prolonged icing; reflex-inhibiting positions.
  • Active practice: grasp and release of objects of different sizes; task practice with many repetitions.
  • Electrical stimulation of wrist and finger extensors; mirror therapy; CIMT if at least 20° active wrist and 10° finger extension.
  • Botulinum toxin to finger and wrist flexors, followed by stretching and task practice.
  • Splints: resting splints do not prevent spasticity (AHA/ASA 2026); use only for a specific reason (developing contracture, hygiene) and check the skin.

Home programme: daily self-stretch with the strong hand, weight-bearing on the open hand, and using the hand as a helper.

Three techniques for the spastic hand

  1. Slow sustained stretch and weight-bearing through the open hand (wrist and fingers extended) in sitting.
  2. Prolonged icing or slow rhythmic rotation to reduce tone, followed at once by active grasp and release practice.
  3. Electrical stimulation of wrist and finger extensors (or mirror therapy, CIMT when eligible).

Botulinum toxin plus therapy for marked focal spasticity.

Physiotherapy for the flaccid upper limb after stroke

In the flaccid stage (Brunnstrom 1) there is no tone and no voluntary movement; the risks are shoulder subluxation, pain, oedema and learned non-use.

  • Protect the shoulder: arm trough or lap tray in sitting, positioning in bed, handle with the scapula forward, never pull on the arm.
  • Facilitate activity (Rood): quick stretch, tapping, fast brushing and vibration over the muscles to be activated (triceps, wrist extensors, deltoid).
  • Weight-bearing through the extended arm in sitting (approximation).
  • Placing and holding the arm in different positions.
  • Electrical stimulation of supraspinatus, deltoid and wrist extensors.
  • Mirror therapy and bilateral arm training.
  • Passive then active-assisted, then active task practice as the first movements return; keep full pain-free range.
  • Oedema: elevation, active movement of the hand.

EPOS: some finger extension and shoulder abduction within 72 hours predicts arm recovery.

Motor Relearning Programme for the hemiplegic upper limb

Carr and Shepherd: a task-oriented approach based on motor learning. The patient is an active learner practising real tasks; muscle activity is trained in its functional context.

Four steps

  1. Analyse the task: observe reach, grasp and manipulation; find the missing components and compensations.
  2. Practise the missing components: forward reach with elbow extension; wrist extension while grasping; opening the hand to release; thumb opposition; supination and pronation.
  3. Practise the whole task: reach, grasp, move and release a glass; pick up and place objects.
  4. Transfer into daily life with practice outside therapy time (written programme, family supervision).

Principles

  • Clear goal, instruction and demonstration; feedback faded over time.
  • Many repetitions; avoid unnecessary muscle activity; do not let the strong hand take over.
  • Prevent soft-tissue shortening and learned non-use.
