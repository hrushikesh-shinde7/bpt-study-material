Signs and symptoms of Bell's palsy

Acute, one-sided LMN facial palsy of unknown cause, developing over less than 72 hours.

  • Weakness of the whole half of the face: cannot raise the eyebrow or wrinkle the forehead; eye does not close (Bell's phenomenon visible); flat nasolabial fold; mouth droops and pulls to the normal side.
  • Food collects in the cheek; drinks dribble; unclear lip sounds (p, b, m).
  • Pain behind the ear; watering or dry eye; loss of taste (front two-thirds of tongue); hyperacusis.

Bell's phenomenon

  • Definition: when the patient tries to close the eyes, the eyeball on the weak side rolls upwards and outwards.
  • It is a normal protective reflex that becomes visible in LMN facial palsy because the eyelid (orbicularis oculi) cannot close over it.
  • Importance: shows incomplete eye closure, so the cornea is at risk: eye drops, ointment, taping at night, glasses. A poor Bell's phenomenon means greater corneal risk.

Facial synkinesis

  • Definition: involuntary movement of one part of the face during voluntary movement of another (the eye narrows on smiling; the mouth corner lifts on blinking).
  • Cause: misdirected regrowth of facial nerve axons after severe (axonal) palsy; appears months later. "Crocodile tears" (tears while eating) are a similar misdirection.
  • Management: slow, small, isolated, symmetrical movements with a mirror or EMG biofeedback (neuromuscular retraining); relax the unwanted muscle; massage and stretching; avoid forceful mass movements and gum chewing; botulinum toxin by the doctor.

Bell's (facial) palsy: features and physiotherapy, with education and advice

Bell's palsy: acute, one-sided LMN facial palsy, probably viral inflammation swelling the nerve in its bony canal. Features: whole half of the face weak (forehead included), eye does not close with Bell's phenomenon, mouth droop, dribbling, loss of taste, hyperacusis.

Medical: oral steroids within 72 hours (recovery 83% vs 64% at 3 months); eye protection; refer if no recovery by 3 months.

Physiotherapy by stage

  • Acute (paralysed): reassurance; eye care; gentle upward massage towards the eye and ear; moist warmth with the eye protected; cheek support taping.
  • Recovering: active-assisted then active mirror exercises for each group: raise eyebrows, close eyes gently, smile, purse lips, puff cheeks, flare nostrils. Slow, small, symmetrical; hold the strong side back.
  • Late: neuromuscular retraining with mirror or EMG biofeedback for synkinesis; stretch and massage tight muscles.

Education and advice

  • Most recover in weeks to months.
  • Eye: drops by day, ointment and taping at night, glasses outdoors; report pain or redness.
  • Chew on the strong side; clear food from the cheek; sip through a straw.
  • Exercise gently several times a day; avoid forceful grimacing and gum chewing.

Electrical stimulation shows no benefit over placebo (Cochrane 2011) and may encourage mass movement.

Clinical assessment of the trigeminal nerve

  • Sensory: light touch and pinprick over the forehead (V1 ophthalmic), cheek (V2 maxillary) and jaw (V3 mandibular), comparing sides.
  • Motor: clench the teeth and feel masseter and temporalis; open the mouth against resistance: jaw deviates to the weak side (pterygoids).
  • Reflexes: corneal reflex (V1 afferent, facial efferent); jaw jerk (brisk in UMN lesions above the pons).

Trigeminal neuralgia: features and physiotherapy

Features

  • Sudden, severe, electric shock-like pain on one side of the face, usually V2 or V3, lasting seconds to 2 minutes, in repeated attacks.
  • Triggered by light touch, talking, chewing, brushing teeth, shaving or cold wind (trigger zones).
  • Normal sensation between attacks; weight loss and poor oral hygiene from avoiding triggers.
  • Cause: usually a blood vessel compressing the root; MS or tumour in young people or with sensory loss.

Medical: carbamazepine (first choice) or oxcarbazepine; microvascular decompression if drugs fail.

Physiotherapy (supportive)

  • Education on avoiding triggers: scarf in cold wind, lukewarm water, soft toothbrush, chew on the other side.
  • Relaxation and stress management; posture and jaw relaxation.
  • TENS may be tried away from trigger zones.
  • Encourage nutrition and oral hygiene.
  • Do not stimulate trigger zones directly.
