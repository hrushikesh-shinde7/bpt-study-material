Three Rood techniques to manage spasticity

  1. Slow stroking down the back over the spinal muscles for 3–5 minutes (calming sensory input).
  2. Neutral warmth: wrap the part or body in a blanket for 10–20 minutes.
  3. Prolonged (maintained) stretch of the spastic muscle.

Others: maintained pressure over the tendon insertion; slow rhythmic rocking; light joint compression; prolonged cold. Effects are short-lived: follow at once with active practice.

Three principles of Rood's approach

  1. Normalise tone and evoke the wanted response with the right sensory stimulus: some stimuli facilitate (fast brushing, quick icing, tapping), others inhibit (slow stroking, neutral warmth).
  2. Sensorimotor control develops in a developmental (ontogenetic) sequence: supine withdrawal, roll over, pivot prone, neck co-contraction, prone on elbows, all fours, standing, walking; levels mobility → stability → controlled mobility → skill.
  3. Movement is purposeful and needs repetition to be learned.

Principles and techniques of Rood's approach

Principles

  • Normalise tone and evoke the wanted motor response with appropriate sensory stimulation.
  • Follow the ontogenetic motor sequence: supine withdrawal → roll over → pivot prone → neck co-contraction → prone on elbows → all fours → standing → walking.
  • Levels of control: mobility, stability, controlled mobility, skill.
  • Purposeful activity calls out the response automatically; repetition is needed for learning.

Facilitatory techniques (hypotonia)

  • Fast brushing over the skin of the muscle.
  • Quick icing (brief strokes of ice).
  • Quick stretch, tapping over the muscle belly or tendon.
  • High-frequency vibration.
  • Heavy joint compression for co-contraction; resistance.
  • Fast vestibular stimulation (fast rocking, spinning).

Inhibitory techniques (spasticity)

  • Slow stroking down the back for 3–5 minutes.
  • Neutral warmth (blanket wrap) for 10–20 minutes.
  • Prolonged stretch; maintained tendon pressure.
  • Light joint compression; slow rhythmic rocking; slow vestibular input; prolonged cold.

Effects are short-lived and evidence is limited: use them to prepare for active, task-specific practice (Cochrane 2014: no approach is superior).

Three principles of Bobath (NDT)

  1. Inhibit abnormal tone and abnormal postural reflexes, which block normal movement (reflex-inhibiting patterns).
  2. Facilitate normal automatic postural reactions (righting, equilibrium, protective) and normal movement patterns through handling at key points of control (head, shoulder girdle, pelvis; hands and feet).
  3. Treat the person as a whole and include the affected side in all activity (bilateral, symmetrical).

Modern Bobath is a problem-solving approach for postural control and selective movement; not superior to other approaches after stroke.

Reflex-inhibiting patterns with an example

Reflex-inhibiting patterns (Bobath): positions or movements opposite to the abnormal spastic pattern, used to reduce tone before facilitating movement.

  • Hemiplegic arm (flexor pattern): shoulder forward and externally rotated, elbow extended, forearm supinated, wrist and fingers extended, thumb abducted; for example, sitting with weight on the extended arm beside or behind the body.
  • Hemiplegic leg (extensor pattern): hip and knee flexed, hip abducted and externally rotated, ankle dorsiflexed.
  • Child with extensor thrust: flexed position (head, trunk, hips, knees bent) in side lying or carried in flexion.

Three principles of the Motor Relearning Programme

  1. Task-specific practice: recovery is relearning of real-life tasks, practised in the right context.
  2. Active learner: the therapist teaches with instruction, demonstration and feedback; eliminate unnecessary muscle activity and practise the missing components.
  3. Practice and carry-over: many repetitions, continued on the ward and at home.

Four steps: analyse the task → practise missing components → practise the whole task → transfer to daily life (Carr and Shepherd).

Principles of proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF)

Philosophy: a positive approach that builds on what the person can do, works on function, uses strong parts to help the weak, treats the whole person and uses motor learning.

Basic principles (procedures)

  • Resistance: optimal, to facilitate contraction and learning.
  • Irradiation and reinforcement: strong muscles spread activity to weak ones in the pattern.
  • Manual contact: lumbrical grip over the working muscles guides direction.
  • Body position and mechanics of the therapist in line with the movement.
  • Verbal commands: short, clear, well timed; vision: the patient watches.
  • Traction (facilitates movement) and approximation (facilitates stability).
  • Stretch stimulus and stretch reflex.
  • Timing: normal distal-to-proximal; timing for emphasis.
  • Patterns: diagonal, spiral mass movements (D1 and D2 flexion and extension).

Neurophysiological basis (Sherrington): after-discharge, temporal and spatial summation, irradiation, successive induction, reciprocal innervation.

Techniques: rhythmic initiation, combination of isotonics, repeated stretch; dynamic and stabilising reversals, rhythmic stabilisation; contract-relax, hold-relax.

Upper limb D2 flexion ("drawing a sword") and extension ("sheathing the sword").
