Three functions of the basal ganglia

  1. Select and start the wanted movement and suppress unwanted ones.
  2. Scale the size, speed and force of movement.
  3. Run well-learned movements automatically and in sequence (walking, writing, turning in bed).

Also: motor learning and habits; loops for thinking, motivation and mood. Loss of nigral dopamine (PD) gives slow, small movements and loss of automaticity; other lesions give chorea, athetosis or dystonia.

Cardinal features of Parkinson's disease

Memory hook TRAP:

  • Tremor at rest, 4–6 per second, "pill-rolling"; starts in one hand; less with movement, absent in sleep.
  • Rigidity: lead-pipe, or cogwheel when tremor is added; flexors and extensors equally.
  • Akinesia/bradykinesia: slowness, difficulty starting, movements shrink as they repeat; mask face, soft voice, micrographia.
  • Postural instability: poor balance reactions, a later feature; falls backwards on the pull test.

MDS (2015): diagnosis needs bradykinesia plus rest tremor or rigidity.

Rigidity and its types

Rigidity: increased resistance to passive movement, present through the whole range, in flexors and extensors equally, independent of speed; an extrapyramidal sign.

  • Lead-pipe: smooth, even resistance throughout the range (PD).
  • Cogwheel: jerky, ratchet-like resistance: tremor superimposed on lead-pipe (PD).
  • Decorticate: arms flexed, legs extended; lesion above the red nucleus.
  • Decerebrate: arms and legs extended, arms internally rotated; brainstem lesion (worse prognosis).

Parkinsonian (festinating) gait: abnormalities and gait training

Gait abnormalities

  • Flexed posture: head forward, rounded shoulders, hips and knees bent.
  • Short, shuffling steps, flat foot (no heel strike), poor clearance.
  • Festination: the line of gravity falls in front of the feet, so steps become shorter and faster as if chasing it; propulsion and retropulsion.
  • Reduced arm swing and trunk rotation; turning en bloc.
  • Start hesitation and freezing at doorways, turns, narrow spaces, or with a second task.

Gait training

  • External cues: floor stripes at target step length; metronome or music; verbal cue "big step". Cues bypass faulty basal ganglia timing.
  • Attention on long steps and heel strike; no dual tasks early.
  • Practise long steps with arm swing; treadmill; Nordic poles; wide-arc turns; doorways; stops and starts; backwards and sideways.
  • Festination: stop, stand tall, restart with a long step; widen the base.
  • Freezing: stand tall, shift weight, step over a line or mark, count or march on the spot.
  • Trunk extension and rotation; stretch hip flexors and hamstrings.
  • Dose: 20–60 min, 3–5 days a week, 4–12 weeks (APTA 2022); in the "on" period.

Physiotherapy for bradykinesia and rigidity in Parkinson's disease

Bradykinesia: slowness and progressively smaller movements (hypokinesia), difficulty starting. Rigidity: lead-pipe or cogwheel resistance in flexors and extensors, causing a stiff flexed trunk.

Bradykinesia

  • Large-amplitude training (LSVT BIG: 16 one-hour sessions, 4 days a week for 4 weeks): whole-body movements at maximal size.
  • Speed and power training (40% 1RM fast).
  • External cues (visual, auditory) and cognitive movement strategies: break tasks into steps, rehearse mentally, perform each step consciously (sit to stand: "nose over toes, push up, stand tall").
  • Aerobic exercise: 3 days a week, 30–40 min, 60–85% HRmax.

Rigidity

  • Slow rhythmic rotation of the trunk and limbs.
  • Rhythmic initiation (PNF): passive → active-assisted → active.
  • Large reciprocal movements (arm swing, cycling); trunk extension and rotation.
  • Flexibility for pectorals, hip flexors and hamstrings; prone lying if tolerated.
  • Relaxation; warm-up before exercise; chest expansion exercises.

Train in the "on" period; monitor with RPE (heart rate response may be blunted).

Physiotherapy for Parkinson's disease at Hoehn and Yahr stage III

Stage III: both sides affected, mild to moderate disability, postural instability present (falls begin), still physically independent.

Aims: prevent falls, keep gait and transfers independent, maintain fitness and posture, keep social roles.

  • Balance and fall prevention: multimodal balance (reactive steps, weight shifts, reaching, turning, dual tasks when safe) 2–3 times a week, 16–30 hours over 5–10 weeks.
  • Gait training with cues: floor stripes, metronome, "big step"; wide-arc turns; strategies for freezing.
  • Cognitive movement strategies for sit to stand, turning in bed, getting up from the floor.
  • Aerobic: treadmill, cycling, brisk walking, 3 days a week, 30–40 min, moderate to high intensity.
  • Resistance: legs and trunk extensors, 1–2 days a week; power training.
  • Posture: trunk extension and rotation, chest expansion, flexibility.
  • Home safety: remove rugs, grab bars, raised toilet, good lighting, stripes at freezing spots.
  • Community classes (dance, tai chi) and behaviour change for lifelong exercise.

Train in the "on" period; check lying and standing BP; guard with a gait belt.
