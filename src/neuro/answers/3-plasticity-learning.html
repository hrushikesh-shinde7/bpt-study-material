Neuroplasticity: definition, brain changes and synaptogenesis

Neuroplasticity is the ability of the nervous system to change its structure and function in response to experience, learning, training or injury. It can be adaptive (recovery) or maladaptive (spasticity, learned non-use).

  • Synapse: stronger or weaker transmission (long-term potentiation, depression); unmasking of silent synapses.
  • Neuron: synaptogenesis (new synapses), new dendritic spines, axonal sprouting from intact neurons into areas that lost input.
  • Cortical maps: trained areas enlarge; areas next to a lesion take over lost functions.

Synaptogenesis is the formation of new synapses: fastest in early development, continues with learning, and after injury intact neurons sprout new synapses (reactive synaptogenesis) that practice can shape. Principles: use it or lose it, specificity, repetition, intensity, salience (Kleim and Jones, 2008).

Three factors that facilitate motor learning

  1. Practice: many repetitions; distributed rather than massed; variable and random practice for retention and transfer; whole-task practice where possible.
  2. Feedback: the right type and amount (knowledge of results and of performance), more early and faded later to avoid dependence.
  3. Motivation and attention: meaningful, task-specific goals in a real-life context; an external focus ("reach the cup") rather than on the body part.

Also: demonstration and mental practice, sleep and rest, a stimulating environment.

Motor learning and its stages

Motor learning is a set of processes linked to practice or experience that leads to relatively permanent changes in the capability for skilled movement, shown by retention and transfer.

Extrinsic and intrinsic feedback

  • Intrinsic feedback: information from the person's own senses during and after movement: proprioception, vision, vestibular, touch (feeling the heel strike, seeing the cup move).
  • Extrinsic (augmented) feedback: added from outside to supplement intrinsic feedback: therapist's words, mirror, video, EMG biofeedback, force platforms. It is knowledge of results (outcome: "10 m in 12 s") or knowledge of performance (pattern: "your knee locked back").

In neurological patients extrinsic feedback replaces lost intrinsic feedback (vision for poor proprioception) and is faded as learning progresses.

Role and types of feedback in neuro-rehabilitation

Feedback is information about a movement that the learner uses to correct errors and learn the skill.

Concurrent or terminalDuring the movement (mirror) or after it (verbal)
Immediate or delayed; summary, faded, bandwidthSchedules that reduce dependence: feedback after several trials, less over time, or only when error exceeds a band

Role

  • Information to detect and correct errors.
  • Motivation and reinforcement of correct movement.
  • Substitution for lost intrinsic feedback: vision for proprioceptive loss, EMG biofeedback for a flicker of activity, mirror therapy.

Rules

  • More feedback in the cognitive stage; fade it so the patient relies on intrinsic feedback (too much creates dependence and poor retention).
  • Give KP early, KR later; keep it short with an external focus.

Example: sit-to-stand after stroke: mirror and "nose over toes" (KP) early; number of stands in 30 seconds (KR) later.
