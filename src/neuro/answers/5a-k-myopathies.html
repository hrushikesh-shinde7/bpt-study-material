Three features of myasthenia gravis

Autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction: antibodies block ACh receptors.

  1. Fatigable weakness: worse with repeated use and towards evening, better after rest; proximal limbs and neck.
  2. Ocular: ptosis and diplopia (commonest first symptom).
  3. Bulbar: slurred or nasal speech, difficulty chewing and swallowing, "snarling" smile; breathing can fail (myasthenic crisis).

Reflexes, sensation and bladder are normal; wasting unusual.

Physiotherapy management of myasthenia gravis

MG: antibodies against ACh receptors at the neuromuscular junction cause fatigable weakness: ptosis, diplopia, bulbar, neck, proximal limb and breathing muscles.

Aims: keep strength and function, protect breathing, manage fatigue, educate about crisis.

  • Exercise (stable MG): moderate resistance and aerobic training, started low and built up; short sets with rests; aim for 150 min a week of activity; no exhaustion (Gilhus 2021).
  • Timing: about 1–2 hours after pyridostigmine, earlier in the day, in a cool room.
  • Breathing: inspiratory muscle training (3 times a week for 8 weeks, 20% rising to 60% of MIP) with breathing retraining; huffing, assisted cough, positioning.
  • Fatigue: energy conservation: plan, prioritise, pace; sit for tasks.
  • Aids: soft collar for head drop; eyelid support or alternating eye patch; fall-proof home.
  • After thymectomy: breathing exercises, early walking, sternal precautions.
  • Education: triggers (infection, heat, stress, some drugs) and signs of crisis.

No exercise during a flare, infection or crisis; avoid heat; stop and call the team if breathing, speech or swallowing worsen.
