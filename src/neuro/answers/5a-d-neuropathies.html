Diabetic neuropathy: classification; physiotherapy and care of the diabetic foot

Classification (ADA, 2017)

  • Diffuse: distal symmetric polyneuropathy (commonest, "glove and stocking"); autonomic neuropathy (heart, BP, gut, bladder, sweating).
  • Mononeuropathy: single cranial or limb nerve (third nerve, median, peroneal); mononeuritis multiplex.
  • Radiculopathy: lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy (diabetic amyotrophy); thoracic radiculopathy.

Diabetic foot = neuropathy + peripheral artery disease + deformity → ulcer, infection, amputation. Loss of protective sensation, weak intrinsics (clawed toes, high pressure under metatarsal heads), dry cracked skin.

Assessment

10 g monofilament, 128 Hz tuning fork, foot pulses and ABI, deformity, skin and nails, balance; IWGDF risk category (0 very low to 3 high: screen yearly to every 1–3 months).

Physiotherapy and foot care

  • Daily inspection with a mirror; wash, dry between toes, moisturise (not between toes); nail and callus care.
  • Protective footwear that fits; no barefoot walking; check shoes before wearing.
  • Foot-ankle exercises: toe flexion and extension, ankle range and strength.
  • Balance training with vision, then varied surfaces; good home lighting.
  • Graded walking in proper footwear (extra 1000 steps a day); cycling or swimming if an ulcer is present.
  • Ulcer: offloading (total contact cast, non-removable walker), crutches or wheelchair.
  • Burning pain: graded activity, TENS trial.

Precautions: no heat or ice over numb feet; check feet after exercise; check sugar before exercise; a hot, swollen painless foot may be Charcot foot: refer the same day.

Leprous neuritis; features of lepromatous leprosy

Leprosy: chronic infection with Mycobacterium leprae affecting skin and peripheral nerves. Spectrum (Ridley–Jopling): tuberculoid → borderline → lepromatous.

Lepromatous leprosy

  • Many symmetrical skin lesions and nodules; thickened facial skin (leonine face); loss of eyebrows; collapsed nose.
  • Glove-and-stocking anaesthesia; symmetrical nerve thickening; eye damage.
  • Skin smears strongly positive (multibacillary).

Leprous neuritis

  • A painful, tender, swollen nerve with new loss of sensation or power, often during a reaction (type 1 reversal; type 2 ENL). "Silent neuritis": loss without pain.
  • Nerves: ulnar (elbow), common peroneal (fibular neck), posterior tibial, median, facial, great auricular.
  • Deformities: claw hand, foot drop, clawed toes, plantar ulcers, lagophthalmos.

Management

  • WHO multidrug therapy (rifampicin, dapsone, clofazimine) 6 or 12 months; oral steroids for neuritis.
  • Physiotherapy: rest the limb in a splint during acute neuritis (no exercise of the inflamed nerve); then gentle range and graded exercise; monitor nerve function (voluntary muscle and monofilament testing) every visit; knuckle-bender splint for claw hand; AFO and microcellular rubber footwear; self-care "soak, scrape, oil"; padded tools and gloves; rehabilitation after tendon transfer; counselling against stigma.

Alcoholic neuropathy and its physiotherapy

Definition: a length-dependent, mainly axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy from long-term heavy drinking, worsened by poor nutrition (especially thiamine deficiency).

Features

  • Burning, painful feet; numbness in a glove-and-stocking pattern; lost ankle reflexes.
  • Sensory ataxia (worse in the dark; Romberg positive).
  • Distal weakness and wasting; foot drop in severe cases; autonomic problems.

Medical: stop alcohol; thiamine and vitamins; good diet; medicines for nerve pain. Recovery is slow and often partial.

Physiotherapy

  • Balance and ataxia: balance training with visual feedback; Frenkel's exercises; a stick; good lighting.
  • Weakness: progressive strengthening of ankle and hand muscles; AFO for foot drop.
  • Pain: graded activity, TENS trial, education.
  • Foot protection: daily inspection, proper footwear (as for the diabetic foot).
  • General fitness; support to stay off alcohol.

Precautions: withdrawal, low sugar, liver disease; protect numb skin from heat.

Physiotherapy for neuropathic pain

Definition (IASP): pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system.

  • Features: burning, shooting or electric pain; pins and needles; allodynia (pain from light touch); hyperalgesia; numbness in the same area.
  • Causes: diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, nerve injury, radiculopathy, SCI, stroke, MS, phantom limb.
  • Medicines (doctor): gabapentin or pregabalin, duloxetine, amitriptyline.

Physiotherapy

  • Pain education in plain words: hurt does not always mean harm; nerves become over-sensitive.
  • Graded activity and regular exercise with pacing (avoid boom-and-bust).
  • Desensitisation for allodynia: graded textures and pressure.
  • Sensory discrimination training; graded motor imagery and mirror therapy (CRPS, phantom pain).
  • TENS for short-term relief; relaxation, sleep advice.

Physiotherapy of reflex sympathetic dystrophy (CRPS)

CRPS: a painful limb condition, usually after injury, surgery or immobilisation (often a wrist fracture), with pain out of proportion to the injury and sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor, oedema, motor and trophic changes. Type I (RSD): no nerve injury; type II (causalgia): with nerve injury.

Budapest criteria: continuing disproportionate pain; symptoms in 3 of 4 and signs in 2 of 4 groups: sensory (allodynia), vasomotor (colour, temperature), sudomotor/oedema, motor/trophic (stiffness, weakness, hair and nail change); no other diagnosis.

Physiotherapy

  • Early, gentle movement and functional use; avoid prolonged immobilisation.
  • Graded motor imagery: left/right recognition → imagined movement → mirror therapy.
  • Desensitisation and tactile discrimination training.
  • Oedema: elevation, active pumping, retrograde massage or compression if tolerated.
  • Graded exercise and return to activity and work; pacing.
  • Team: medicines, psychology, occupational therapy.

Do not force painful passive stretching; never splint for long without movement.
