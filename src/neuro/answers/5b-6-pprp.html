What is PPRP?

  • Post-polio residual paralysis: the permanent weakness, wasting and deformity left once recovery from paralytic poliomyelitis has stopped, about 2 years after the illness (residual stage).
  • Poliovirus destroys anterior horn cells: asymmetrical flaccid paralysis, legs more than arms, lost reflexes, normal sensation and bladder.
  • Muscle imbalance and growth cause deformities (hip flexion-abduction-external rotation from a tight iliotibial band, knee flexion or recurvatum, equinus), shortening and a thin, cold limb.
  • Not the same as post-polio syndrome: new weakness and fatigue after at least 15 stable years.

Define PPRP; describe three deformities in detail

PPRP: permanent LMN paralysis, wasting and deformity remaining after recovery from paralytic polio stops (about 2 years after onset); asymmetrical, flaccid, sensation normal.

1. Hip flexion, abduction and external rotation

  • Cause: tight iliotibial band and tensor fasciae latae, from lying in the frog position during the acute illness.
  • Signs: positive Thomas and Ober tests; pelvic obliquity, compensatory lumbar scoliosis, apparent shortening.
  • Management: prone lying, stretching; soft-tissue release if fixed.

2. Genu recurvatum

  • Cause: weak quadriceps; the person locks the knee back for stability; equinus adds to it.
  • Management: leave mild recurvatum (it stabilises the knee); >15° needs a brace (KAFO) or osteotomy.

3. Equinus (equinovarus)

  • Cause: weak tibialis anterior with a working calf, plus gravity.
  • Signs: foot drop, high-stepping gait, tripping.
  • Management: calf stretching, AFO; tendon transfer (tibialis posterior) later.

Others: knee flexion, knee valgus with external tibial rotation, calcaneus foot, paralytic scoliosis, flail shoulder.

Physiotherapy management of post-polio residual paralysis

  • Assess: dated muscle chart (MRC 0–5), contractures (Thomas, Ober, popliteal angle), limb length, gait, function.
  • Strengthen: grade ≥3 muscles with progressive resistance (8–12 reps, 2–3 days a week, short of fatigue); grade 1–2 with assisted and gravity-eliminated work, hydrotherapy.
  • Stretch: iliotibial band, hip flexors, hamstrings, calf; daily prone lying; serial casts or night splints.
  • Orthoses: AFO for foot drop; floor-reaction orthosis (weak quadriceps, normal hip) or KAFO with drop-lock knee (weak hip too); shoe raise for shortening over 2 cm; crutches or stick.
  • Gait and function: walking with orthosis, stairs, transfers, daily activities.
  • Around surgery: strengthen the donor muscle (grade 4–5) before a tendon transfer; afterwards re-educate using the donor's old action with biofeedback; protect the transfer.
  • Education: home programme, skin checks under orthoses, pacing, signs of post-polio syndrome.

Do not stretch away a mild recurvatum or equinus that keeps a weak knee stable.

PPRP: clinical features and physiotherapy of its complications

PPRP: residual asymmetrical flaccid paralysis after polio; thin, short, cold limb; normal sensation; gaits: hand-on-thigh (weak quadriceps), Trendelenburg, gluteus maximus lurch, high-stepping, short-limb.
