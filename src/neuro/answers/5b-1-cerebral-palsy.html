Classification of cerebral palsy

  • By movement disorder: spastic (commonest), dyskinetic (athetoid, dystonic), ataxic, hypotonic, mixed.
  • By distribution (spastic): hemiplegia (arm more than leg), diplegia (legs much more than arms), quadriplegia (all four limbs and trunk).
  • By function: GMFCS levels I–V (walks without limitation → transported in a wheelchair); MACS I–V for hand use; CFCS for communication.

Classification and aetiology of cerebral palsy

CP: a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture, causing activity limitation, due to a non-progressive disturbance of the developing fetal or infant brain (Rosenbaum 2007); about 3 per 1000 in India.

Aetiology

  • Prenatal: maternal infection (TORCH), multiple pregnancy, placental insufficiency, pre-eclampsia, malformations, Rh incompatibility.
  • Perinatal: prematurity and low birth weight, birth asphyxia, prolonged labour, kernicterus, neonatal infection.
  • Postnatal: meningitis, encephalitis, head injury, near drowning, hypoglycaemia.

Three impairments in hypotonic cerebral palsy

  1. Low tone with weak antigravity muscles: poor head and trunk control; "frog-leg" lying posture.
  2. Hypermobile joints and poor co-contraction (unstable proximal joints).
  3. Very delayed milestones; low endurance.

Also: shallow breathing and weak cough; feeding difficulty. Often changes later to dyskinetic or ataxic CP.

Three spinal deviations in quadriplegic cerebral palsy

  1. Neuromuscular scoliosis: long C-shaped curve, often with pelvic obliquity.
  2. Kyphosis from poor trunk control in sitting.
  3. Increased lumbar lordosis with hip flexion contracture.

Often with windswept hips and hip displacement. Prevention: supportive seating, positioning, standing programme, hip and spine surveillance.

Posture and movement problems of a child with spastic diplegic CP

Spastic diplegia: legs much more affected than arms; often preterm with periventricular leukomalacia; intelligence often normal.

Posture

  • Hips flexed, adducted and internally rotated; knees flexed; ankles in equinus.
  • Sitting: on the sacrum with posterior pelvic tilt and rounded back (tight hamstrings), or W-sitting for stability.

Movement

  • Poor selective control of the legs; moves legs together (commando crawl, bunny hopping).
  • Standing and walking: scissoring, toe walking, crouch or jump-knee pattern, in-toeing (femoral anteversion); arms in high guard.
  • Poor standing balance; associated reactions in the arms with effort.

Secondary problems

  • Contractures of calf, hamstrings, hip flexors and adductors with growth; hip migration; torsional deformities.

Management in one line: stretching through casting and AFOs, strengthening, gait training with posterior walker and treadmill, botulinum toxin when indicated.

Posture and movement problems of a child with athetoid CP

Athetoid (dyskinetic) CP: basal ganglia damage from asphyxia at term or kernicterus.

Problems

  • Involuntary writhing movements that increase with effort, excitement and attempts to move.
  • Fluctuating tone from low to high.
  • Poor head and midline control; persistent ATNR pulls the body into asymmetry on head turning.
  • Poor proximal stability (little co-contraction): cannot hold positions, grades movement poorly; sudden extensor thrusts push the child out of the chair.
  • Drooling, unclear speech, feeding difficulty; hearing loss after kernicterus; intelligence often normal.
  • Walking, if achieved, is late, unsteady, wide-based with arms held high.

Physiotherapy approach

  • Proximal stability: weight-bearing (prone on elbows, four-point, kneeling, standing frame); approximation; holding against gentle resistance.
  • Midline control: head and hands in midline, symmetrical sitting, two-handed play.
  • Graded, slow, small-range movement; avoid excitement.
  • Stable seating with pelvic belt and foot support; walker or gait trainer when ready.

Physiotherapy for spastic cerebral palsy

Principles: family-centred, goal-directed, active task-specific practice through play; start early; home programme; prevent deformity (Novak 2019).

  • Assessment: GMFCS, GMFM, MAS and Tardieu, range (popliteal angle, Thomas, calf), selective control, gait, hips.
  • Range and contracture: active use, standing frame in hip abduction (60 min a day), serial casting, AFOs; botulinum toxin plus stretching and practice.
  • Strength: progressive training (sit to stand, step-ups), 2–4 times a week, 1–3 sets of 6–15, for 12–16 weeks.
  • Gait: standing balance and weight shift; parallel bars → posterior walker; treadmill with or without body-weight support; AFO for equinus, ground-reaction AFO for crouch.
  • Hand (hemiplegia): CIMT or bimanual training as play, about 40 hours in total.
  • Fitness: cycling, swimming, active play ≥20 min, 2–3 times a week.
  • Positioning and seating; avoid W-sitting; hip surveillance (refer if migration >30%).
  • Home programme and parent training; school adaptations.

Passive NDT does not improve motor function; handling only sets the child up for active movement.
