Sacral sparing

  • Definition: preservation of any function in the lowest sacral segments (S4–S5) after SCI: light touch or pinprick at the anal margin, deep anal pressure, or voluntary anal contraction.
  • Why it happens: sacral fibres lie on the outer edge of the long tracts, so they often survive a central lesion.
  • Importance: it shows some fibres cross the lesion, so the injury is incomplete (AIS B–D) with a better prognosis for recovery and walking. No sacral sparing = complete (AIS A).

Spinal shock and its features

Spinal shock is the temporary loss or depression of all spinal reflex activity below the level of an acute cord injury.

  • Flaccid paralysis and areflexia below the lesion, including the bulbocavernosus reflex; loss of sensation.
  • Atonic bladder with retention; paralytic ileus; loss of sweating and temperature control below the lesion.
  • Phases (Ditunno, 2004): areflexia (day 0–1); first reflexes return, bulbocavernosus first (days 1–3); early hyperreflexia (1–4 weeks); late hyperreflexia and spasticity (1–12 months).

Not neurogenic shock: that is low BP and bradycardia from loss of sympathetic tone in lesions at or above T6.

Autonomic dysreflexia (features)

Definition: a sudden rise in systolic BP of more than 20 mmHg above the usual level in SCI at or above T6; an emergency (stroke, seizure, death).

  • Cause: a harmful stimulus below the lesion, most often a full bladder (blocked catheter); also bowel impaction, pressure sore, ingrown toenail, tight clothes.
  • Features: sudden pounding headache; high BP; slow pulse; flushing and sweating above the lesion; goose bumps; blurred vision; blocked nose; anxiety.
  • First aid: sit upright, lower the legs, loosen clothes, check BP every 1–2 minutes, check the bladder first, then the bowel; call the doctor.

Autonomic dysreflexia: causes, symptoms and management

Definition: a sudden rise in systolic BP of more than 20 mmHg above the person's usual level (15 mmHg in children) in SCI at or above T6. A medical emergency.

Mechanism

  1. A noxious stimulus below the lesion sends impulses up the cord.
  2. A massive reflex sympathetic discharge narrows the splanchnic vessels (T6–L2, below the lesion): BP rises sharply.
  3. The brain slows the heart through the vagus and dilates vessels above the lesion (flushing, headache).
  4. Calming signals cannot pass down the cord, so BP stays high until the trigger is removed.

Causes: bladder distension (commonest: kinked or blocked catheter), bowel impaction, pressure injury, ingrown toenail, tight clothes or straps, fractures, menstruation, labour, sexual activity, procedures.

Symptoms and signs: pounding headache; high BP; bradycardia; flushing and sweating above the lesion; goose bumps; blurred vision; blocked nose; anxiety.

Management (in order)

  1. Sit upright, lower the legs; loosen clothes, straps and binders.
  2. Check BP and pulse every 1–2 minutes.
  3. Check the bladder first: catheterise or unblock the catheter.
  4. If systolic BP stays ≥150 mmHg, the doctor gives a fast, short-acting drug (nitrate paste, nifedipine).
  5. Check the bowel with anaesthetic gel; look for other triggers.
  6. Monitor for 2 hours; record the cause; educate; give a wallet card.

Prevention in physiotherapy: ask about the bladder before each session; no tight straps on tilt table or standing frame; know the usual BP (90–110 in tetraplegia); stop exercise or stimulation at once if headache starts.

Brown-Séquard syndrome: features

Hemisection of the cord (stab or bullet wound, tumour, disc).

  • Same side below the lesion: weakness with UMN signs (corticospinal tract); loss of position sense and vibration (dorsal columns).
  • Opposite side: loss of pain and temperature from 1–2 segments below (spinothalamic tract crosses).
  • At the level, same side: a band of total sensory loss and LMN signs.
  • Prognosis: the best of the cord syndromes; most walk again.

Cauda equina injury: features

Fracture or disc below L1–L2 vertebrae damages the nerve roots (not the cord), so the picture is LMN:

  • Flaccid, often asymmetrical leg weakness with wasting; lost knee and ankle reflexes.
  • Saddle anaesthesia; radicular (root) pain.
  • Areflexic (atonic) bladder and bowel; sexual dysfunction.
  • Better outlook than cord lesions: roots are peripheral nerves and can regenerate.

Ambulation training after an L3 vertebral fracture

An L3 fracture lies below the end of the cord (L1–L2), so it damages the cauda equina: LMN, often incomplete and uneven leg weakness, sensory loss and an areflexic bladder. Community walking is expected.

Before walking

  • Follow brace and lifting precautions; check skin and insensate feet.
  • Strengthen hip extensors, abductors and quadriceps (if partially working), and the upper limbs (shoulder depressors, latissimus, triceps) for crutches.
  • Keep range: hip flexors, hamstrings, calves.
  • Sitting and standing balance; tilt table or frame if needed.

Orthoses

  • AFOs for weak ankles (foot drop); KAFOs only if quadriceps are weak.

Gait training

  1. Parallel bars: sit to stand, standing balance, weight shift.
  2. Walker or elbow crutches: four-point gait, then two-point gait, then canes.
  3. Stairs, kerbs, slopes, rough ground; getting up from the floor.
  4. Endurance: 6-minute walk; wheelchair for long distances if needed.

Protect insensate feet (footwear, daily checks); teach safe lifting.
