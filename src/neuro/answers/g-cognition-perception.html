Figure-ground discrimination

The inability to pick out an object from its background (a visual perceptual disorder, usually after right parieto-occipital damage).

  • Examples: cannot find a white shirt on a white bed, the brake on a wheelchair, or a step edge on a patterned floor.
  • Assessment: find an item in a cluttered drawer or picture; Ayres' figure-ground test; observe daily tasks.
  • Physiotherapy: reduce clutter; use colour contrast (tape on step edges and brakes); teach a systematic search; practise in real settings.

Combined (cortical) sensations

Sensations that need intact primary sensation plus integration in the parietal cortex; tested with the eyes closed.

  • Stereognosis: recognising an object (coin, key) by touch.
  • Graphaesthesia: recognising a number drawn on the skin.
  • Two-point discrimination: telling one point from two (fingertip about 2–5 mm).
  • Tactile localisation; barognosis (weight); texture recognition.
  • Bilateral simultaneous stimulation: one side "disappears" in extinction.

Loss with intact primary sensation points to a parietal lesion (stroke); it limits hand use, so add sensory re-education.

Assessment of unilateral visual neglect

Neglect: failure to report, respond or orient to stimuli on the side opposite a brain lesion, not due to weakness or sensory loss; commonest after right parietal lesions (left neglect).

  • Line bisection: mark is shifted towards the good side.
  • Cancellation tests (star, letter, Albert's lines): targets missed on the neglected side.
  • Clock drawing, figure copying: one half missing.
  • Reading and writing: starts mid-line, drifts to one side.
  • Functional observation: eats half the plate, bumps door frames (Catherine Bergego Scale).

Tell it from hemianopia: a hemianopic patient knows of the loss and turns the head to compensate; a neglect patient does not.

Apraxia and its types

Apraxia: inability to carry out learned purposeful movements on command despite adequate strength, sensation, coordination, comprehension and willingness (left parietal or frontal lesion).

  • Ideomotor: cannot do it on command or by imitation, but does it automatically.
  • Ideational: loses the idea or sequence of a task (toothpaste on the comb).
  • Limb-kinetic: clumsy fine finger movements.
  • Constructional, dressing, buccofacial, gait apraxia.

Aphasia and its types; Broca's and Wernicke's aphasia

Aphasia: loss of language (speaking, understanding, reading, writing) from brain damage, usually left hemisphere; unlike dysarthria, which is a motor speech problem with normal language.

Apraxia is the inability to carry out learned, purposeful movements on command, even though strength, sensation, coordination, comprehension and willingness are adequate. It usually follows a left parietal or frontal lesion (stroke, head injury, dementia).

Assessment

Pantomime tool use ("show me how you brush your teeth"), imitation of gestures, real-object use, a multistep task (making tea); note wrong movement, body part as tool, wrong order, perseveration.

Physiotherapy

  • Practise real tasks with real objects in their natural setting.
  • Strategy training: talk through each step, picture or step cards.
  • Errorless learning and chaining (forward or backward); physical guidance rather than long verbal commands; fade cues.
  • Gesture training for ideomotor apraxia; involve the family.
