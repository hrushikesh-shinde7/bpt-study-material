Signs and symptoms of vestibular lesions; adaptation exercises

Signs and symptoms

  • Vertigo: false sense of spinning; dizziness.
  • Nystagmus: peripheral lesions give horizontal-torsional nystagmus beating away from the damaged side, reduced by fixing the gaze.
  • Imbalance: unsteady standing and walking, veering or falling to the damaged side.
  • Oscillopsia: the world jumps during head movement (VOR failure).
  • Nausea, vomiting, sweating; hearing loss, tinnitus (Ménière's, labyrinthitis); anxiety and avoidance of head movement.

Central signs (refer): direction-changing or vertical nystagmus, normal head impulse test, skew deviation, double vision, slurred speech, weakness.

Adaptation (gaze stabilisation) exercises

  • Principle: image slip on the retina during head movement drives the cerebellum to recalibrate the VOR (increase gain).
  • VOR ×1: eyes fixed on a stationary target (a letter at arm's length) while moving the head side to side, then up and down, for 1–2 minutes.
  • VOR ×2: target and head move in opposite directions while the eyes stay on the target.
  • Progress: speed, duration, busy background, sitting → standing → walking.
  • Dose (Hall 2022): acute one-sided loss 3 times a day, at least 12 minutes daily; chronic 3–5 times a day, at least 20 minutes, for 4–6 weeks. Mild symptoms during exercise are expected.

Tests for vestibular disorders (explain two)

Enumerate: head impulse test; dynamic visual acuity; Dix-Hallpike; supine roll test; head-shaking test; Romberg and sharpened Romberg; Fukuda stepping test; mCTSIB; Dizziness Handicap Inventory; Motion Sensitivity Quotient; Dynamic Gait Index; laboratory tests (caloric, video head impulse, VEMP).

1. Head impulse test (VOR)

  • The person fixes on the examiner's nose; the examiner turns the head quickly and unpredictably 10–20° to each side.
  • Positive: a catch-up saccade back to the target shows a weak VOR on the side the head was turned towards.

2. Dix-Hallpike test (posterior canal BPPV)

  • Sitting, head turned 45° to one side; lie back quickly with the neck extended about 20° below horizontal.
  • Positive: vertigo with upbeating, torsional nystagmus after a short delay, lasting under a minute: BPPV on the lower ear.

Other good choices: Fukuda (turning more than 45° in 50 steps towards the lesion); dynamic visual acuity (a drop of more than two lines with head movement).

BPPV: diagnostic test for the posterior canal and physiotherapy

BPPV: otoconia come loose from the utricle into a semicircular canal (posterior most often); head movement shifts them, causing brief vertigo (seconds) on lying down, turning in bed, looking up or bending; no hearing loss.

Diagnosis: Dix-Hallpike test

From sitting with the head turned 45° to one side, bring the person quickly to lying with the neck extended 20° (affected ear down). Positive: vertigo with torsional, upbeating nystagmus after a short latency, lasting under a minute. If negative, test the other side; if horizontal nystagmus, do the supine roll test (horizontal canal).

Epley manoeuvre (right posterior canal)

  1. Sit, head turned 45° to the right.
  2. Lie back quickly with the neck extended 20° (right Dix-Hallpike); wait until vertigo stops (≥30 s).
  3. Turn the head 90° to the left; wait 30 s.
  4. Roll onto the left side, head turned a further 90° (nose down); wait 30 s.
  5. Sit up with the chin slightly down; repeat Dix-Hallpike and repeat if positive.

Alternatives: Semont (liberatory) manoeuvre; barbecue roll or Gufoni for horizontal canal; Brandt–Daroff exercises at home for residual symptoms.

Advice (BPPV guideline, 2017): no post-manoeuvre postural restrictions; no routine suppressant drugs; reassess within a month; teach recurrence and fall risk.

The Dix-Hallpike position: head turned 45°, neck extended 20° over the couch end.

Vestibular rehabilitation: principles and manoeuvres

Principles

  • Offer exercise-based rehabilitation to adults with one- or two-sided vestibular hypofunction; start early (within 2 weeks of acute loss).
  • Recovery comes from adaptation (recalibrating the VOR), habituation (reducing response to provoking movements) and substitution (other senses and strategies).
  • Exercises should provoke mild symptoms; stop vestibular suppressants early (they slow compensation).
  • Supervised sessions plus a daily home programme; progress step by step; add balance and gait training.

Exercises

  • Gaze stabilisation: VOR ×1 and ×2, progressed in speed, background and position.
  • Habituation: repeat the movements that provoke mild dizziness (Motion Sensitivity Quotient), twice a day.
  • Balance and gait: narrowing base, foam, eyes closed; walking with head turns, pivots, dual tasks.
  • Dose: chronic one-sided loss: gaze 3–5 times a day (≥20 min) and balance ≥20 min daily, 4–6 weeks.

Two manoeuvres (BPPV)

  1. Epley: Dix-Hallpike position → head turned 90° to the other side → roll onto that side, nose down → sit up; about 30 s in each position.
  2. Semont: head turned away from the affected ear; move quickly onto the affected side (1 min), then swing through to the other side, nose down (1 min); sit up slowly.

Do not use eye exercises without head movement (classic Cawthorne–Cooksey start) to improve gaze stability.

Substitution and habituation exercises

Habituation

  • Principle: repeated, controlled exposure to movements or visual scenes that provoke mild dizziness reduces the response over time (central nervous system learns to ignore it).
  • Method: find provoking movements with the Motion Sensitivity Quotient (sit to supine, rolling, bending, head turns); choose 3–4 that give mild to moderate symptoms; repeat each a few times, twice a day, letting symptoms settle between repetitions.
  • Examples: Brandt–Daroff exercises; head turns while walking; optokinetic (busy visual) exposure.
  • Used for: motion-provoked dizziness, visual motion sensitivity, persistent postural-perceptual dizziness.

Substitution

  • Principle: replace lost vestibular function with vision, somatosensation and planned strategies, when the loss is bilateral or complete.
  • Methods: balance training that uses vision and feet (firm surfaces, then progressively harder); planned eye–head movements (look at the target first, then turn the head); central pre-programming of eye movements; use of the cervico-ocular reflex.
  • Strategies: a stick, night lights, avoid dark and uneven ground, turn slowly; fall prevention at home.

Third type, adaptation (VOR ×1, ×2), is for one-sided loss; most programmes combine all three with gait training.
