Types of spina bifida

  • Spina bifida occulta: gap in the bony arch only; skin closed (tuft of hair, dimple, fatty lump); usually no deficit.
  • Meningocele: skin-covered sac of meninges and CSF; cord stays in the canal; few deficits.
  • Myelomeningocele: cord and roots in the sac (often open); paralysis, sensory loss and neurogenic bladder below the level; commonest serious type.
  • Others: lipomyelomeningocele, myeloschisis.

Deformities in spina bifida

  • Spine: scoliosis, congenital kyphosis, lordosis.
  • Hip: flexion contracture; flexion–adduction with subluxation or dislocation (L3–L4); frog posture in high lesions.
  • Knee: flexion contracture (high lesions); valgus (low lumbar, sacral).
  • Foot: clubfoot, vertical talus, calcaneus (low lumbar), cavovarus and claw toes (sacral).
  • Rotation: femoral anteversion; tibial torsion.

Physiotherapy (5)

  • Assess motor and sensory level by muscle charting; record deformities and skin.
  • Positioning: daily prone lying for hip extension; hips in slight abduction; avoid frog posture.
  • Stretching: hip flexors and adductors, hamstrings, calf; gentle (thin bones).
  • Clubfoot: serial casting with the surgeon; then AFOs or night splints.
  • Standing frame from about 12 months for bones and hips; orthoses by level.
  • Strengthen working muscles and the trunk and arms.
  • Parent education: handling, stretches, skin checks; latex-free equipment.

Physiotherapy of (lumbar) myelomeningocele / spina bifida

Myelomeningocele: cord and roots in an open sac; flaccid paralysis and numb skin below the level, neurogenic bladder and bowel, hydrocephalus (Chiari II) in most.

By age

  • Newborn, before closure: prone or side lying to protect the sac; record which muscles move.
  • Infancy: positioning, gentle ROM, parent handling; head control, prone play, rolling, sitting, arm strength.
  • Toddler: standing frame from about 12 months; parapodium or RGO from about 2 years in high lesions.
  • School age: gait training with orthoses and crutches; wheelchair skills; fitness; self-care.

Lumbar level: key points

  • Strengthen working muscles (hip flexors, adductors, quadriceps, trunk) and the arms for crutches.
  • Prevent deformity: prone lying, stretch hip flexors and adductors, hamstrings, calf; night splints.
  • Orthoses by level: no quadriceps → RGO or HKAFO + walker; weak quadriceps → KAFO; strong quadriceps (L4) → AFOs + crutches; sacral → AFOs or none.
  • Gait: standing balance → walker → crutches (swing-through or four-point); stairs; falls.
  • Wheelchair for distances.

Latex-free equipment; skin checks twice a day; no heat on numb skin; refer for shunt signs or new weakness (tethered cord).

Hydrocephalus: types and clinical features

Types: non-communicating (obstructive: aqueduct stenosis, Chiari II, tumour) and communicating (not absorbed: after premature bleed or meningitis); congenital or acquired; normal-pressure in older adults.

Features in infants:

  • Head growing too fast (crossing centiles); bulging anterior fontanelle; widened sutures; prominent scalp veins.
  • "Setting-sun" eyes; irritability, vomiting, poor feeding, drowsiness, high-pitched cry.
  • Stiff legs, delayed milestones, seizures.

Older children: morning headache, vomiting, papilloedema. NPH: gait difficulty, memory loss, incontinence.

Physiotherapy management for hydrocephalus

Hydrocephalus: excess CSF in the ventricles under raised pressure; treated with a VP shunt or endoscopic third ventriculostomy. Child has a large, heavy head, poor head control and delayed milestones.

  • Handling: always support the heavy head and neck when lifting and carrying; teach parents.
  • Positioning: change head position every few hours (prevent scalp sores and flattening); avoid pressure over the shunt valve; supported side lying, prone on a wedge.
  • Development: head control (prone on elbows, supported sitting), rolling, sitting balance, reaching, standing in a frame; follow the developmental sequence.
  • Tone and range: stretch and position stiff legs; strengthen weak muscles.
  • Stimulation: sensory, visual and cognitive input through play.
  • Seating with head support.
  • Education: shunt warning signs: vomiting, drowsiness, irritability, bulging fontanelle, fast head growth, fever, headache: seek help at once.
