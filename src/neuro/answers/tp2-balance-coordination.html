Three motor strategies of balance

StrategyWhenHow
AnkleSmall, slow disturbances; firm, wide surfaceBody sways as one piece about the ankles; muscles fire distal to proximal
HipLarger, faster disturbances; narrow or soft surfaceQuick hip flexion or extension moves the centre of mass; proximal to distal
Stepping (reaching)Centre of mass moves outside the baseA rapid step or reach creates a new base of support

A suspensory strategy (bending the knees to lower the centre of mass) is sometimes added.

Three strategies to improve dynamic balance

  1. Reaching beyond arm's length in all directions, to the floor and overhead.
  2. Weight shifts and stepping: forward, backward, sideways, onto a step, over obstacles; turning.
  3. Reactive (perturbation) training: expected, then unexpected pushes or pulls to train ankle, hip and stepping responses.

Also: walking variations (speed, direction, head turns, dual tasks); tai chi, dance, ball games. Dose: more than 3 hours a week of challenging balance exercise reduces falls by 39%.

Training static and dynamic standing balance

Balance keeps the centre of mass over the base of support: static, dynamic and reactive. It needs vision, vestibular and somatosensory input, central integration, strength and range, and attention.

Static balance

Hold each position (for example 30 seconds) before progressing; equal weight on both legs with mirror or scale feedback.

Dynamic balance

  • Reaching in all directions; weight shifts; stepping and turning; step-ups; obstacles.
  • Perturbation training for ankle, hip and stepping responses.
  • Walking with speed and direction changes, head turns, dual tasks; tai chi, dance, ball games.

Dose: challenging balance exercise >3 hours a week (Sherrington 2017). Safety: gait belt, parallel bars, stand on the weaker side, clear the area.

Evaluating sensory and motor strategies of balance

Sensory strategies

  • mCTSIB: 30 seconds in four conditions: firm eyes open, firm eyes closed, foam eyes open, foam eyes closed. Falls on firm surface with eyes closed → somatosensory loss; falls on foam with eyes closed → vestibular loss or poor sensory reweighting.
  • Romberg and sharpened Romberg: loss of balance on eye closure → sensory (proprioceptive) ataxia.
  • Sensory Organisation Test on posturography (six conditions) where available.

Motor strategies

  • Reactive responses: push-and-release test or nudges in each direction; observe ankle, hip or stepping response and its speed. Pull test in Parkinson's.
  • Limits of stability and anticipatory control: functional reach (<15 cm = high fall risk); multidirectional reach; rising on toes.
  • Systems tools: BESTest or Mini-BESTest (biomechanical constraints, stability limits, anticipatory, reactive, sensory orientation, dynamic gait).

Function and fall risk

Berg Balance Scale (0–56), Timed Up and Go, Dynamic Gait Index or Functional Gait Assessment, dual-task walking.
