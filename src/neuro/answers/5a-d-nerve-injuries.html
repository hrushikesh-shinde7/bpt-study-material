Seddon has three types; Sunderland splits axonotmesis into degrees II–IV by how much connective tissue is cut.

Sunderland's classification; physiotherapy for foot drop (4 + 3)

Sunderland's classification (4)

  • Degree I (neurapraxia): conduction block, axon intact; full recovery in days to 3 months.
  • Degree II (axonotmesis): axon cut, endoneurium intact; Wallerian degeneration; full recovery at about 1 mm a day.
  • Degree III: axon and endoneurium cut, perineurium intact; incomplete recovery (misdirection).
  • Degree IV: only the epineurium intact (neuroma in continuity); needs excision and repair.
  • Degree V (neurotmesis): nerve divided; no recovery without surgery.

Physiotherapy for foot drop (3)

  • AFO (posterior leaf spring) to lift the foot in swing and prevent toe catching.
  • Daily calf stretching to prevent equinus; passive range of the ankle.
  • Re-education of dorsiflexors and evertors as recovery comes: biofeedback, electrical stimulation, gravity-eliminated then against gravity.
  • Gait training to replace the high-stepping pattern; protect numb skin on the top of the foot.
  • Tendon transfer (tibialis posterior) if no recovery.

Radial nerve injury (palsy): features and physiotherapy

Causes: fracture of the shaft of the humerus (spiral groove); "Saturday night palsy"; crutch pressure in the axilla.

Features (at the spiral groove)

  • Wrist drop, finger drop and thumb drop; triceps usually spared.
  • Weak grip because the wrist cannot be held extended.
  • Sensory loss over a small area on the back of the first web space.
  • Posterior interosseous lesion: finger and thumb drop only; wrist extends with radial deviation; no sensory loss.

Physiotherapy

  • Splints: static cock-up splint at night; dynamic cock-up splint by day for grasp and release.
  • Daily passive ROM of wrist and fingers; prevent shortening of the flexors and overstretching of the extensors.
  • Re-education as recovery comes (brachioradialis and wrist extensors first, then finger and thumb extensors): biofeedback or electrical stimulation, gravity-eliminated, then active and resisted.
  • Grip activities with the wrist supported; task and work practice.
  • Explain regrowth time (about 2.5 cm a month); tendon transfer if no recovery.

Claw hand and its physiotherapy

Claw hand: hyperextension of the MCP joints with flexion of the IP joints, because the lumbricals and interossei are paralysed: the long extensors pull the knuckles back and the long flexors bend the fingers, unopposed.

  • Ulnar lesion: ring and little fingers clawed; hypothenar and interossei wasting; Froment's sign; card test.
  • Ulnar paradox: lesion at the elbow claws less, because the long flexors to ring and little fingers are also weak.
  • Median + ulnar: all four fingers clawed with an ape-thumb.
  • Other causes: Klumpke's palsy, leprosy, Volkmann's contracture.

Physiotherapy

  • Knuckle-bender (anti-claw) splint: blocks MCP hyperextension so the long extensors straighten the IP joints.
  • Passive stretching of the MCP joints into flexion; keep the thumb web open.
  • Intrinsic re-education: finger spreading, "table-top" position, with biofeedback or electrical stimulation.
  • Protect the numb little finger; sensory re-education.
  • Grip and pinch practice; tendon transfer if no recovery.

Physiotherapy of carpal tunnel syndrome

CTS: compression of the median nerve under the flexor retinaculum: tingling and numbness of thumb, index, middle and half the ring finger, worse at night; later thenar wasting. Tests: Phalen's, Tinel's at the wrist, carpal compression.

  • Splint: neutral wrist splint at night for short-term relief; day wear if night wear alone fails.
  • Education: avoid sustained wrist bending and forceful gripping; keyboard and mouse habits; breaks.
  • Manual therapy to the neck and upper limb; superficial heat, interferential or diathermy; splint plus stretching (weak evidence, mild to moderate cases).
  • Ergonomic changes to tools and work posture.
  • Do not use low-level laser, iontophoresis, magnets or thermal ultrasound (APTA 2019); nerve gliding has conflicting evidence.
  • Refer for injection or surgery if thenar wasting, constant numbness or no response.
  • After release: wound care, tendon gliding, grip strengthening.

Physiotherapy in common peroneal nerve injury

Causes: fibular neck fracture, tight plaster, prolonged squatting or crossed legs, knee dislocation.

Features: foot drop (weak dorsiflexion, eversion, toe extension); high-stepping gait with foot slap; sensory loss over the outer leg and back of the foot.

Physiotherapy

  1. Protect: posterior leaf spring AFO; remove the cause (loosen plaster, avoid crossed legs and squatting).
  2. Range: daily calf stretching to prevent equinus.
  3. Re-education: dorsiflexors and evertors with biofeedback or electrical stimulation as Tinel's sign advances; functional electrical stimulation during gait if available.
  4. Strengthening: gravity-eliminated, then against gravity, then resisted.
  5. Gait training: heel strike, normal swing; uneven ground.
  6. Skin care of the numb top of the foot; footwear.
  7. Tendon transfer if no recovery.

Erb's palsy: features and physiotherapy (motor re-education)

Erb's palsy: upper plexus (C5–C6, upper trunk) injury when the head and shoulder are forced apart (two-wheeler fall; in babies, shoulder dystocia).

Features

  • Lost shoulder abduction (deltoid, supraspinatus), external rotation (infraspinatus), elbow flexion (biceps, brachialis, brachioradialis), supination.
  • "Waiter's tip" posture: arm adducted and internally rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated.
  • Sensory loss over the outer arm and forearm; biceps reflex lost; risk of shoulder subluxation.

Physiotherapy

  • Support the arm (sling or shoulder support); prevent subluxation and overstretching.
  • Range: full shoulder range daily, especially external rotation and abduction, and supination.
  • Motor re-education of deltoid, spinati, biceps and supinator: gravity-eliminated positions first (lying, powder board, sling suspension), with EMG biofeedback or electrical stimulation; then against gravity and resisted.
  • Functional reaching and task practice; sensory protection.
  • Refer to a plexus surgeon if no recovery by about 3–6 months; after nerve transfer, fire the donor muscle to activate the target, then practise alone.

Klumpke's paralysis: features and physiotherapy

Klumpke's palsy: lower plexus (C8–T1) injury when the arm is pulled forcibly overhead (grabbing a branch while falling; breech delivery).

Features

  • Paralysis of hand intrinsics, finger and wrist flexors: claw hand.
  • Sensory loss on the inner forearm and hand.
  • Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) if the T1 root is avulsed: poor prognosis.

Physiotherapy

  • Anti-claw (knuckle-bender) splint; keep the thumb web open.
  • Passive range of fingers and wrist; MCP flexion stretch.
  • Re-education of intrinsics and long flexors with biofeedback or electrical stimulation; grip and pinch practice.
  • Sensory protection and re-education of the inner hand.
  • Task practice for work; refer for surgery if no recovery.
