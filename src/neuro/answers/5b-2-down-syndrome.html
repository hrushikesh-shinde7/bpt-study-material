Physical traits and neurological impairments in Down's syndrome

Down's syndrome: trisomy 21 (non-disjunction 96%, translocation 3–4%, mosaicism 1–2%); risk rises with maternal age; about 1 in 1000 births in India.

Physical traits

  • Head and face: small, flat-backed (brachycephalic) head; flat facial profile and nasal bridge; upward-slanting eyes with epicanthic folds; Brushfield spots; small mouth with protruding tongue; small low-set ears; excess skin at the back of the neck.
  • Hands: short, broad; single palmar crease; clinodactyly of the little finger.
  • Feet: wide sandal gap between first and second toes; flat feet.
  • Body: short stature, hypotonia, hypermobile joints.

Neurological impairments

  • Hypotonia and reduced strength.
  • Intellectual disability (usually moderate, IQ 35–50); speech and language delay.
  • Delayed milestones: sitting about 10 months, crawling 14 months, walking about 26 months.
  • Poor balance and postural control; slow reaction times; poor motor planning.
  • Gait: wide base, out-turned feet, knees locked in extension.
  • Hearing loss (75%), vision problems, seizures, early Alzheimer's in adults.

Associated: congenital heart disease (40–50%), sleep apnoea, thyroid disease, atlantoaxial instability (1–2% symptomatic).

Physiotherapy management of a child with Down's syndrome

Problems: hypotonia, lax joints, weak muscles, delayed milestones, poor balance, low endurance (heart), intellectual disability.

Assess: milestones, tone, laxity, balance, endurance, heart status, neck symptoms; AIMS, GMFM or Peabody.

  • Developmental training in milestone order: head and trunk control (tummy time, prone play), sitting, crawling, pull to stand, cruising, walking; handling in midline; avoid the frog position.
  • Treadmill training: supported stepping on a small treadmill, 8 minutes a day, 5 days a week, from sitting alone; walked about 101 days earlier (Ulrich 2001).
  • Strength and postural control: weight-bearing play, squat to stand, step-ups, ball activities; progressive resistance in adolescents (twice a week for 10 weeks).
  • Balance on varied surfaces; running, jumping, ball skills through play.
  • Fitness: active play, swimming, cycling, dance; weight control.
  • Joint protection: no end-range stretching; supramalleolar orthoses or insoles for flat feet.
  • Teaching style: short, frequent, play-based sessions; demonstration, simple words, praise; parents run a daily home programme.

Safety: atlantoaxial instability signs (neck pain, head tilt, new weakness, gait or bladder change) → stop and refer; avoid trampolines; know the heart status.
