Explain theories of motor control (any two)

Motor control is the ability to regulate or direct the mechanisms essential to movement. Movement emerges from the individual, the task and the environment; the theory a therapist follows decides what is treated.

1. Hierarchical theory (Hughlings Jackson, Magnus)

  • Control is top-down: cortex → brainstem → spinal cord; development follows a fixed sequence.
  • A higher lesion releases primitive reflexes and abnormal patterns (for example after stroke).
  • Limitation: lower levels also influence higher ones; not all movement is hierarchical.
  • Use: basis of neurofacilitation approaches (Bobath, Brunnstrom, Rood): inhibit reflexes, follow developmental sequences.

2. Systems theory (Bernstein)

  • The body is a mechanical system with many degrees of freedom; the nervous system controls them through synergies.
  • Movement arises from many systems working together (musculoskeletal, sensory, perceptual, cognitive) plus forces from body and environment.
  • Limitation: less detail on how nervous system and environment interact.
  • Use: assess and treat every system that limits the task (strength, range, sensation, balance); basis of task-oriented training.

Other theories in one line each: reflex (Sherrington: movement as chains of reflexes; cannot explain movement without input); motor programming (central pattern generators; treadmill stepping); dynamic systems (self-organisation, attractor states, control parameters such as speed); ecological (Gibson: perception–action, affordances; practise real tasks in real places).

The individual, task and environment circles; cortex over brainstem over spinal cord.
