Rebound test

  • Method: the patient flexes the elbow against the examiner's resistance; the examiner suddenly lets go while guarding the patient's face with the other hand.
  • Normal: the triceps checks the movement at once.
  • Positive (cerebellar lesion): the arm flies towards the face or shoulder: impaired check, Holmes' rebound phenomenon, on the side of the lesion.
  • Variant: tap the outstretched arms downward and watch for excessive swing.

Assessment of sensory ataxia

  • Romberg's test: steady with feet together and eyes open; sways or falls with eyes closed (positive).
  • Joint position sense and vibration (128 Hz) reduced, starting in the toes.
  • Finger-nose and heel-shin tests much worse with eyes closed.
  • Pseudoathetosis: outstretched fingers wander with eyes closed.
  • Gait: high-stepping, stamping; watches the feet; worse in the dark.

Cause: posterior columns, dorsal roots or peripheral nerves (B12 deficiency, tabes, neuropathy).

Physiotherapy (treatment) of cerebellar ataxia

Cerebellar ataxia: dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor, decomposition, rebound, hypotonia; truncal sway; wide-based staggering gait; nystagmus, dysarthria. Assess with SARA, Berg, TUG.

Principles

  • Intensive, repeated coordination and balance practice: 4 weeks of coordinative training reduced ataxia and improved gait, gains kept with home practice (Ilg 2009).
  • Train the person's own goals as whole tasks; increase difficulty gradually; lifelong in progressive disease.

Techniques

  • Proximal stability: prone on elbows, four-point kneeling, kneeling, half-kneeling, standing; approximation; PNF rhythmic stabilisation and stabilising reversals.
  • Balance: sitting then standing, reducing base, reaching, weight shift, stepping, perturbations.
  • Coordination: slow, controlled movements to targets, gradually faster; mirror or video feedback; Frenkel's for the sensory component.
  • Gait: slightly wide base then narrowing; walking on lines, over obstacles, turning; treadmill; rollator or weighted walker.
  • Upper limb: task practice (writing, eating, buttoning) with elbows stabilised; weighted cutlery and pens, wrist weights.
  • Fitness: stationary or recumbent cycling.

Guard with a gait belt; avoid fatigue (worsens ataxia); no alcohol or sedatives; home safety.

Cerebellar ataxia and the rationale of balance training

Cerebellar ataxia: uncoordinated movement (errors of direction, range, timing and force) not due to weakness, from lesions of the cerebellum or its connections. The cerebellum compares intended and actual movement and corrects errors; when it fails, movements overshoot, decompose and tremble near the target.

  • Hemisphere lesion: same-side limb ataxia (dysmetria, intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, rebound).
  • Vermis lesion: truncal sway (titubation), wide-based staggering gait, cannot tandem walk.
  • Nystagmus, scanning dysarthria; Romberg usually negative (unsteady even with eyes open).

Rationale of balance training

  • The cerebellum adapts movement through practice and error; repeated balance challenges let remaining cerebellar circuits and other areas learn to reduce sway and correct errors (plasticity).
  • Strengthens postural muscles and improves proximal stability.
  • Builds confidence, reduces fear of falling and falls (balance exercise >3 hours a week prevents most falls, Sherrington 2017).
  • Because cerebellar learning is slower, training needs more repetitions and longer programmes.

Progression

Sitting → kneeling → standing; wide → narrow base; static → dynamic (reaching, stepping, perturbations); eyes open → closed; firm → soft surface; single → dual task.
