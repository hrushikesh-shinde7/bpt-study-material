Asked: 7 marks: theories of motor control, any two (W19, S21, S23). No 3-mark or long-case questions; expect dynamic systems theory or limitations of reflex theory as 3 marks.

Definition

Motor control: the ability to regulate or direct the mechanisms essential to movement. Movement emerges from the individual (perception, cognition, action systems), the task and the environment acting together; change any one and the movement changes. The theory chosen decides what the therapist treats: reflexes, tone, or the person practising a task in a real setting.

The six theories (7m W19, S21, S23)

  • Reflex (Sherrington): reflexes are the building blocks; complex movement = chain of reflexes triggered by sensory input. Limit: cannot explain movement without sensory input (deafferented animals still move), fast sequences, new movements. Use: assess reflexes; sensory stimuli to bring out or inhibit movement (Rood).
  • Hierarchical (Hughlings Jackson, Magnus): top-down: cortex → brainstem → spinal cord; higher lesion releases primitive reflexes; development in a fixed sequence. Limit: lower levels also control higher ones (spinal reflexes can dominate); not every adult movement goes through a hierarchy. Use: Bobath, Brunnstrom, Rood: inhibit abnormal reflexes, follow developmental sequences.
  • Motor programming: stored central program or central pattern generator moves without sensory input (spinal stepping generator). Limit: cannot alone explain adaptation to body and environment. Use: retrain whole patterns, not single muscles; treadmill stepping uses spinal pattern generators.
  • Systems (Bernstein): body as a mechanical system with many degrees of freedom; many systems work together, controlled through synergies, with forces from body and environment. Limit: less detail on how nervous system and environment interact. Use: assess and treat all systems (strength, range, sensation, perception); train functional synergies.
  • Dynamic systems: movement self-organises; preferred patterns are stable attractor states; a control parameter (speed) shifts behaviour to a new pattern (walk to run). Limit: can underplay the nervous system. Use: find the parameter that shifts an abnormal pattern (speed, support, task demand); practise at transition points.
  • Ecological (Gibson): perception and action linked; people perceive what the environment allows them to do (affordances) and act to achieve goals. Limit: less on the nervous system's organisation. Use: real tasks in varied real environments; teach reading the environment (kerbs, crowds).

Theory to approach

TheoryApproach and what the therapist does
Reflex, hierarchicalNeurofacilitation: Rood, Bobath, Brunnstrom, PNF; changes tone and reflexes with handling and sensory input; developmental sequences
Systems, dynamic, ecological; motor learningTask-oriented training, MRP, CIMT; practises real tasks, adapts task and environment, treats impairments that limit the task

Current: practice draws on several theories at once; the systems view (person, task, environment) is the basis of task-oriented training. Hemiplegia: hierarchical view → inhibit reflexes and tone; systems view → treat all impairments, many repetitions of sit to stand, reaching, walking, varied task and setting, feedback. Evidence favours task-oriented practice, borrowing older techniques when they help. See approaches, motor learning.
