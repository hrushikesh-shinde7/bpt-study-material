Asked: no long case. 7 marks: principles of PNF (W25), MRP for the hemiplegic upper limb (W18), principles and techniques of Rood's approach (S17). 3 marks: three Rood techniques for spasticity (S16), three Bobath principles (W16), three MRP principles (S18), reflex-inhibiting patterns with an example (W19), three Rood principles (S22).

Approaches and evidence

  • Neurophysiological (1940s–1970s): Rood, Bobath, Brunnstrom, PNF: reflexes, sensory stimulation, handling to change tone and bring out movement.
  • Motor learning: MRP, task-oriented training, CIMT: patient as an active learner practising real tasks.
  • Cochrane 2014 (Pollock): rehabilitation combining components works after stroke; no one named approach is more effective; do not use compartmentalised approaches.

Classic vs current: not one approach for the whole programme; evidence-based components centred on active, repetitive, task-specific practice; passive handling alone does not improve function. End every answer: "used within task-oriented practice; no single approach superior (Cochrane 2014)".

Rood: principles (3m S22; 7m S17)

  • Normalise tone and evoke the response with the right sensory stimulus: some facilitate, some inhibit.
  • Sensorimotor control develops in sequence (ontogenetic sequence): treatment follows developmental patterns.
  • Movement is purposeful: activities call out the response automatically.
  • Repetition of sensorimotor responses is needed for learning.

Ontogenetic patterns: supine withdrawal → roll over → pivot prone → neck co-contraction → prone on elbows → all fours → standing → walking. Levels of control: mobility → stability → controlled mobility → skill.

Bobath / NDT (3m W16, W19)

  • Abnormal tone and postural reflexes block normal movement: inhibit first.
  • Facilitate normal automatic postural reactions (righting, equilibrium, protective) and normal patterns.
  • Handling through key points of control: proximal (head, shoulder girdle, pelvis); distal (hands, feet).
  • Treat the whole person; include the affected side in all activity (bilateral, symmetrical).

Reflex-inhibiting pattern (RIP): position or movement opposite to the spastic pattern to reduce tone before facilitating. Arm: shoulder forward and ER, elbow extended, forearm supinated, wrist and fingers extended, thumb abducted; e.g. sitting with weight on the extended arm beside or behind the body. Leg: hip and knee flexed, hip abducted and ER, ankle dorsiflexed. Child with extensor thrust: flexed side lying or carried in flexion.

Now: contemporary Bobath = problem-solving for postural control and function; not superior after stroke (Pollock 2014); passive NDT ineffective for motor goals in CP (Novak 2020).

Brunnstrom

Based on stages of recovery (flaccid → synergies with spasticity → voluntary synergies → out of synergy → isolated; stroke). Early: use associated reactions, tonic reflexes, synergies to bring out movement; then break out of synergy. Phenomena: associated reactions; Souques' (arm above ~150° releases finger extension); Raimiste's (resisted hip abduction or adduction of strong leg → same in weak leg); homolateral limb synkinesis. Today: stages for assessment only; do not train synergies.

Motor Relearning Programme (3m S18; 7m W18)

  • Recovery = relearning real-life tasks: task-specific practice in context.
  • Patient is an active learner; instruction, demonstration, feedback.
  • Eliminate unnecessary muscle activity and compensation; practise missing components.
  • Practice and carry-over: many repetitions on ward and at home; biomechanics and motor learning.

Four steps: analyse task → practise missing components → practise whole task → transfer to daily life. Seven sections: upper limb; oro-facial; sitting up over the side of the bed; balanced sitting; standing up and sitting down; balanced standing; walking.

Upper limb (W18): analyse reach, grasp and release, manipulation → in lying, reach to ceiling and control arm at points of range; in sitting, reach forward and sideways; wrist extension while grasping a cup; release; thumb opposition to each finger; turning hand over → whole tasks: drink from cup, place objects, comb, write → avoid shoulder hitching, trunk leaning; protect shoulder, prevent shortening → practice sheets for ward and home with family.

PNF principles (7m W25)

Philosophy: positive, builds on what the person can do, functional, strong parts help weak, whole person, motor learning.

  • Resistance: optimal, to facilitate contraction and learning.
  • Irradiation and reinforcement: strong muscles spread activity to weak in the pattern.
  • Manual contact: lumbrical grip guides direction. Body position and mechanics in line with movement.
  • Verbal commands short, clear, timed; vision: patient watches.
  • Traction facilitates movement; approximation stability and weight-bearing.
  • Stretch stimulus and reflex; timing: normal distal to proximal, timing for emphasis.
  • Patterns: diagonal, spiral mass movements.

Sherrington basis: after-discharge, temporal and spatial summation, irradiation, successive induction, reciprocal innervation.

Techniques: agonist: rhythmic initiation (passive → assisted → active → resisted; starting, rigidity), combination of isotonics (control), repeated stretch; reversals: dynamic reversals (coordination), stabilising reversals and rhythmic stabilisation (stability); relaxation: contract-relax, hold-relax (range; hold-relax for pain).

Imagery, CIMT, transfer

Mental imagery added to practice improves arm function after stroke (low-quality, Cochrane 2020): short guided sessions, own view, of practised tasks. CIMT: restrain strong hand + intensive shaped practice; needs some wrist and finger extension; effective in children (Novak 2020). Transfer: context-specific practice; part → whole; bilateral transfer; varied practice.
