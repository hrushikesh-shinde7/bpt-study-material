Neuroplasticity (3m S16, S18, S26)

Neuroplasticity: the ability of the nervous system to change its structure and function in response to experience, learning, training or injury. In the intact brain (learning) and after a lesion (reorganisation); adaptive or maladaptive (spasticity, pain, not using the weak arm).

Brain changes (S18): synapse: long-term potentiation and depression, unmasking silent synapses; neuron: synaptogenesis, new dendrites and spines, axonal sprouting into areas that lost input; white matter: myelination changes; cortical maps: trained part's area enlarges, areas next to a lesion take over, other hemisphere recruited.

Synaptogenesis (S26): formation of new synapses; fastest in development, continues with learning; after injury intact neurons sprout and form new synapses (reactive synaptogenesis), which therapy can shape.

Kleim and Jones (2008): use it or lose it; use it and improve it; specificity; repetition; intensity; time; salience; age; transference; interference (a compensation can block learning).

Recovery and muscle adaptation

  • Spontaneous: swelling settles, penumbra reperfused, diaschisis resolves (distant areas switched back on); first weeks to months. Experience-dependent: practice-driven, months to years.
  • Recovery = same movement as before; compensation = new way (trunk lean to reach); can block true recovery (Levin 2009). Learned non-use: brain area shrinks; CIMT reverses it. Enriched environment helps; ward inactivity slows.
  • Muscle: motor units lost, fibres smaller, types change; shortened muscles lose sarcomeres → stiffness, contracture; non-neural stiffness adds to tone: treat length and strength.

Strength and conditioning

Progressive resistance, functional strengthening (sit-to-stand, step-ups), power training, load to fatigue in a set; aerobic conditioning improves fitness and walking. Watch BP, fatigue, heat (MS), dysreflexia (SCI), overwork (GBS, DMD). Classic "no resisted exercise in spasticity" vs current: strength training does not increase spasticity (Ada 2006); weakness limits function most.

Motor learning (3m S21, W24, W25; W17)

Motor learning: processes linked to practice or experience leading to relatively permanent change in capability for skilled movement; shown by retention and transfer, not performance during practice.

Stage (Fitts, Posner)Learner; therapist
CognitiveWhat to do; many errors, slow, full attention; clear instruction, demonstration, frequent feedback, quiet setting
AssociativeRefining how; fewer errors; less feedback, more practice, vary the task
AutonomousAutomatic, can dual-task; real-world varied practice, little feedback

Factors (W17): enough, distributed, variable and random practice (contextual interference), whole task; feedback faded; meaningful goals, external focus ("reach the cup"); task- and context-specific; demonstration, mental practice, sleep. Blocked practice helps early performance only.

Feedback (7m S25; 3m S22)

Intrinsic: own senses (foot hitting the ground, seeing the cup move). Extrinsic (augmented): therapist's words, mirror, video, EMG biofeedback, force platform. KR = outcome ("10 m in 12 s"); KP = pattern ("knee locked back in stance"). Concurrent or terminal; immediate or delayed. Schedule: frequent early, then fade; summary or bandwidth (too much → dependence). Role: corrects errors, motivates, reinforces, replaces lost sensation (vision for poor proprioception, EMG for weak dorsiflexor).
