Meningitis: definition and types

Inflammation of the meninges, usually infection. Bacterial (most serious): group B streptococcus in newborns; meningococcus, pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae later. Tuberculous (TBM): slow, over weeks; common in India; leaves hydrocephalus, strokes, cranial nerve palsies; stages I alert, no focal signs; II drowsy or focal; III coma. Viral (milder), fungal, parasitic.

Features and sequelae

Fever, headache, neck stiffness, light sensitivity, vomiting, confusion; seizures, coma, weakness if severe; non-blanching rash (meningococcal). Infants: irritability, poor feeding, drowsiness, bulging fontanelle.

Bacterial: about 1 in 6 die; 1 in 5 survivors have lasting effects: hearing loss, seizures, limb weakness, vision, speech, memory, amputations after sepsis (WHO). TBM complications: hydrocephalus, strokes (hemiparesis), cranial nerve palsies, vision loss, seizures, spinal arachnoiditis, cognitive and behaviour problems.

Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs (3m W19, S21)

Kernig'sSupine, hip and knee to 90°, try to straighten the knee → pain and resistance in back of thigh stop extension
Brudzinski's neckPassive neck flexion, chin to chest → hips and knees flex involuntarily
Brudzinski's contralateral legFlex one hip and knee → other leg flexes

Meaning: meningeal irritation (meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage); stretch on inflamed roots. Limit: positive in only about 5% of adults with meningitis (Thomas 2002): a negative sign never rules it out; neck stiffness often absent.

Tests: lumbar puncture (cells, protein, sugar, organisms), blood cultures, CT or MRI first when needed. Hearing test for every child after bacterial meningitis.

Medical; encephalitis; others

Urgent antibiotics (steroids in some); anti-TB drugs for months; shunt for hydrocephalus.

Encephalitis: brain inflammation, usually viral: fever, confusion, behaviour change, seizures, coma, focal weakness. Japanese encephalitis: Culex mosquitoes; 1 in 250 infections severe; up to 30% with symptoms die; 20–30% of survivors keep problems (WHO 2024); vaccine in endemic districts. After-effects: parkinsonism, dystonia, spasticity, cognitive and behaviour problems, epilepsy. PT as for brain injury.

Others: neurocysticercosis (seizures in India); brain abscess; Pott's spine, transverse myelitis (spinal cord); GBS, polio, leprosy, HIV.

PT after meningitis (ICF: hemiparesis, ataxia, hearing loss; school, play)

  • Acute, drowsy or ICU: head up about 30°, turning, chest care, gentle ROM, foot and hand splints: no rise in brain pressure.
  • Weakness, spasticity: task practice, strengthening, stretching, positioning, orthoses (as stroke or CP).
  • Lost milestones: developmental training in sequence, play-based.
  • Ataxia: balance and coordination training.
  • Hearing loss, attention: face the child, gestures, demonstration, short sessions; hearing aids.

Precautions

Infection control (masks, hand hygiene) as the team directs; avoid neck flexion and head-down while brain pressure may be high; watch seizures; after TBM watch shunt problems and new weakness (stroke, hydrocephalus).
