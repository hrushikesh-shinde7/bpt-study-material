1Uses of electrotherapy in neurology

  • Pain relief: TENS.
  • Muscle activation and re-education: neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) and functional electrical stimulation (FES).
  • Feedback: EMG biofeedback.
  • Denervated muscle: long-pulse stimulation (limited evidence).
  • Electrodiagnosis: the strength-duration curve (now largely replaced by EMG and nerve conduction; see assessment).

  • Neuro uses: painful hemiplegic shoulder (alongside positioning and movement), musculoskeletal pain secondary to abnormal posture, some neuropathic pain (evidence is limited).
  • Electrodes over or around the painful area or along the nerve; the patient must be able to feel and report the tingling.

3NMES and FES

  • NMES: stimulation of an innervated nerve to produce muscle contraction, for strengthening or re-education.
  • FES: stimulation timed to a functional task, for example the dorsiflexors during swing, or wrist and finger extensors during grasp and release.
  • Typical settings: about 30–50 Hz for a smooth contraction; pulse width about 200–300 µs (longer for large muscles); a ramp up and down; on–off cycles with long rests at first (about 1:3 to 1:5) to avoid fatigue; intensity for a strong, comfortable contraction; sessions of about 20–30 minutes.
  • Best used with active effort and task practice (for example the patient tries to open the hand while the stimulator assists, then grasps an object).

4EMG biofeedback

  • Surface electrodes pick up muscle activity and show it as a sound or visual signal, so the patient can learn to increase activity in a weak muscle or reduce it in an overactive one.
  • Uses: early muscle re-education after nerve injury or stroke (a flicker shows up before visible movement), relaxing overactive muscles, retraining after tendon transfers.
  • Evidence from small trials suggests it may help as an add-on to exercise; it does not replace practice.

5Denervated muscle and the strength-duration curve

  • Denervated muscle responds only to long pulses (tens to hundreds of milliseconds, for example interrupted direct or triangular currents). Stimulation is sometimes used to slow wasting until the nerve regrows, but evidence in people is limited; for Bell's palsy, electrical stimulation has shown no benefit (see facial nerve).
  • Strength-duration curve: the current needed to produce a minimal contraction plotted against pulse duration.
    • Rheobase: the smallest current that causes a contraction with a long pulse.
    • Chronaxie: the pulse duration needed at twice the rheobase; under about 1 ms in normally innervated muscle and much longer in denervated muscle.
    • Denervation shifts the curve upwards and to the right; a kink suggests partial denervation; serial curves can show reinnervation.

6Precautions and contraindications

Take care
  • Spinal cord injury at or above T6: stimulation below the lesion can trigger autonomic dysreflexia; monitor blood pressure and stop at once if symptoms start.
  • Pacemakers and implanted defibrillators (avoid the chest and seek advice); over the carotid sinus or the front of the neck; over the eyes.
  • Over known malignancy, active infection, deep vein thrombosis, or broken or fragile skin.
  • The abdomen or low back in pregnancy; around the head and neck in uncontrolled epilepsy.
  • Numb skin or poor understanding: lower intensities, frequent skin checks, close supervision.
  • Always check skin before and after, and explain the sensation.

3 mFunctional electrical stimulation.

Electrical stimulation of nerves to produce contractions timed to a functional activity, for example peroneal nerve stimulation during swing for foot drop or wrist and finger extensor stimulation during grasp. About 30–50 Hz, 200–300 µs, triggered by a switch or sensor. Peroneal FES is as good as an AFO after stroke; check skin and watch for autonomic dysreflexia in high cord injury.

3 mStrength-duration curve.

A plot of the current needed for a minimal contraction against pulse duration. Rheobase is the smallest current with a long pulse; chronaxie is the pulse duration at twice the rheobase (under about 1 ms normally, much longer when denervated). Denervation shifts the curve up and to the right; a kink suggests partial denervation; serial curves show reinnervation.

7 mElectrotherapy in the management of hemiplegia.

Use with task practice, not alone. NMES of supraspinatus and posterior deltoid for early shoulder subluxation (reduces subluxation, pain-free range improves). FES for wrist and finger extension during grasp practice. Peroneal FES for foot drop as an alternative to an AFO. EMG biofeedback for early muscle re-education. TENS for shoulder or musculoskeletal pain. Settings (30–50 Hz, 200–300 µs, ramps, rests). Precautions: sensation, cognition, skin, pacemakers, DVT.

LAQPractice: a 50-year-old woman 6 weeks after a right MCA stroke has a flaccid left arm with a two-finger-breadth shoulder subluxation and left foot drop. Plan electrotherapy and how it fits with the rest of her treatment. (15)

Assess sensation, skin, cognition, pain and contraindications. NMES to supraspinatus and posterior deltoid daily, with positioning and arm support, aiming to reduce subluxation and protect the shoulder; combine with active-assisted shoulder movement. As movement returns, FES for wrist and finger extension during grasp-and-release tasks. Peroneal FES or an AFO for foot drop during gait training. Settings 30–50 Hz, 200–300 µs, ramps, rest periods, building tolerance; skin checks; family taught to apply at home. Measure subluxation, pain-free range, Fugl-Meyer arm score and walking speed.
