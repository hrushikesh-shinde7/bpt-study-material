1Definition

  • Poliomyelitis is an infection by the poliovirus that destroys anterior horn cells (lower motor neurons) in the spinal cord and sometimes the brainstem, causing sudden, asymmetrical, flaccid paralysis with normal sensation.
  • Post-polio residual paralysis (PPRP) is the permanent weakness, wasting and deformity that remain once recovery from paralytic polio has stopped, about 2 years after the illness (the residual stage).
  • It is different from post-polio syndrome: new weakness, fatigue and pain that start many years (at least 15) after a long stable period.

2Classification and grading

Stages of paralytic polio

StageWhat happensPhysio focus
1. Acute (paralysis) stage: the first few weeksFever, painful muscle spasm, paralysis reaching its maximum within daysRest, positioning and splints in the functional position to prevent deformity; gentle passive movement as pain allows; chest care if breathing is weak
2. Recovery (convalescent) stage: up to about 2 yearsSurviving neurons recover and sprout; most recovery comes in the first 6 monthsMuscle charting, graded re-education and strengthening, standing and walking in orthoses, stretching

Some people develop post-polio syndrome decades later (section 8).

Types of polio infection

  • Most infections cause no symptoms or a mild illness; about 1 in 200 causes irreversible paralysis, usually of the legs (WHO).
  • Spinal paralytic (the commonest paralytic form): limb and trunk paralysis.
  • Bulbar: brainstem involvement with swallowing, speech and breathing problems; bulbospinal is both.

Grading: each muscle is graded on the MRC scale (0–5) and recorded on a muscle chart, which guides orthoses and surgery.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Poliovirus (an enterovirus, types 1, 2 and 3), spread mainly by the faecal–oral route; it mainly affects children under 5 (WHO).
  • Unvaccinated children are at risk; oral and injectable polio vaccines prevent it.
  • India: the last wild polio case was on 13 January 2011 (Howrah, West Bengal), and the WHO South-East Asia Region, including India, was certified polio-free on 27 March 2014. Most patients with PPRP in India today are adults who had polio as children.
  • What makes deformity worse: marked muscle imbalance, lying in the frog position, growth of the bones while weak muscles and contracted soft tissues lag behind, and no splinting, orthosis or physiotherapy.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. The virus multiplies in the gut, enters the blood and destroys anterior horn cells, so the muscles they supply become flaccid and waste and reflexes are lost. Sensory and autonomic neurons are spared, so sensation and bladder control are normal.
  2. The damage is patchy and asymmetrical. Muscles whose anterior horn cells lie in a small area of the cord are hit hardest: tibialis anterior, quadriceps, deltoid and opponens pollicis.
  3. Surviving neurons sprout to take over orphaned muscle fibres (very large motor units), which explains recovery up to about 2 years. These overworked units may fail decades later (post-polio syndrome).
  4. Because strong muscles pull against weak ones, and gravity and posture add to it, contractures and deformities develop; a joint with no muscle control becomes unstable (flail).
  5. The paralysed limb grows less (shortening), carries less weight (thin bones) and loses its muscle pump (a cold, bluish limb).

5Clinical features

  • Asymmetrical flaccid weakness and wasting, legs more than arms; reduced or absent reflexes; normal sensation, bladder and bowel.
  • The affected limb is thin, shorter, and often cold and bluish.
  • Gait: hand-on-thigh gait (weak quadriceps), Trendelenburg gait (weak hip abductors), backward lurch (weak gluteus maximus), high-stepping gait (foot drop), short-limb gait.

6Investigations

  • Acute flaccid paralysis: two stool samples, at least 24 hours apart, within 14 days of onset, tested for poliovirus (national surveillance).
  • PPRP is diagnosed from the history and examination: a detailed muscle chart, range of motion, deformities and limb lengths.
  • X-rays: deformities, hip subluxation, scoliosis; a scanogram or block test for limb-length difference.
  • EMG and nerve conduction if the diagnosis is unclear: chronic denervation with large motor units, normal sensory conduction.
  • Breathing tests if the trunk is weak or the scoliosis is severe; bone density in adults with fractures.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Prevention: vaccination (oral and injectable polio vaccines; the Pulse Polio programme in India). There is no antiviral cure; acute care is supportive.
  • Surgery is planned in the residual stage, once recovery has stopped (usually after 2 years), using the muscle chart:
ProblemSurgery
ContractureSoft-tissue release (iliotibial band, hip flexors, hamstrings, tendo Achillis lengthening)
Muscle imbalance or weaknessTendon transfer from the strong side of the joint, only if the donor muscle is grade 4–5 (it usually loses about one grade); for example hamstrings to the quadriceps, tibialis posterior to the dorsum of the foot, or a superficialis tendon to restore thumb opposition
Unstable or deformed jointOsteotomy (for example supracondylar extension osteotomy of the femur near skeletal maturity) or arthrodesis (foot, shoulder, wrist, spine)
ShorteningShoe raise; lengthening of the short limb or growth arrest of the long one. A difference under 2 cm can be ignored
ScoliosisSpinal fusion for severe or progressive curves

Post-polio syndrome (Halstead criteria, recommended by the EFNS)

  1. A confirmed history of paralytic polio.
  2. Partial or fairly complete recovery after the acute illness.
  3. At least 15 years of neurological and functional stability.
  4. Two or more new problems after the stable period: marked fatigue, muscle or joint pain, new weakness, new wasting, loss of function, cold intolerance (new weakness is the key one).
  5. No other medical explanation.

The EFNS guideline rates supervised aerobic and muscle training (isometric and isokinetic) as a safe, effective way to prevent further decline in people with moderate weakness (Level B); training in warm water without swimming is particularly useful.

9PT assessment

  • History: age at polio, recovery, previous surgery and orthoses, falls, pain, fatigue, any new weakness, work and home.
  • Muscle chart: manual muscle testing (MRC 0–5) of every muscle on both sides, dated, so change and transfer options can be judged.
  • Range and deformity: Thomas test (hip flexion), Ober's test (iliotibial band), popliteal angle (hamstrings), ankle dorsiflexion, recurvatum angle.
  • Limb length: true and apparent; girth.
  • Spine and posture: scoliosis, pelvic obliquity, sitting balance.
  • Gait and function: pattern, aids, orthoses, stairs, transfers; endurance (for example 6-minute walk).
  • Skin under orthoses, circulation, bone health and breathing if the trunk is weak.

Practice case (typical presentation)

A 30-year-old woman who had polio at the age of 2 works as an office clerk. Her right quadriceps is grade 2 and her right ankle dorsiflexors grade 1. She walks pushing her right thigh with her hand, has a tight right iliotibial band (hip flexion–abduction contracture and knee valgus), a foot drop and a right leg 3 cm shorter. For a year she has had right knee pain and tires by evening.

12PT management

By stage

  • Acute stage: rest; keep the limbs in neutral (no frog position) with pillows, sandbags and splints in the functional position; warmth for painful spasm; gentle passive movement once pain allows; chest care if breathing is weak.
  • Recovery stage (up to 2 years): muscle chart every few months; graded re-education (grades 0–1: passive movement and facilitation; grade 2: assisted and gravity-eliminated work, hydrotherapy; grade 3 and above: active and resisted work, short of fatigue); stand and walk early in orthoses; stretching and night splints.
  • Residual stage (PPRP): strengthen partially weak muscles; stretch contractures; prescribe orthoses and a shoe raise; gait and functional training; physiotherapy before and after surgery; in adults, pacing and joint protection.

Answer for W18: physiotherapy for PPRP (7 marks)

  1. Assess: muscle chart, contractures, limb length, gait, function.
  2. Strengthen muscles of grade 3 or more against resistance, and grade 1–2 muscles with assisted and gravity-eliminated work, avoiding fatigue.
  3. Stretch the iliotibial band, hip flexors, hamstrings and calf; prone lying; serial casts or night splints.
  4. Orthoses: AFO for foot drop; floor-reaction orthosis or KAFO for a weak quadriceps; shoe raise; crutches or a stick.
  5. Gait and function: walking with the orthosis, stairs, transfers, daily activities.
  6. Around surgery: prepare and re-educate after tendon transfers and releases.
  7. Education: home programme, skin checks, pacing, warning signs of post-polio syndrome.

Rehabilitation after a tendon transfer

  • Before: strengthen the donor muscle (it must be grade 4–5), teach its action, and stretch any contracture.
  • After the immobilisation the surgeon sets: start by asking for the donor's old action (for example "bend the knee" after a hamstring-to-quadriceps transfer) while the tendon now produces the new one; use touch, mirror or EMG biofeedback; progress to the new action in function.
  • Protect the transfer from stretching; after foot transfers an AFO is usually worn for about 6 months.

Classic answer vs current guidance: correcting deformities

Classic

Stretch and correct every contracture and deformity.

Current

With a weak quadriceps, mild genu recurvatum or mild equinus keeps the knee straight in stance, so leave them alone; correct recurvatum beyond about 15° (Joseph and Watts, 2015).

Write in the exam

"Mild recurvatum or equinus that stabilises a knee with weak quadriceps is not corrected."

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Do not stretch away a mild recurvatum or equinus that keeps a weak knee stable.
  • Avoid exercise to exhaustion; reduce it if pain, weakness or fatigue lasts into the next day.
  • Thin bones: use gentle, sustained force when stretching, and protect against falls.
  • Check the skin under orthoses: wasted limbs have little padding over bony points.
  • After a tendon transfer, do not stretch the transferred tendon.
  • New weakness, fatigue, breathing or swallowing problems in an adult: refer (possible post-polio syndrome).

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Daily stretches and prone lying; strengthening a few times a week.
  • Wear and care of orthoses and the shoe raise; check the skin daily.
  • Pacing, rest breaks, a healthy weight and joint protection (correct crutch height and grip).
  • Report new weakness, unusual fatigue, pain or breathing problems.
  • Make sure children in the family complete their polio vaccination.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • After about 2 years no further nerve recovery occurs; function then depends on the pattern of weakness, the control of deformity, orthoses and surgery.
  • Many people walk and work independently with orthoses; some develop post-polio syndrome after at least 15 stable years.
  • Discharge when: orthoses fit and are used, walking and transfers are safe, the home programme is independent, and the warning signs are known.

3 mWhat is PPRP? (S24)

Post-polio residual paralysis is the permanent asymmetrical flaccid weakness, wasting and deformity left after paralytic poliomyelitis once recovery has stopped, about 2 years after the illness. Polio passes through the acute stage (weeks), the recovery stage (up to about 2 years) and the residual stage. Sensation is normal; deformities come from muscle imbalance, posture and growth.

7 mDefine PPRP. Describe any 3 deformities seen in PPRP in detail. (W22)

Definition as above. 1. Hip flexion, abduction and external rotation: a tight iliotibial band and tensor fasciae latae, often from the frog position in the acute stage; positive Thomas and Ober tests; leads to pelvic obliquity, lumbar scoliosis and knee valgus. Treat with prone lying, stretching, and release if fixed. 2. Genu recurvatum: weak quadriceps, so the person locks the knee backwards to stand (with equinus adding to it); leave mild recurvatum, brace or osteotomy if over about 15°; floor-reaction orthosis. 3. Equinus (foot drop): weak tibialis anterior with a working calf and gravity; high-stepping gait and tripping; calf stretching, AFO, and a tendon transfer (for example tibialis posterior) when a grade 4–5 donor is available.

7 mDescribe the clinical features and physiotherapy management of PPRP complications. (S25)

Features: asymmetrical flaccid weakness, wasting, lost reflexes, normal sensation, a short cold limb, abnormal gait. Complications with physiotherapy: contractures and deformities (stretching, positioning, casts, orthoses); joint instability (strengthening, floor-reaction orthosis or KAFO); limb-length difference (shoe raise, gait training); scoliosis and pelvic obliquity (trunk strengthening, seating, breathing exercises); osteoporosis and fractures (weight bearing, falls prevention); overuse pain and arthritis (joint protection, crutch advice, pacing); post-polio syndrome (pacing, moderate non-fatiguing exercise, orthosis review).
