1Definition

  • Ataxia is uncoordinated movement (errors in the direction, range, timing and force of movement) that is not due to weakness.
  • The cerebellum compares the intended movement with what is happening and corrects it; when it fails, movements overshoot, break into parts and tremble as they near the target.

2Classification and grading

TypeLesionKey difference
Cerebellar ataxiaCerebellum or its connectionsPresent with eyes open and closed; nystagmus, dysarthria, intention tremor; Romberg's test usually negative (unsteady even with eyes open)
Sensory ataxiaPosterior columns, dorsal roots or peripheral nervesMuch worse with eyes closed or in the dark; loss of position and vibration sense; Romberg's test positive; stamping gait while watching the feet
Vestibular ataxiaInner ear, vestibular nerve or nucleiVertigo, nystagmus; limb coordination normal; veers to one side
  • Cerebellar hemisphere lesions cause limb ataxia on the same side; midline (vermis) lesions cause truncal and gait ataxia.
  • Scales: Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA: 8 items, 0–40, higher is worse); International Cooperative Ataxia Rating Scale (ICARS).

3Causes and risk factors

  • Vascular: cerebellar infarct or haemorrhage (posterior circulation stroke).
  • Inherited: Friedreich's ataxia, spinocerebellar ataxias, ataxia telangiectasia.
  • Acquired: multiple sclerosis; alcohol; drugs (phenytoin); tumours; head injury; infections and post-infectious cerebellitis in children; hypothyroidism; vitamin E and B12 deficiency.
  • Congenital: ataxic cerebral palsy.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. The cerebellum receives the motor plan and sensory feedback and corrects errors in timing and force; it also adapts and learns movements.
  2. Because error correction fails, movements overshoot or undershoot (dysmetria), break into separate parts (decomposition), and tremble near the target (intention tremor).
  3. Because postural control is poor, the trunk sways and the gait is wide-based and staggering; falls are common.
  4. Because motor learning depends on the cerebellum, progress is slower, so training needs more repetitions.
  5. Secondary problems: reduced activity and fitness, fear of falling, contractures and scoliosis in progressive disease.

5Clinical features

  • Limbs: dysmetria (past-pointing), dysdiadochokinesia (slow, irregular rapid alternating movements), intention tremor, decomposition of movement, rebound phenomenon, hypotonia.
  • Trunk and gait: truncal sway (titubation), wide-based, staggering gait with irregular steps; cannot walk heel to toe.
  • Eyes and speech: nystagmus; dysarthria with scanning or slurred speech.

Rebound test (3 marks in W17)

The patient pulls the forearm towards the shoulder against the examiner's resistance (elbow flexion); the examiner suddenly lets go while guarding the patient's face. Normal: the triceps checks the movement at once. Cerebellar lesion: the arm flies towards the face or shoulder (impaired check, Holmes' rebound phenomenon). A variant: tap the outstretched arms downward and watch for excessive swing.

Assessment of sensory ataxia (3 marks in S19)

  • Romberg's test: steady with feet together and eyes open, sways or falls with eyes closed.
  • Joint position sense and vibration (128 Hz tuning fork) reduced, starting in the toes.
  • Finger-nose and heel-shin tests much worse with the eyes closed.
  • Pseudoathetosis: outstretched fingers wander with the eyes closed.
  • High-stepping, stamping gait; the person watches the feet and is worse in the dark.

Friedreich's ataxia (W18 and W24 cases)

  • The commonest inherited ataxia; autosomal recessive (a GAA repeat in the frataxin gene); onset usually in childhood or adolescence, before 25.
  • Progressive gait and limb ataxia, dysarthria, loss of knee and ankle reflexes with extensor plantar responses, loss of position and vibration sense (mixed cerebellar and sensory ataxia), nystagmus.
  • Pes cavus, kyphoscoliosis, cardiomyopathy (the main cause of death), diabetes, sometimes hearing and visual loss.
  • Most people need a wheelchair within 10–20 years of onset.

6Investigations

  • MRI brain: stroke, MS plaques, tumours, cerebellar atrophy; spinal cord atrophy in Friedreich's ataxia.
  • Genetic testing for inherited ataxias.
  • Nerve conduction (sensory neuropathy), ECG and echocardiogram (cardiomyopathy), blood sugar, vitamin levels, thyroid function.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Treat the cause: stroke care, MS treatment, stopping alcohol or the causative drug, vitamin replacement.
  • Friedreich's ataxia: omaveloxolone, the first approved drug (2023, in some countries, for ages 16 and over); treatment of cardiomyopathy and diabetes; scoliosis and foot surgery when needed.
  • Few drugs help ataxia itself, so rehabilitation is the main treatment.

9PT assessment

W18 asked for assessment and tests (5 marks).

  • History: onset and progression, family history, falls, function, work, home.
  • Coordination tests: finger-nose, finger-finger, heel-shin, rapid alternating movements, rebound test, drawing circles; eyes open and closed.
  • Sensation: position sense, vibration, Romberg's test.
  • Balance and gait: sitting and standing balance, tandem walk, Berg Balance Scale, Timed Up and Go, 10-metre walk.
  • Ataxia scale: SARA.
  • Others: tone, strength (often near normal), range, posture and spine, feet, speech, upper limb function (Nine-Hole Peg Test), fitness.

12PT management

Asked for 7 marks in S16 and W16 (physiotherapy of cerebellar ataxia) and W19 (rationale of balance training).

Principles

  • Intensive, repeated coordination and balance practice: in degenerative ataxia, 4 weeks of intensive coordinative training reduced ataxia and improved gait, and the gains lasted a year with home practice (Ilg et al., 2009 and 2010).
  • Train the person's own goals as whole tasks; increase difficulty gradually.
  • Separate cerebellar from sensory ataxia: use vision and feedback to compensate in sensory ataxia.
  • In progressive disease, keep training for life to maintain function; add aids before falls happen.

Rationale of balance training in cerebellar ataxia (W19)

The cerebellum adapts movements through practice and error. Balance training repeatedly challenges postural control, so the remaining cerebellar circuits and other brain areas learn to reduce sway and correct errors; it also strengthens postural muscles, builds confidence and reduces falls. Exercise that challenges balance for more than 3 hours a week reduces falls most in older people (Sherrington et al., 2017).

Techniques

  • Proximal stability: weight-bearing in prone on elbows, four-point kneeling, kneeling, half-kneeling and standing; approximation; PNF rhythmic stabilisation and stabilising reversals.
  • Balance training: sitting, then standing with a reducing base, eyes open and closed (for sensory ataxia start with vision), reaching, weight shifts, stepping, perturbations (see the balance page).
  • Coordination training: whole-body tasks (kneeling to standing, stepping onto targets), slow, controlled limb movements to targets, gradually faster; video or mirror feedback; Frenkel's exercises for the sensory component.
  • Gait training: walking with a slightly wider base, then narrowing; walking along lines and over obstacles; turning; treadmill with or without support; a rollator or weighted walker if needed.
  • Upper limb: task practice of writing, eating, buttoning; proximal stabilisation (elbows on the table); weighted cutlery and pens, wrist weights for some tasks; adaptive devices.
  • Fitness: stationary cycling or a recumbent bike; strength for proximal muscles.
  • Friedreich's ataxia extras: posture and spinal care, foot orthoses for pes cavus, cardiac precautions, wheelchair and seating when walking becomes unsafe.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Guard closely; use a gait belt and parallel bars at first.
  • Friedreich's ataxia: watch for breathlessness, palpitations and chest pain (cardiomyopathy); check blood sugar (diabetes).
  • Cerebellar stroke: watch for new headache, vomiting or drowsiness (swelling can compress the brainstem early on).
  • Avoid fatigue, which worsens ataxia; rest between sets.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • A daily home programme of balance, coordination and walking practice; exercise is the main treatment.
  • Home safety: grab bars, rails on stairs, non-slip floors, good lighting, sitting for dressing.
  • Adaptive equipment: weighted cutlery, thick pens, rollator, later a wheelchair for distances.
  • Avoid alcohol and sedatives, which worsen ataxia; genetic counselling for inherited ataxias.
  • Support to stay in education, work and social life.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Cerebellar stroke often recovers well over months with training.
  • Degenerative ataxias progress; training slows functional decline and keeps people safer.
  • Friedreich's ataxia: most people need a wheelchair within 10–20 years of onset; cardiomyopathy limits life expectancy.
  • Review regularly; discharge from a block when goals are met and the home programme is established.

3 mDescribe the rebound test.

The patient flexes the elbow against the examiner's resistance; the examiner suddenly releases while guarding the face. Normally the movement is checked at once; in cerebellar disease the hand flies towards the face (Holmes' rebound phenomenon).

3 mHow do you assess sensory ataxia?

Romberg's test (steady with eyes open, sways with eyes closed); position and vibration sense; finger-nose and heel-shin worse with eyes closed; pseudoathetosis; stamping gait, worse in the dark.

7 mPhysiotherapy management of cerebellar ataxia.

Assess (coordination, balance, gait, SARA). Intensive coordination and balance training with progression; proximal stability (weight-bearing, approximation, rhythmic stabilisation); gait training with a wider base, then narrowing, treadmill, aids; upper limb task practice with stabilisation and weighted utensils; fitness (cycling); home programme; fall prevention; precautions. Evidence: 4 weeks of intensive coordinative training improves ataxia and gait, maintained with home practice.

LAQA 65-year-old diabetic man 3 months after a right cerebellar stroke has weakness and poor balance, with a Berg score of 38. Discuss ICF and physiotherapy. (7 + 8, W22)
