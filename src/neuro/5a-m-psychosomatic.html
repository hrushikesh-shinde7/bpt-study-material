1What it is

  • Functional neurological disorder (FND) (older terms: conversion disorder, hysteria, psychosomatic paralysis) is a problem of how the nervous system works, not of its structure: real, involuntary symptoms such as weakness, abnormal movements, seizures or numbness, without the damage seen in stroke or MS.
  • It is common in neurology clinics and often follows a trigger: an injury, an illness, pain, a panic attack or stress (not always psychological trauma).
  • Not the same as malingering (deliberately faking for gain) or factitious disorder (deliberately producing symptoms to be a patient). People with FND are not pretending.
  • It can coexist with other neurological disease (for example functional seizures with epilepsy).

2Types

  • Functional weakness or paralysis: one limb, one side, or both legs.
  • Functional movement disorders: tremor, dystonia (often a fixed clenched fist or inverted ankle), jerks, gait disorders.
  • Functional (dissociative) seizures.
  • Functional sensory symptoms, dizziness and cognitive symptoms; fatigue and pain are frequent companions.

3Diagnosis by positive signs

FND is diagnosed from positive clinical signs showing that the system can work, not only by excluding other disease.

Reflexes, tone and plantar responses are normal in pure functional weakness, and there is no wasting early on.

4Physiotherapy for functional motor disorders

Principles (consensus, Nielsen et al., 2015)

  1. Explain the diagnosis clearly and positively: the symptoms are real, the nervous system is not damaged, and it can relearn; show the patient their positive signs (for example normal movement during Hoover's test).
  2. Set expectations of recovery and agree goals with the patient.
  3. Retrain movement with attention directed away from the limb: use automatic movements, rhythm, dual tasks, music, imagery and distraction; start with what the patient can do (for example walking backwards or sideways, or on a treadmill) and build towards normal patterns.
  4. Avoid reinforcing abnormal patterns: minimise aids, braces and hands-on support where safe; withdraw them gradually when used.
  5. Self-management plan: daily practice, pacing for fatigue, handling setbacks, return to activities and work.
  6. Team work: psychology (CBT) and psychiatry where needed, especially for anxiety, depression or trauma.

Classic answer vs current guidance: "psychosomatic paralysis"

Classic

A diagnosis of exclusion; reassure that "nothing is wrong" and refer to psychiatry; physiotherapy has no role.

Current

A positive diagnosis with specific signs; physiotherapy based on explanation and movement retraining is a core treatment. In the Physio4FMD trial (2024), specialist physiotherapy did not improve the main physical-function score at 12 months more than usual community physiotherapy, but patients more often felt improved and it was likely cost-effective.

Write in the exam

"Positive signs (Hoover's), clear explanation, movement retraining with redirected attention, minimal aids, self-management and team care."

5Chronic pain with psychological factors

  • Long-lasting pain often involves an over-sensitive nervous system (central sensitisation) and is worsened by fear, low mood, poor sleep and inactivity; the pain is real.
  • Physiotherapy: pain education (how pain works), graded activity and graded exposure to feared movements, regular exercise, pacing, sleep and stress strategies, and a cognitive-behavioural approach; reduce reliance on passive treatments.
  • Screen for red flags first (see ICF page), and refer for psychological care when distress is high.

3 mWhat is Hoover's sign?

A positive sign of functional leg weakness: hip extension of the "weak" leg is weak on direct testing but returns to normal when the patient flexes the other hip against resistance, because hip extension then happens automatically. The examiner feels the heel press down into the hand.

3 mDifferentiate functional weakness from malingering.

Functional weakness is real and involuntary, arising from altered nervous system function, often after a trigger; the person is not pretending. Malingering is deliberate feigning of symptoms for external gain (money, leave, avoiding duty).

7 mPhysiotherapy for a patient with functional paralysis of one leg.

Assessment including positive signs (Hoover's, hip abductor, give-way, dragging gait) and normal reflexes and tone. Explain the diagnosis positively and show the patient that the leg can move. Agree goals. Retrain movement with attention directed away from the leg: automatic tasks (sit to stand, walking backwards, treadmill, rhythm, dual tasks), then normal walking; minimal aids and hands-on help, withdrawn gradually; graded progression; daily self-management plan; manage fatigue and pain; involve psychology when needed; plan return to work.

LAQPractice: a 28-year-old bank clerk developed weakness of the right leg after a minor fall 3 months ago; reflexes and tone are normal and Hoover's sign is positive; she walks dragging the leg with a stick and has stopped working. Write assessment, goals and physiotherapy. (5 + 3 + 7)

Assessment (5): history and triggers, fears and beliefs, fatigue and pain; positive signs, normal reflexes and plantar; gait pattern; function and work; screen mood. Goals (3): short term: walks indoors without the stick; long term: returns to work and normal walking outdoors. Physiotherapy (7): explanation with demonstration of normal movement; retraining walking with redirected attention (treadmill, rhythm, walking backwards, dual tasks); gradual removal of the stick; graded activity and fatigue pacing; home practice plan and setback plan; liaison with psychology; graded return-to-work plan.
