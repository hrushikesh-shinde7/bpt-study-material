1Definitions and aims

  • An orthosis is an externally applied device that supports, aligns, protects or improves the function of a body part. Splint usually means a temporary or custom-made orthosis; brace is an older word for the same.
  • Static orthoses hold a joint still; dynamic ones allow or assist movement (springs, elastics, hinges).
  • Aims: improve function (walking, grasp); stabilise weak or unstable joints; prevent or reduce deformity; protect healing structures or overstretched muscles; position for comfort, skin and hygiene.
  • Named by the joints they cross: FO (foot), AFO (ankle-foot), KAFO (knee-ankle-foot), HKAFO (hip-knee-ankle-foot), WHO (wrist-hand), CTLSO (cervico-thoraco-lumbo-sacral).

2Biomechanical principles

  • Three-point force system: two forces on one side and one opposite force in between control a joint. In a foot-drop AFO, the footplate lifts the forefoot, a strap holds the front of the ankle, and the calf section presses on the back of the upper calf.
  • Pressure = force ÷ area: wide, well-moulded contact and long lever arms spread pressure and protect the skin, which matters most over wasted or numb limbs.
  • Ground reaction force: where it passes relative to the knee decides whether the knee bends or straightens; a ground-reaction AFO uses this to push the knee back in stance.
  • Joint alignment: the orthotic joint must line up with the anatomical joint axis, or the device rubs and pistons.
  • Materials: thermoplastics (polypropylene for AFOs; low-temperature sheets for quick splints), metal and leather (adjustable, heavier), carbon fibre (light, springy).

3Prescription and check-out

  1. Assess: range of motion (fixed or flexible deformity), tone, strength, sensation and skin, balance and gait, cognition, home and goals.
  2. Set the aim in ICF terms: for example "walk to the bus stop without tripping".
  3. Choose the design (type, joints, stops, material) with the orthotist.
  4. Check the fit: alignment, trim lines, pressure areas, ease of putting on; shoes that fit over it.
  5. Train: donning and doffing, walking with it, a gradual wearing schedule.
  6. Review: skin after each use, growth in children, wear and tear; measure the effect (gait speed, falls, Berg, task performance).

4Lower limb orthoses

Ankle-foot orthosis (AFO; 3 marks in W21)

  • Parts: calf section with a strap, ankle section (solid or hinged), footplate; worn inside a shoe.
  • Principle: a three-point system that controls the ankle (and, through the ground reaction force, the knee).
  • Uses: foot drop (stroke, peroneal nerve injury, MS, CMT), equinus and spasticity, ankle instability, crouch gait, knee hyperextension from a plantarflexed ankle.
  • Types: posterior leaf spring, solid, hinged (articulated) with a plantarflexion stop, ground-reaction (anterior shell), and supramalleolar orthosis below it.
  • Evidence: AFOs increase walking speed after stroke (Nascimento et al., 2020); peroneal nerve stimulation (FES) was as good as an AFO in a large trial (Bethoux et al., 2014).

Above-knee and standing orthoses

  • KAFO: for a weak quadriceps or an unstable knee (polio, spinal cord injury, spina bifida); knee joints with a drop lock (locked in standing), an offset joint (stable when weight is in front of it) or a Swiss lock.
  • HKAFO and reciprocating gait orthosis (RGO): for paraplegia and high spina bifida; the RGO links the hips so that extending one hip swings the other leg forward.
  • Standing frames and parapodium: supported standing for children and adults who cannot stand alone (bones, hips, bladder, development).

Foot orthoses for children with cerebral palsy (7 marks in S22)

Solid AFO, hinged AFO with plantarflexion stop, ground-reaction AFO, posterior leaf spring AFO, supramalleolar orthosis and dynamic (thin, moulded) AFO, each matched to the gait problem. The full answer is on the cerebral palsy page.

6Making a temporary splint

  1. Decide the position (for a cock-up splint, the wrist in about 20–30° of extension).
  2. Make a paper pattern around the limb, marking landmarks (for a cock-up: two-thirds of the forearm to the distal palmar crease, clear of the thumb).
  3. Trace and cut the pattern from low-temperature thermoplastic sheet.
  4. Heat in a water bath at about 65–70 °C (usually 1–2 minutes, by sheet thickness), lift out and dry.
  5. Test the warmth on your own skin, then mould it on the patient (over stockinette) in the chosen position and hold it for the working time (a few minutes) until it sets.
  6. Trim and smooth the edges, flare them away from the skin, pad bony points, and fix straps (forearm, wrist and palm).
  7. Check the fit after a short trial: red areas, numbness, function; spot-heat with a heat gun to adjust.
  8. Teach the wearing schedule, skin checks and care: clean with cool water and keep away from heat (hot water, stoves, a closed car in the sun), which deforms it.

Serial casting: plaster or synthetic casts changed every few days to gain range step by step, for example for a fixed equinus.

7Evidence, precautions and common mistakes

Classic answer vs current guidance: splinting to prevent contracture

Classic

Give every hemiplegic or spastic hand a resting splint to prevent contracture and reduce tone.

Current

Sustained stretch from splints, positioning or casts has no clinically important effect on joint mobility in neurological conditions (Cochrane, 2017), and the 2024 Cochrane review of orthoses after stroke found only very low-certainty evidence. Use splints for a specific aim (function, protection, comfort, hygiene) and train active use.

Write in the exam

"Splints are prescribed for a stated functional aim and reviewed; contracture is prevented mainly by active use, positioning and early movement."

Take care
  • Check the skin after every use, especially over numb, wasted or swollen limbs; redness that does not fade soon after removal, blisters or numbness mean the orthosis must be adjusted.
  • Fixed deformity must be corrected or allowed for before a rigid orthosis is fitted.
  • Too heavy or too long an orthosis raises energy cost; use the lightest that does the job.
  • Children grow: review fit every few months.
  • Check that the patient can put it on and actually uses it.

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mAnkle-foot orthosis. (W21)

An orthosis crossing the ankle and foot: calf section and strap, ankle section, footplate, worn in a shoe. Works by a three-point force system controlling the ankle (and the knee through the ground reaction force). Types: posterior leaf spring, solid, hinged with plantarflexion stop, ground-reaction. Uses: foot drop (stroke, peroneal palsy, MS), spastic equinus, ankle instability, crouch gait. Check the skin and fit.

3 mDescribe the cock-up splint. (W25)

A forearm-based wrist orthosis holding the wrist in about 20–30° of extension (neutral for carpal tunnel), ending at the distal palmar crease so the fingers and thumb are free. Static for rest and night; dynamic (lively) with an outrigger and elastic slings for daytime function in radial palsy. Prevents overstretching of the paralysed extensors and lets the flexors grip strongly.

7 mDescribe various foot orthoses used for children with cerebral palsy. (S22)

Aims (stable base, control equinus, improve gait, protect range). Solid AFO (equinus with poor control, non-walkers, night stretch); hinged AFO with plantarflexion stop (walkers with enough calf length); ground-reaction AFO (crouch with good passive range); posterior leaf spring (mild foot drop); supramalleolar orthosis (flat valgus feet); dynamic thin AFO (mild tone problems). Prescription and check-out, skin checks, review with growth, and combine with stretching and strengthening.

LAQPractice: a 55-year-old man 4 months after stroke walks with his toes catching and his knee snapping back into hyperextension in stance; ankle dorsiflexion reaches neutral passively. Assess, choose an orthosis with reasons, and plan training. (5 + 5 + 5)

Assess (5): ankle range (fixed or dynamic equinus), calf tone (Modified Ashworth), dorsiflexor and quadriceps strength, knee control, sensation and skin, balance, gait speed and TUG. Choose (5): an AFO set in slight dorsiflexion, hinged with a plantarflexion stop (or solid if the ankle is unstable): it clears the foot in swing, and because the ankle cannot drop into plantarflexion in stance, the shank tilts forward and the knee is no longer pushed back into hyperextension. Train (5): donning, a gradual wearing schedule, gait training with the AFO (heel strike, knee control drills, step practice), strengthening and calf stretching; review skin and gait speed in 2–4 weeks; consider peroneal FES as an equal alternative.
