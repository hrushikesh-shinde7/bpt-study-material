1Definition

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked recessive disease in which the muscle protein dystrophin is absent, so muscle fibres are damaged by their own contractions and are slowly replaced by fat and fibrous tissue. It causes progressive, mainly proximal weakness that begins in early childhood and later affects breathing and the heart.

  • It affects boys: about 16–20 per 100,000 live male births (about 1 in 5,000–6,000).
  • Signs usually appear between 2 and 5 years of age.

The five stages of DMD (2018 care considerations)

  1. At diagnosis: mild delay; falls; family learns about the disease.
  2. Early ambulatory: Gowers' sign, waddling gait, toe walking; can still climb stairs.
  3. Late ambulatory: walking is laboured; loses the ability to climb stairs and rise from the floor.
  4. Early non-ambulatory: can self-propel a wheelchair for a time; posture is maintained; scoliosis may start.
  5. Late non-ambulatory: arm function and posture are hard to maintain; breathing and heart problems grow.

3Causes and risk factors

  • A mutation of the DMD gene on the X chromosome, most often a deletion of one or more exons, so no working dystrophin is made.
  • Inheritance: a carrier mother passes the gene to half of her sons (affected) and half of her daughters (carriers). Some cases arise from new mutations.
  • A family history on the mother's side, such as an affected maternal cousin or uncle (W20 case), is a strong clue.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Dystrophin anchors the inside of the muscle fibre to its membrane. Without it, the membrane tears during contraction, especially eccentric (lengthening) contraction.
  2. Calcium floods in, the fibre dies, and repair cannot keep up, so muscle is replaced by fat and fibrous tissue.
  3. Because fat and fibrous tissue enlarge some muscles, the calves look big but are weak (pseudohypertrophy).
  4. Because proximal muscles (hip extensors, quadriceps, shoulder girdle) fail first, the child struggles to rise from the floor, climb stairs and run.
  5. Because weak muscles and fibrosis shorten, contractures develop (ankle plantarflexors, iliotibial band, hip flexors, later knees, elbows and wrists).
  6. Because trunk and breathing muscles weaken, scoliosis and restrictive lung disease follow after walking is lost; the heart muscle is also affected (cardiomyopathy).
Why avoid eccentric and heavy resistance exercise?

Lengthening contractions and high loads put the most stress on the fragile membrane, so they cause more fibre damage than the muscle can repair. Submaximal activity such as swimming and cycling keeps the child fit without that damage.

5Clinical features

  • Early signs: late walking; frequent falls; difficulty running, jumping and climbing stairs; toe walking; enlarged calves; Gowers' sign; learning or speech delay in some.
  • Later: loss of walking; contractures; scoliosis; weak cough and breathing; cardiomyopathy; weight gain on steroids, weight loss later.

Gowers' sign (3 marks in S16 and S24)

Gowers' sign is the way a child with weak hip and knee extensors gets up from the floor: he turns face down, rises onto hands and knees, then hands and feet with the hips high, walks his hands up onto his knees and thighs, and pushes the trunk upright. It shows proximal lower-limb weakness, typical of DMD but also seen in other myopathies and spinal muscular atrophy.

Gait deviations (7 marks with Gowers' sign in S26)

  • Waddling (Trendelenburg) gait: the pelvis drops and the trunk sways over the stance leg because the hip abductors are weak.
  • Increased lumbar lordosis with the shoulders held back: weak hip extensors, so the child keeps the line of gravity behind the hips.
  • Wide base for balance.
  • Toe walking (equinus) from tight calves and weak dorsiflexors; no heel strike.
  • Knee locked back in stance as the quadriceps weaken.
  • Arms held behind the trunk; slow, short steps; frequent falls; cannot run or jump.

6Investigations

S16 asked to plan investigations (3 marks).

  • Creatine kinase (CK): very high, many times normal, from early life.
  • Genetic testing: deletion and duplication testing of the DMD gene first, then sequencing if needed; it confirms the diagnosis and the mutation type.
  • Muscle biopsy: absent dystrophin on staining, only if genetic tests are unclear.
  • EMG: myopathic pattern (small, brief, polyphasic potentials).
  • Heart: ECG, echocardiogram or cardiac MRI.
  • Lungs: FVC, peak cough flow, maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressures.
  • Bones: spine X-ray for vertebral fractures; bone density.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Glucocorticoids started before substantial physical decline: prednisone or prednisolone 0.75 mg/kg a day or deflazacort 0.9 mg/kg a day. They prolong walking: in large studies the mean age at losing walking rose from 10.0 years (little or no steroid) to 11.2 years on daily prednisone and 13.9 years on daily deflazacort.
  • Steroids must never be stopped suddenly (adrenal crisis); watch weight, bones, behaviour and growth.
  • Newer treatments for some mutations: exon-skipping drugs and gene therapy, available in a few countries.
  • Heart: regular checks and heart-failure medicines.
  • Breathing support: lung volume recruitment, assisted cough and non-invasive ventilation at set thresholds (section 8).
  • Surgery: Achilles tendon lengthening in selected walkers with a significant ankle contracture and good hip and knee extensor strength; posterior spinal fusion for a progressive curve after walking is lost.

9PT assessment

  • History: milestones, falls, family history on the mother's side, steroid use, school, home (floor living, stairs, toilet), parents' concerns.
  • Timed tests: time to rise from the floor (Gowers' time), 10-metre walk or run, four-stair climb; 6-minute walk.
  • Function: North Star Ambulatory Assessment (walkers); Performance of the Upper Limb scale.
  • Strength: manual muscle testing of proximal and distal groups.
  • Range of motion: ankle dorsiflexion with the knee straight, iliotibial band (Ober test), hip flexors (Thomas test), hamstrings, elbows, wrists.
  • Posture and spine: lordosis, scoliosis in sitting and standing.
  • Breathing: FVC, peak cough flow, chest expansion, cough.
  • Repeat at least every 6 months to define the stage.

12PT management

Principles (7 marks in S16, W22, S24 and S25): preserve walking and range for as long as possible; keep the child active without overworking fragile muscle; prevent deformity and falls; support breathing; keep him independent and included at every stage.

By stage

  • Early ambulatory: home stretching and night AFOs; submaximal aerobic play (swimming, cycling); energy conservation (a buggy or wheelchair for long distances so he can still play); fall prevention; parent education.
  • Late ambulatory: continue the above; serial casting for ankle tightness; KAFOs with locked knees to prolong standing and walking; a manual wheelchair for longer distances; standing frame.
  • Early non-ambulatory: power wheelchair with custom seating; supported standing; daytime AFOs for foot position; arm and hand stretching; breathing exercises; spine checks.
  • Late non-ambulatory: seating and pressure care; lung volume recruitment and assisted cough; arm supports and assistive technology; transfers with a hoist; pain management.

Classic answer vs current guidance: strengthening

Classic

Progressive resistance exercise to strengthen weak muscles.

Current

Avoid eccentric and high-resistance exercise; give regular submaximal aerobic activity such as swimming and cycling, with rests and energy conservation (2018 care considerations).

Write in the exam

"Submaximal aerobic activity (swimming, cycling) and functional play; no eccentric or heavy resistance work; stop before fatigue."

Wheelchair prescription (S26 long case)

  1. Assess: stage, walking ability, arm strength, trunk control, contractures, spine, breathing, cognition; home, school and terrain (a rural area: rough roads, no ramps); who will push; budget and repair services.
  2. Choose: first a light manual chair for long distances (pushed by family) while he still walks indoors; then a power chair when he can no longer walk, ideally with tilt, recline, elevating leg rests, standing support and adjustable seat height. In a rural setting, a sturdy frame with large pneumatic tyres that local mechanics can repair.
  3. Seating: solid seat and back, lateral trunk supports, hip guides, adductor pads (pommel), headrest, pressure-relieving cushion, lap belt.
  4. Measure: seat width about 5 cm more than hip width; seat depth about 5 cm less than buttock-to-knee length; seat height so the feet clear the ground with the footplate; armrest height about 2.5 cm above the elbow with the arm by the side; backrest high enough to support the trunk.
  5. Train and review: teach the child and family to use, fold and maintain it; check fit and posture every few months as he grows.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Avoid eccentric work (jumping down, running downhill, lowering heavy loads) and heavy resistance.
  • Stop before exhaustion; muscle pain or weakness lasting into the next day, or dark urine, means the activity was too hard.
  • Fragile bones: no forceful stretching; safe transfers; prevent falls.
  • Never stop steroids suddenly; in illness or injury the doctor may need to give extra steroid.
  • Heart and breathing involvement: watch breathlessness and colour during exercise.

14Parent education and home care

W16 asked for home-care advice to parents (3 marks); S20 for parent education and counselling.

  • Explain the disease honestly and simply; the child did nothing wrong, and active play is good when it is not exhausting.
  • Daily stretching routine and night splints; check the skin under splints.
  • Fun activity: swimming, cycling, play; avoid jumping games and long walks to exhaustion; use a buggy or wheelchair for outings.
  • Home safety: clear floors, rails on stairs, a western toilet or commode; chairs of the right height so he can rise easily.
  • School: ground-floor classroom or lift, extra time, rest breaks, adapted sports, help on stairs.
  • Healthy diet and weight control, especially on steroids; vitamin D and calcium as advised.
  • Genetic counselling for the mother and sisters; disability certificate and benefits; parent support groups; emotional support for the child and siblings.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • DMD is progressive. Without steroids, walking is lost at about 10 years on average; steroids delay this by one to several years.
  • Scoliosis, breathing failure and cardiomyopathy follow in the teens; with steroids, breathing support and heart care, many now live into adulthood.
  • Physiotherapy continues for life, with reviews at least every 6 months to stage the disease and update the programme.

3 mExplain Gowers' sign.

A child with weak hip and knee extensors rises from the floor by turning face down, getting onto hands and knees, then hands and feet with the hips high, walking the hands up onto the knees and thighs, and pushing the trunk upright. It shows proximal lower-limb weakness, classically in DMD.

3 mWrite three investigations for a boy suspected of DMD.

Serum creatine kinase (very high); genetic testing of the DMD gene (deletion and duplication testing, then sequencing); muscle biopsy for dystrophin if genetics are unclear. Also ECG and echocardiogram, lung function tests.

7 mExplain the complications of DMD and their management.

Contractures (stretching 4–6 times a week, night AFOs, casting, standing); scoliosis after walking is lost (seating with lateral supports, spine checks, spinal fusion); falls and fractures (home safety, vitamin D, quick remobilisation); respiratory failure (lung volume recruitment at FVC 60% or less, assisted cough at FVC under 50% or peak cough flow under 270 L/min, non-invasive ventilation); cardiomyopathy (regular cardiac review); obesity on steroids (activity and diet); pain and low mood (positioning, support).

LAQA 5-year-old only son has frequent falls, difficulty standing up from the floor and enlarged calves (DMD). Give parental advice for home care, his functional impairments with reasoning, and a short-term plan. (3 + 5 + 7, W16)

Home care (3): daily stretching and night splints; fun swimming and cycling but no exhausting play or jumping; safe floors and rails; chairs of the right height; diet; genetic counselling and support.

Impairments with reasoning (5): proximal weakness (fibre loss from absent dystrophin), so Gowers' sign and falls; calf pseudohypertrophy and early tightness; lordosis and waddling gait; poor endurance; balance problems.

Short-term plan (7): parents taught stretching of ankles, knees and hips 4–6 times a week; night AFOs; submaximal play-based aerobic activity with rests; balance games; posture; energy conservation with a buggy for long distances; home and school safety; review every 6 months.
