1Types of involuntary movement

MovementWhat it looks like
DystoniaSustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing twisting, repetitive movements or abnormal postures, often triggered or worsened by trying to move
AthetosisSlow, continuous, writhing movements, mostly of the hands and feet
ChoreaBrief, irregular, flowing jerks that move from one body part to another
BallismusLarge, flinging movements of a whole limb
TremorRhythmic oscillation at rest, in posture or on action
TicsSudden, repetitive movements or sounds that can be held back for a while
MyoclonusSudden, shock-like jerks

All come from disordered basal ganglia circuits (and their links with the cortex and cerebellum); they usually disappear in sleep and worsen with effort, emotion and excitement.

2Dyskinetic cerebral palsy

  • The commonest extrapyramidal disorder in children: dystonic or choreo-athetoid movements, fluctuating tone, poor head and trunk control, drooling and unclear speech, often with normal intelligence.
  • Linked to severe birth asphyxia (deep grey matter injury) and to kernicterus.
  • The full answers (types, ICF of the 5-year-old athetoid girl, carrying, management, the W25 case and the S26 posture and movement problems) are on the cerebral palsy page.

3Kernicterus (bilirubin encephalopathy)

  • Very high unconjugated bilirubin in a newborn crosses into the brain and damages the basal ganglia, brainstem nuclei and hearing pathways.
  • Classic picture: athetosis (dyskinetic CP), limited upward gaze, sensorineural hearing loss, and enamel defects of the baby teeth; intelligence often spared.
  • Prevention: early detection of jaundice, phototherapy and exchange transfusion.
  • Physiotherapy: as for dyskinetic CP, with hearing checked early and communication through vision and gesture.

4Wilson's disease (hepatolenticular degeneration)

  • An inherited (autosomal recessive) defect of copper handling: copper builds up in the liver and brain (basal ganglia).
  • Neurological picture in older children and young adults: tremor (including a coarse "wing-beating" tremor), dystonia, slow and slurred speech, drooling, parkinsonism, clumsy handwriting; behaviour and mood change.
  • Signs: Kayser–Fleischer rings (brownish rings at the edge of the cornea); liver disease.
  • Treatment: lifelong copper-removing drugs or zinc; liver transplant in liver failure. Treated early, much of the disability can improve.
  • Physiotherapy: dystonia and tremor management (stable positions, weighted or built-up utensils, task practice), balance and gait training, speech and swallowing referral.

5Sydenham's chorea

  • A feature of rheumatic fever, weeks to months after a streptococcal throat infection, usually in school-age children.
  • Chorea (often worse on one side), low tone and clumsiness, a "milkmaid's" grip that tightens and relaxes, emotional lability.
  • Usually settles over weeks to months; the child needs long-term antibiotic prophylaxis because of the heart risk.
  • Physiotherapy: safety during the active phase, rest and graded return to activity, handwriting and fine-motor support, and checking the heart before strenuous exercise.

6Childhood dystonias and tics

  • Early-onset generalised dystonia (genetic): often starts in a leg or foot in childhood and spreads; deep brain stimulation of the globus pallidus helps many.
  • Dopa-responsive dystonia: childhood leg dystonia that is worse in the evening and better after sleep, with a dramatic, lasting response to low-dose levodopa; every child with unexplained dystonia should have a levodopa trial.
  • Tics and Tourette syndrome: multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic for more than a year, starting in childhood; physiotherapy has little role beyond advice.

7Physiotherapy principles for involuntary movements

  • Stable base first: seating with a pelvic belt, foot support and trunk supports; standing frames; work in positions where the child is steady.
  • Midline and symmetry: hands together, head in midline, toys in front.
  • Graded, sustained holding (weight bearing through arms and legs) to build proximal stability.
  • Task practice of useful skills in stable positions; reduce effort and excitement that trigger overflow movements; calm surroundings.
  • Supports and devices: wrist and thumb splints for function, weighted or built-up utensils, switches and communication aids.
  • Teamwork: medicines (anticholinergics, baclofen, botulinum toxin for focal dystonia), speech and feeding therapy, and education.

3 mDifferentiate athetosis, chorea and dystonia.

Athetosis: slow, continuous, writhing movements of the hands and feet. Chorea: brief, irregular jerks flowing between body parts. Dystonia: sustained contractions producing twisting movements or abnormal postures, worse with attempted movement.

3 mWhat is kernicterus?

Brain damage from very high newborn bilirubin, affecting the basal ganglia, brainstem and hearing pathways: athetoid (dyskinetic) CP, limited upward gaze, sensorineural hearing loss and dental enamel defects. Prevented by early treatment of jaundice (phototherapy, exchange transfusion).

7 mPhysiotherapy principles for a child with involuntary movements.

Assess movement type, tone changes, postural control, function and seating. Provide a stable base (adapted seating with pelvic belt, foot and trunk supports; standing frame); midline and symmetrical positions; sustained holding and weight bearing for proximal stability; task practice in stable positions with low effort and calm surroundings; splints and adapted utensils; communication aids; work with medicines, speech and feeding therapy; family education and home programme.

LAQPractice: a 14-year-old girl with newly diagnosed Wilson's disease has dystonia of the right arm, tremor, slurred speech and falls. Write impairments, goals and physiotherapy. (5 + 3 + 7)

Impairments (5): dystonia and tremor of the right arm, poor balance and gait, dysarthria, fatigue, possible mood change. Goals (3): short term: safe walking at home and school, writing with adaptations; long term: independent at school with improving control as treatment works. Physiotherapy (7): balance and gait training, falls prevention, task practice of writing and self-care in stable positions, weighted or built-up utensils, stretching for dystonic postures, endurance exercise, speech-therapy referral, education about lifelong medication.
