Ischaemic heart disease, myocardial infarction and CABG

The most-asked long answer in this paper. Every case asks the same routine: pull the ICF out of the story, set goals that fit the patient's work and home, then plan cardiac rehab with doses.

My revision

1Definition

IHD, also called coronary artery disease (CAD), is a condition in which the heart muscle gets less blood and oxygen than it needs. The cause is usually atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries.

Angina pectoris is chest pain or discomfort caused by temporary ischaemia of the heart muscle. The muscle is not permanently damaged.

Myocardial infarction (MI) is death of heart muscle caused by prolonged ischaemia.

Exam point: the modern definition of MI

The Fourth Universal Definition of MI (2018) needs two things:

  1. a rise and/or fall of cardiac troponin, with at least one value above the 99th percentile of normal, and
  2. evidence of ischaemia: typical symptoms, new ECG changes, new Q waves, a new wall-motion defect on imaging, or a clot seen on angiography.

ACS is the umbrella term for sudden ischaemia. It covers unstable angina, NSTEMI and STEMI.

PCI, also called angioplasty or PTCA, opens the blocked artery from inside with a balloon, and usually leaves a stent behind.

CABG is open-heart surgery. The surgeon joins a graft vessel around the blocked part of the coronary artery so blood can bypass the block.

Killip class grades heart failure in the first days after MI: I no failure; II crackles in the lower lungs or a third heart sound; III frank pulmonary oedema; IV cardiogenic shock.

Universal Definition types of MI: type 1 is plaque rupture with clot; type 2 is a supply–demand mismatch (for example severe anaemia or fast arrhythmia); type 3 is sudden cardiac death; types 4 and 5 are related to PCI and CABG.

3Causes and risk factors

Main cause: atherosclerosis. Fatty plaque builds up in the artery wall and narrows the lumen. Less common causes include coronary spasm, an embolus, a tear in the artery (dissection), and extra demand from severe anaemia or a very fast heart rate.

Risk factors (asked as 3 marks in S17 and W17)
Non-modifiableModifiable
  • Age: men over 45, women over 55
  • Male sex (women catch up after menopause)

  • South Asian origin: heart disease starts earlier and is more severe
  • Smoking and all tobacco: cigarettes, bidi, gutkha
  • High blood pressure
  • Diabetes and insulin resistance
  • Abnormal lipids: high LDL, low HDL, high triglycerides
  • Obesity, especially a large waist
  • Physical inactivity
  • Unhealthy diet: excess salt, fried food, vanaspati and other trans fats
  • Excess alcohol
  • Stress and depression
Exam point

For "3 modifiable and 3 non-modifiable", write the three strongest in each column: age, male sex, family history and smoking, hypertension, diabetes. Abnormal lipids is an equally good fourth.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

Examiners never ask for pathophysiology alone. They ask for "clinical reasoning for the impairments". This chain is that reasoning.

The heart muscle's oxygen demand rises with heart rate, blood pressure and the force of contraction. Supply falls when a coronary artery narrows. Ischaemia starts when demand is greater than supply.

Why physio cares about healing time

Dead muscle is slowly replaced by scar. The scar takes about 6–8 weeks to become firm. Early activity is safe and helpful, but heavy straining is avoided until the scar matures.

Why does heart rate × blood pressure matter?

Heart rate multiplied by systolic BP is the rate–pressure product. It is a bedside estimate of how much oxygen the heart muscle needs. Angina tends to start at the same rate–pressure product in the same patient. Keep exercise below that level, and progress slowly so the heart can adapt.

Extra problems after CABG

  • Pain from the sternotomy and graft sites makes breathing shallow and cough weak.
  • Anaesthesia, ventilation and lying flat reduce lung volumes. This leads to basal atelectasis.
  • Guarding of the chest stiffens the shoulders and thoracic spine and causes a rounded posture.
  • Graft sites in the leg or forearm can swell, hurt or get infected.

5Clinical features

Angina

  • Central chest tightness, heaviness or pressure. Patients often describe it as a weight rather than a sharp pain.
  • Spreads to the left arm, jaw, neck or back.
  • Triggered by exertion, cold, heavy meals or emotion.
  • Stable angina settles within a few minutes of rest or a nitrate under the tongue.

Myocardial infarction

  • Severe chest pain lasting more than 20 minutes, not relieved by rest or nitrate.
  • Sweating, nausea and vomiting, breathlessness, weakness, and a sense of impending doom.
  • Signs: fast or slow pulse, low blood pressure, extra heart sounds, and crackles at the lung bases if the left ventricle is failing.
  • Inferior MI often causes a slow pulse or heart block, because the right coronary artery usually supplies the SA and AV nodes.
Silent or atypical MI

People with diabetes, older people and women may have little chest pain. They present with breathlessness, tiredness, upper-abdominal pain or confusion. The S20 case of a woman with diabetes and an EF of 25% is a typical example.

After CABG

Expect a patient with sternotomy and graft-site pain, chest drains, shallow breathing, reduced breath sounds at the lung bases, a weak cough, tiredness, a swollen leg or forearm at the graft site, and stiff shoulders. The S19 case ("unable to raise his hands above head") is this last problem.

6Investigations

Write only what changes physio decisions. That is what examiners look for in a case answer.

ECG

  • STEMI: new ST elevation in two neighbouring (contiguous) leads. The threshold is at least 1 mm in most leads. In V2–V3 it is 2 mm for men aged 40 or over, 2.5 mm for men under 40, and 1.5 mm for women.
  • Sequence in STEMI: tall (hyperacute) T waves → ST elevation → pathological Q waves → T-wave inversion.
  • NSTEMI and unstable angina: ST depression or T-wave inversion.

7Medical and surgical management

Emergency treatment of STEMI

  • Reperfusion is the priority. Primary PCI should be done within 90 min of first medical contact, or within 120 min if the patient must be transferred to another hospital.
  • If PCI cannot be done within 120 minutes, give thrombolysis (clot-dissolving drugs), ideally within 30 min. The W22 case was "thrombolysed".
  • Aspirin plus a second antiplatelet drug, and an anticoagulant.
  • Give oxygen only if SpO₂ is below 90%.
  • Nitrates for pain. Use an opioid only for severe pain.

Classic answer vs current guideline: MONA

Classic (older books)

Every MI gets MONA: Morphine, Oxygen, Nitrates, Aspirin.

Current (ESC 2023; ACC/AHA 2025)

Oxygen only if SpO₂ is below 90%. Opioids only for severe pain. The key steps are antiplatelets and urgent reperfusion.

Write in the exam

"Aspirin and early reperfusion by primary PCI or thrombolysis; oxygen only if SpO₂ < 90%."

PCI (angioplasty with stent)

  • A catheter enters through the radial artery at the wrist or the femoral artery in the groin. A balloon opens the block and a stent keeps it open.
  • After femoral access, the leg is kept straight for a few hours according to hospital protocol. Check the groin for bleeding or swelling before walking.
  • After radial access, walking can start early. The wrist is protected from heavy loading for a few days.
  • Driving: the UK DVLA allows car driving 1 week after successful PCI for an acute coronary syndrome, if EF is at least 40% and no further urgent procedure is planned; otherwise 4 weeks. India has no specific rule, so this is a useful reference. The S20 bank manager who must drive one hour fits here.

CABG

Usually chosen for left main disease, triple vessel disease (especially with diabetes or a weak ventricle), or when PCI is not possible or has failed.

  • Approach: median sternotomy. The sternum is split vertically and later closed with steel wires. Unlike a thoracotomy, no major muscle belly is cut, so the main post-op problems are sternal pain and protecting the healing bone.
  • Grafts: left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to the LAD is the standard because it stays open longest. Other grafts: right internal mammary (RIMA), radial artery, and great saphenous vein.
  • On-pump or off-pump: on-pump surgery uses a heart–lung machine while the heart is stopped. Off-pump surgery is done on the beating heart.
  • Lines and tubes after surgery: endotracheal tube on a ventilator for a few hours, mediastinal and pleural drains, temporary pacing wires, arterial and central lines, urinary catheter.

9PT assessment

Subjective

  • Present complaints: chest pain (site, character, spread, triggers, relief), breathlessness, palpitations, fatigue, wound pain.
  • Event and treatment: date of MI, thrombolysis, PCI or CABG with the post-operative day, grafts used, complications in ICU.
  • Risk factors and past history: diabetes, hypertension, lipids, previous heart events.
  • Medications, especially beta-blockers and nitrates.
  • Social history (this fills the ICF): job and its physical demands, travel and driving, earning role in the family, home floor and lift, Indian or Western toilet, family support, smoking, tobacco, alcohol, diet.
  • Patient's own goals and fears.

Objective

  • Vital signs at rest and with activity: heart rate and rhythm, BP lying and standing, respiratory rate, SpO₂, temperature.
  • Observation: monitors, lines, drains, oxygen, breathing pattern, posture, colour, swelling.
  • Chest: expansion, auscultation (listen especially at the bases), cough effectiveness.
  • Wounds: sternotomy and graft sites.
  • Musculoskeletal: shoulder and thoracic range, posture, muscle strength, calf for DVT signs.
  • Function: bed mobility, sit-to-stand, walking distance with vital signs, stairs.

12PT management

Two layouts, because papers ask both ways: by phase ("plan his cardiac rehabilitation") and by impairment ("physiotherapy for each impairment", S19).

Before surgery (when CABG is planned)

  • Explain the operation, the ICU, the drains and the plan for the first days. This reduces anxiety.
  • Teach deep breathing, holding a full breath in for a few seconds, and a supported huff and cough with a pillow hugged over the chest.
  • Teach sternal-safe bed mobility and ankle pumps.
  • Record baseline walking distance and vital signs.
  • In high-risk patients, inspiratory muscle training before surgery reduced lung complications after CABG in a randomised trial (Hulzebos, JAMA 2006).

Phase I: in hospital

Start when stable. After MI this means no new chest pain, no new ECG changes or dangerous arrhythmias, troponin stable or falling, and no uncontrolled heart failure. This is usually within the first day. After CABG, start on day 1.

Memory hook: Phase I limits (ACSM)

13 / 20 / 30 / 120

  • 13: keep RPE below 13 on the 6–20 Borg scale
  • 20: heart rate no more than 20 above resting after MI
  • 30: heart rate no more than 30 above resting after surgery
  • 120: and below 120 beats per minute after MI

Classic answer vs current evidence: bed rest after MI

Classic (older books)

Strict bed rest for a week or more, then a slow, many-step mobilisation programme over about two weeks.

Current (Cochrane; ACSM)

Mobilise as soon as the patient is stable, often within the first day. Short bed rest was no less safe than long bed rest.

Write in the exam

"Early mobilisation within 12–24 hours once clinically stable, progressing from 1–2 METs to about 3 METs by discharge."

Classic answer vs current evidence: incentive spirometry after CABG

Classic

Every post-CABG patient gets an incentive spirometer.

Current (AARC 2011)

Incentive spirometry is not recommended alone or routinely after CABG. Deep breathing, supported cough and early walking are the core.

Write in the exam

"Deep breathing exercises and early mobilisation; incentive spirometer may be used as an adjunct, not alone."

Phase II: early outpatient, supervised

Starts soon after discharge and usually runs for about 8–12 weeks. It has four parts: exercise, education, risk-factor control and psychological support. Begin with risk stratification (section 9) and an exercise test or 6-minute walk test.

Each session: warm-up 5–10 min, conditioning 20–60 min, cool-down 5–10 min. A longer warm-up and cool-down lower the risk of ischaemia and arrhythmia at the start and end.

Worked target heart rate (Karvonen)

Age 50, resting HR 70. Maximum HR = 220 − 50 = 170. Heart-rate reserve (HRR) = 170 − 70 = 100.

40% intensity = 70 + 40 = 110. 60% intensity = 70 + 60 = 130. Target range: 110–130 beats per minute.

If the patient takes a beta-blocker, this formula overestimates the target. Use RPE 11–13 and the talk test instead.

  • Breathe out on effort. Never hold the breath while lifting (Valsalva), because it causes sudden swings in BP.
  • Talk test: the patient should be able to talk in short sentences while exercising.

Classic answer vs current evidence: sternal precautions

Classic

  • Strict precautions for about 6–8 weeks
  • No lifting heavy objects
  • No pushing or pulling with the arms, no pushing up from a chair
  • No reaching both arms back or overhead against resistance

Current ("Keep your move in the tube")

Keep the upper arms close to the body, as if inside an imaginary tube around the trunk. Move within pain limits, with both arms working together. No fixed weight limit. A randomised trial and before-and-after studies found this as safe as strict precautions, with earlier return of function.

Write in the exam

"Sternal precautions for 6–8 weeks: avoid heavy lifting, pushing and pulling, and one-sided arm loading; keep arm movements close to the body and pain-free." This covers both versions.

Lifting heavy objects is usually avoided for about 3 months after CABG (NHS advice). Light, pain-free upper-limb exercise with the arms kept close to the body can begin earlier.

Phases III and IV: maintenance for life

  • Exercise in the community, at home or in a gym with less supervision.
  • Aim for at least 150 min a week of moderate activity plus resistance exercise on 2 days.
  • Self-monitoring with RPE and pulse. Regular review of risk factors.
Yoga (syllabus: "applied yoga in cardio-respiratory conditions")

The Indian Yoga-CaRe trial (3,959 patients after MI; JACC 2020) found that yoga-based cardiac rehab improved self-rated health and return to pre-infarct activities. It did not significantly reduce major cardiac events. It is a good option when conventional rehab is not available or acceptable. Avoid breath-holding and straining postures in the early weeks.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Do not start or continue exercise if (ACSM)
  • Unstable angina
  • Resting systolic BP above 180 or diastolic above 110 mmHg (decide case by case)
  • BP falls more than 20 mmHg on standing, with symptoms
  • Significant aortic stenosis
  • Uncontrolled arrhythmias, or uncontrolled sinus tachycardia above 120
  • Uncompensated heart failure
  • Third-degree heart block without a pacemaker
  • Active pericarditis or myocarditis
  • Recent embolism; acute thrombophlebitis or DVT
  • Fever or acute illness
  • Uncontrolled diabetes
  • Severe orthopaedic problems that prevent exercise
Stop the session if (ACSM, inpatient)
  • Diastolic BP reaches 110 mmHg or more
  • Systolic BP falls more than 10 mmHg as the workload increases
  • Significant atrial or ventricular arrhythmias
  • Second- or third-degree heart block
  • Angina, marked breathlessness, or ECG changes of ischaemia

Also stop for dizziness, confusion, pallor, cold sweat, or any sign of sternal movement or wound bleeding.

  • Angina during exercise: stop, sit the patient down, give GTN as prescribed. If pain is not better 5 minutes after the first dose, call for emergency help.
  • Avoid exercise for 1–2 hours after a heavy meal, and in very hot or cold conditions.
  • Avoid heavy isometric holds and breath-holding early on.
  • With beta-blockers, use RPE rather than heart rate.
  • After femoral PCI, check the groin before walking.

The ACSM thresholds above were checked against two independent reproductions (a WHO-hosted rehabilitation guideline and an insurer's clinical policy). The primary ACSM book was not available.

Home programme at discharge

  • Walk on level ground twice a day, starting with the distance managed in hospital. Add a few minutes each week, keeping RPE at 11–13.
  • Deep breathing exercises several times a day for the first weeks after surgery.
  • Pain-free shoulder and trunk exercises, with posture correction.
  • Check your pulse and use the RPE scale. Carry GTN.
  • Avoid straining and heavy lifting. Rest between tasks (energy conservation).
  • Report chest pain, unusual breathlessness, palpitations, fainting, wound redness or discharge, or sternal clicking.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

Prognosis depends on the size of the infarct (EF), arrhythmias, risk-factor control and taking part in rehab. Exercise-based cardiac rehab reduces hospital admissions by about a third and heart-attack risk by more than a quarter, and improves quality of life (Cochrane 2021).

Discharge from Phase I: stable vital signs at rest and with activity; independent in self-care and walking at about 3 METs; managed one flight of stairs without symptoms; knows the home programme, warning signs and wound care; referred to Phase II.

Discharge from Phase II: goals met; exercises safely and independently with self-monitoring; risk factors addressed; ready for Phase III–IV.

3 mList three modifiable and three non-modifiable cardiac risk factors.

Non-modifiable: age (men over 45, women over 55), male sex, family history of early heart disease.

Modifiable: smoking or tobacco, hypertension, diabetes. Abnormal lipids, obesity and inactivity are also correct.

3 mWrite Phase I cardiac rehabilitation in brief.
  1. In-hospital phase from the first stable day to discharge. Aims: prevent bed-rest effects, educate, and prepare for home.
  2. Early mobilisation from 1–2 METs to about 3 METs: breathing exercises, limb exercises, sitting, standing, walking, one flight of stairs before discharge.

MCQDay 3 after CABG, resting HR 82. While walking in the corridor HR reaches 118 and RPE is 14. What is the best next step?A. Continue; this is a normal response after surgeryB. Stop, rest, then resume at a slower paceC. Increase speed to reach a training effectD. Switch to the arm ergometer

B. HR is 36 above resting, beyond the "rest + 30" limit after surgery, and RPE is above 13. Reduce the intensity. Nothing suggests an emergency, so stopping completely and calling for help is not needed.

MCQA patient on bisoprolol starts Phase II. How do you best set the exercise intensity?A. 85% of 220 minus ageB. RPE 11–13 and the talk testC. Resting HR plus 40D. A maximal test after stopping the drug

B. A beta-blocker blunts the heart-rate response, so formulas based on age overestimate the target. Never stop cardiac drugs to test.

MCQECG shows ST elevation in II, III and aVF. Which problem do you watch for most during early mobilisation?A. Bradycardia or heart blockB. PneumothoraxC. Sternal instabilityD. Compartment syndrome

A. Inferior MI usually involves the right coronary artery, which supplies the SA and AV nodes in most people.

MCQ"Keep your move in the tube" after sternotomy means:A. No arm movement for 6 weeksB. Upper arms kept close to the body, pain-free, both arms working togetherC. Lifting up to 20 kg from the first weekD. Pushing up from a chair with both arms

B. It replaces fixed weight limits with pain-guided movement close to the trunk.
