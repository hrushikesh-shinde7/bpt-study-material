Mastectomy, lymphoedema and cancer surgery

Breast cancer surgery was asked in 16 of 20 papers (2016–2026), almost always about a working woman: a farm labourer, a teacher, a tiffin cook. Answer with the ICF, the week-by-week shoulder programme, lymphoedema prevention and lifelong arm care, and give the reason for each step.

My revision

1Definition

Mastectomy is surgical removal of the breast, usually for breast cancer. It is often combined with removal or sampling of the lymph nodes in the armpit (axilla).

Lymphoedema is chronic swelling caused by protein-rich fluid collecting in the tissues because the lymphatic system cannot drain it. After breast cancer treatment it affects the arm, hand, breast or chest wall on the operated side.

Lymphoedema stages (International Society of Lymphology)

StageFeatures

Lymphoedema is commonly diagnosed when the affected arm is 2 cm or more larger in circumference at any point, or about 10% or more larger in volume, than the other arm.

3Causes and risk factors

Breast cancer risk factors

  • Female sex, increasing age, family history, BRCA gene mutations.
  • Early first period, late menopause, no children or a first child after 30, not breastfeeding.
  • Obesity after menopause, alcohol, inactivity, hormone therapy, previous chest radiation.

Lymphoedema risk factors

  • Axillary node clearance (more nodes removed, higher risk) and radiotherapy to the axilla.
  • Infection or seroma in the arm or wound; obesity; reduced arm use; cellulitis.
  • Heavy, sudden overuse of the arm without building up gradually.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Surgery cuts skin, fascia, muscle attachments and lymph vessels, and removes axillary nodes.
  2. Pain and fear make the patient hold the arm against the chest, so the shoulder stiffens (capsule and scar tighten), especially in flexion, abduction and external rotation.
  3. Scar and radiotherapy fibrosis tie down the chest wall and axilla.
  4. Lymph drainage is reduced. When the lymph load is more than the remaining vessels can carry, protein-rich fluid collects: lymphoedema. The protein attracts water and later causes fibrosis and infection risk.
  5. Nerve injury during axillary surgery: long thoracic nerve (serratus anterior, so winging of the scapula), thoracodorsal nerve (latissimus dorsi), intercostobrachial nerve (numb inner upper arm).
  6. Axillary web syndrome: tight, painful cords from the armpit down the inner arm in the first weeks, from damaged lymph vessels; they limit abduction.
  7. Posture: the shoulder on the operated side rises and rolls forward; weight imbalance after removing a large breast can strain the neck and spine.
  8. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy add fatigue, low blood counts, nerve damage in the hands and feet, and skin and lung changes.
Recall check. Name the three nerves at risk in axillary surgery and the deficit each causes.

5Clinical features after surgery

  • Pain and tightness across the chest wall and axilla; drains in the first week.
  • Limited shoulder elevation, abduction and external rotation (S17: "shoulder joint restriction").
  • Swelling of the arm or hand on the operated side (S17: "mild oedema"; W20: "swelling in right upper limb").
  • Numbness of the inner upper arm; scapular winging if the long thoracic nerve was injured.
  • Cord-like bands in the axilla (axillary web syndrome).
  • Fatigue, nausea and hair loss during chemotherapy; skin redness during radiotherapy.
  • Anxiety, low mood, altered body image.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Surgery: mastectomy or breast-conserving surgery, sentinel node biopsy or axillary clearance, with or without reconstruction.
  • Chemotherapy: before (to shrink the tumour) or after surgery (W20 case).
  • Radiotherapy to the chest wall, breast or axilla.
  • Hormone therapy (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors) for hormone-positive cancers; aromatase inhibitors cause joint pain and bone loss.
  • Targeted therapy such as trastuzumab, which can affect the heart.

9PT assessment

  • Subjective: type of surgery and side, number of nodes removed, drains, reconstruction; chemotherapy or radiotherapy plans; pain, heaviness, numbness, tightness; hand dominance; occupation and household load (cooking, washing, lifting); children; patient's goals.
  • Observation: wound, drains, posture (shoulder height, protraction), scapular winging, swelling, skin colour.
  • Shoulder range: goniometry of flexion, abduction, external rotation, both sides.
  • Strength: shoulder and scapular muscles, grip.
  • Arm measurements: circumference at fixed points (for example every 4 cm from the wrist), compared with the other arm; volume calculation; pitting test; skin and tissue texture.
  • Sensation of the inner arm; axillary cords on palpation.
  • Outcome measures: DASH (arm function), shoulder ROM, arm volume, FACT-B (quality of life), fatigue scale, 6MWT.

Clues in the story (W21): heaviness of the arm and sleeves or rings that feel tight are early signs of lymphoedema, so measure both arms the same day. Redness, heat and fever in the arm suggest cellulitis, which needs urgent medical treatment before any massage or bandaging. Neck pain after surgery usually comes from guarding: a raised, protracted shoulder and a stiff neck.

12PT management

Before surgery

  • Measure shoulder range and both arms (baseline for lymphoedema).
  • Teach the first-week exercises, breathing exercises and posture; explain drains and what to expect.

Days 1–7 (drains in)

  • Deep breathing, supported cough, early walking.
  • Hand pumping (open and close the fist), wrist and elbow movements, neck and shoulder shrugs; 10 repetitions several times a day.
  • Shoulder flexion and abduction kept to about 90° while drains are in.
  • Rest the arm on a pillow when lying, with the hand slightly higher than the elbow.
Why limit the shoulder to about 90° in the first week?

A Cochrane review found that early exercise improves shoulder range, but starting full-range exercise straight after surgery increases wound drainage and seroma. Delaying full elevation by about a week reduces drainage, and shoulder function still recovers within about a month.

After drains are removed

  • Full active and active-assisted range: wand (stick) exercises, wall climbing with the fingers, pulley, hands behind the head, hands behind the back. Hold stretches 20–30 seconds, 3–5 repetitions, 2–3 times a day.
  • Scapular setting, retraction and posture correction; serratus anterior work if winging.
  • Scar massage once the wound is fully healed; stretches for axillary cords.
  • Continue stretching through radiotherapy to prevent fibrosis.

From about 6 weeks: strength, fitness and return to work

  • Progressive resistance training of the arms, shoulders and legs: start light under supervision and progress slowly.
  • Aerobic exercise such as brisk walking or cycling.
  • Work-specific practice: board writing, cooking, lifting children, carrying.

Lymphoedema: complete decongestive therapy (S21, 7 marks)

PhaseComponents
1. Intensive (reduction), daily for about 2–4 weeks
  • Manual lymph drainage (MLD): light, slow, skin-stretching strokes; first clear the neck, the other axilla and the trunk, then move fluid from the upper arm towards working lymph nodes, then the forearm and hand
  • Multilayer short-stretch compression bandaging, worn day and night
  • Exercises with the bandage on: hand pumping, elbow and shoulder movement, deep breathing (muscle pump)
  • Skin care: keep clean and moisturised; treat cuts and fungal infection early
2. Maintenance, lifelong
  • Compression sleeve (and glove if the hand swells) worn during the day, measured and replaced regularly
  • Simple self lymph drainage at home
  • Regular exercise, including supervised progressive resistance training
  • Skin care and weight control

Bandages used for lymphoedema (3 marks in S23)

  • Short-stretch (low-stretch) bandages: the main lymphoedema bandage. They stretch only a little, so they give low pressure at rest (safe to wear at night) and high pressure when muscles contract against them, which pumps fluid out of the arm.
  • Multilayer lymphoedema bandaging: tubular stockinette next to the skin, narrow gauze bandages on each finger, padding (soft synthetic wool or foam) to even out the arm's shape and protect bony points, then several layers of short-stretch bandage with more pressure at the hand than the upper arm.
  • Adjustable compression wraps (hook-and-loop, Velcro-type): the patient can put them on and tighten them herself; useful for self-management and maintenance.
  • Long-stretch (high-stretch, elastic) bandages are not used for lymphoedema: they keep high pressure even at rest and can act like a tourniquet.

Also: elevate the arm when resting; deep diaphragmatic breathing (helps central lymph flow); pneumatic compression only as an add-on. A 33-year-old receptionist (S21) needs workstation advice too: arm supported on the desk, regular movement breaks, sleeve during work.

Classic answer vs current evidence: lifting and the "at-risk" arm

Classic

Avoid lifting anything heavy with the arm for life; no weights; no BP cuffs, injections or blood tests on that side.

Current (ACSM 2019; supervised trials)

Slowly progressive, supervised resistance training is safe for women with or at risk of lymphoedema and does not make it worse. "Start low, progress slow." The evidence for avoiding BP cuffs and needles is weak, but many centres still advise using the other arm when possible.

Write in the exam

"Avoid sudden heavy or unaccustomed loading; build up arm strength gradually with supervised resistance exercise; protect the skin; use the other arm for injections where possible."

Breast prosthesis (3 marks in S17)

  • Temporary soft prosthesis (light, fibre-filled) worn in a soft bra from soon after surgery, because it does not press on the scar.
  • Permanent silicone prosthesis fitted once the wound has healed and swelling has settled (often around 6–8 weeks, or after radiotherapy skin has recovered). It matches the weight of the other breast. Worn in a mastectomy bra with a pocket; partial and adhesive types exist.
  • Why it matters: restores body symmetry and balance, prevents the shoulder rising and the spine tilting, helps clothes fit, improves confidence, and protects the chest wall.

13Precautions (3 marks in S21)

Precautions after mastectomy
  • Shoulder elevation limited to about 90° while drains are in; no vigorous stretching over a fresh wound.
  • Watch for seroma, wound infection and flap problems; report swelling, redness or discharge.
  • Protect the arm on the operated side: avoid cuts, burns, insect bites and tight sleeves or bangles.
  • No heat (hot packs, saunas) or vigorous massage on a lymphoedematous arm.
  • Build up lifting gradually; avoid sudden heavy loads.
  • Reconstruction: protect the flap donor site; follow the surgeon's limits on arm and trunk movement.
  • During chemotherapy: reduce or hold exercise on days with fever, very low blood counts, severe fatigue or vomiting; avoid crowded gyms when white cells are low.
  • Bone metastases: avoid heavy loading and twisting of affected bones.
  • No electrotherapy over active tumour sites.
Stop and refer urgently if
  • The arm becomes red, hot and painful with fever (cellulitis)
  • Sudden calf or arm swelling with pain (DVT)
  • New bone pain, or breathlessness

14Education and lifelong arm care

S19 asked "Why and how does she need to take care of her hand life long?"

Why: the lymph nodes removed from the armpit do not grow back. The arm always has less capacity to drain fluid, so any extra load (infection, burn, injury, heavy unaccustomed work) can start lymphoedema, even years later.

How:

  • Keep the skin clean and moisturised; dry well between the fingers.
  • Wear gloves for cooking, gardening and washing vessels. Use oven gloves and long-handled spoons (important for the tiffin cook).
  • Treat every cut, scratch or insect bite at once with antiseptic; see a doctor if it becomes red or hot.
  • Avoid tight bangles, rings, watches and tight blouse sleeves on that arm.
  • Build up heavy tasks slowly; take breaks; carry loads with both hands or a trolley.
  • Stay active and keep a healthy weight.
  • Measure or check the arm regularly; report heaviness, tightness or swelling early.
  • Wear the compression sleeve if prescribed, especially for long travel or heavy work.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

Prognosis for shoulder function is good when exercise starts early. Lymphoedema is a lifelong risk, and once established it can be controlled but rarely cured. Cancer prognosis depends on stage and tumour type.

Discharge from physio: full functional shoulder range; independent home programme; knows arm care and lymphoedema warning signs; compression garment fitted if needed; back to work and household roles.

LAQS19: why must Sunita care for her arm lifelong, and how?
