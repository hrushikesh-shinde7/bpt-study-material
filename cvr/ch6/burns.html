My revision

1Definition

A burn is an injury to the skin and deeper tissues caused by heat, electricity, chemicals, radiation or friction.

An inhalation injury is damage to the airway and lungs from breathing in hot gases, smoke or toxic products of burning.

The burn wound is described by three things: its cause, its depth, and its extent as a percentage of total body surface area (TBSA).

2Classification and grading

By cause

  • Thermal: flame (stove, kerosene, firecrackers), scald (hot liquids, commonest in children), contact (hot objects).
  • Electrical: low or high voltage, and lightning. Small skin wounds can hide deep muscle damage.
  • Chemical: acids and alkalis. Alkalis penetrate deeper.
  • Radiation and friction.
  • Inhalation injury, which often comes with face and neck burns.

By extent: Wallace's rule of nines (3 marks in W18; 3 marks inside S21)

  • Palm method: the patient's own palm with fingers is about 1% TBSA. Use it for small or scattered burns.
  • Children: the head is relatively bigger and the legs smaller, so use the Lund and Browder chart, which adjusts each region for age.
  • Count only partial- and full-thickness areas. Superficial (first-degree) burns are not counted.
Exam point: working out a case

S21: face, upper chest and both arms. If the whole of both arms (9 + 9), the front of the head (4.5) and the upper half of the front trunk (9) are burnt, the total is about 31.5%. Show the working; examiners give marks for the method.

By severity

A burn needs a specialist burn unit when any of these apply (classic American Burn Association referral criteria):

  • Partial-thickness burns over 10% TBSA
  • Any full-thickness burn
  • Burns of the face, hands, feet, genitals, perineum or over major joints
  • Electrical, chemical or inhalation injury
  • Burns in a patient with other serious illness or injury (for example the W17 electrician with rib fractures)

3Causes and risk factors

  • Kitchen burns: kerosene or gas stoves, cooking on the floor, loose synthetic clothing such as a dupatta or saree catching fire. Women are at high risk (S21 housemaid, S22 stove refuelling).
  • Scalds in children from hot water, tea and milk.
  • Firecrackers around festivals (W16 boy).
  • Electrical injury in electricians and linemen (W17, W20).
  • Chemical burns at work, and acid attacks.
  • Higher risk with epilepsy, alcohol use, old age and young childhood, crowded housing and poor fire safety.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

Papers ask "discuss the impairments with clinical reasoning" (W22). This chain is that reasoning.

Local wound: Jackson's three zones

  • Zone of coagulation (centre): tissue is dead and cannot recover.
  • Zone of stasis (around it): blood flow is reduced. It can recover with good resuscitation, or die if the patient is under-resuscitated or infected.
  • Zone of hyperaemia (outer): increased blood flow. Usually recovers fully.

Whole-body effects

  • Capillary leak: fluid and protein leak out of vessels, so the tissues swell and blood volume falls. In large burns (over about 20% TBSA) this causes burn shock in the first 24–48 hours.
  • Hypermetabolism: the body burns energy and breaks down muscle for months, which causes weight loss and weakness.
  • Lowered immunity and an open wound lead to infection and sepsis.
  • Inhalation injury: heat swells the upper airway; smoke chemicals damage the lower airways and alveoli; carbon monoxide and cyanide poison oxygen delivery.

Healing causes the long-term problems

Deep burns heal with scar. Cells in the scar called myofibroblasts pull the wound edges together. Scar keeps contracting for months, so it pulls joints into the position of comfort, which is usually flexion and adduction. This is why the position of comfort is the position of contracture.

Stages of wound healing (3 marks in S19; 3 marks inside W22)

5Clinical features

  • Burn wound appearance by depth (see section 2).
  • Pain: severe in superficial partial thickness; less in deep burns.
  • Swelling peaks around 24–48 hours, especially in the hands, face and neck.
  • Circumferential deep burns of the chest can restrict breathing; on a limb, they can cut off circulation.
  • Fever, fast heart rate and fast breathing from hypermetabolism.
Suspect inhalation injury when
  • The burn happened in a closed space (kitchen, room, vehicle).
  • There are burns of the face and neck, singed nasal hair or eyebrows, or soot in the mouth or nose.
  • Sputum is black (carbonaceous).
  • The voice is hoarse, or there is stridor, wheeze or breathlessness.
  • The patient is confused or drowsy (carbon monoxide).

Airway swelling can worsen over the first day, so these patients are watched closely and often intubated early.

Electrical burns

  • Entry and exit wounds can look small while muscles along the current's path are badly damaged.
  • Risks: heart arrhythmias, compartment syndrome, kidney damage from muscle breakdown (dark urine from myoglobin), nerve damage, and fractures from falls or strong muscle contraction.

7Medical and surgical management

First aid

  • Stop the burning: remove the person from the source, take off burnt clothing and jewellery.
  • Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do not use ice, toothpaste or butter.
  • Cover with clean cling film or a clean cloth; keep the patient warm.

Hospital care

  • Airway and breathing first; early intubation if inhalation injury is suspected; high-flow oxygen for CO poisoning.
  • Fluid resuscitation for large burns (about 20% TBSA or more in adults), guided by urine output.
  • Pain relief, tetanus cover, high-protein and high-calorie feeding.
  • Wound care: cleaning, dressings (for example silver-based dressings), and daily review.
  • Escharotomy: cutting through tight full-thickness eschar on the chest or a limb to free breathing or circulation. Fasciotomy if a compartment is tight.
  • Early excision and grafting: dead tissue is removed and the wound is covered, usually with a split-thickness skin graft taken from a donor site such as the thigh. Grafts may be meshed to cover a larger area. Full-thickness grafts and flaps are used for the face, hands and joints.
  • Later reconstruction: contracture release, Z-plasty and further grafts.

Classic answer vs current guideline: fluid formula

Classic (Parkland formula)

4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA of Ringer's lactate in 24 hours. Half in the first 8 hours from the time of the burn, half over the next 16 hours.

Current (American Burn Association)

Start lower, at 2 mL/kg/%TBSA, and adjust the rate to keep urine output around 0.5 mL/kg/hour in adults. Giving too much fluid causes swelling, compartment syndrome and lung problems.

Write in the exam

"Parkland formula 4 mL/kg/%TBSA, half in 8 hours; current guidance starts at 2–4 mL/kg/%TBSA and titrates to urine output 0.5 mL/kg/h."

9PT assessment

Subjective

  • How, when and where the burn happened: closed space? electrical? first aid given?
  • Pain (rest, movement, dressings), itching, breathlessness, cough and sputum.
  • Surgery done or planned: grafts and donor sites, escharotomy.
  • Social history (fills the ICF): occupation and hand dominance, earning role, children at home, house type, toilet, family support.
  • Patient's goals and fears, including appearance.

Objective

  • Vital signs: HR, BP, RR, temperature, SpO₂ (remember the CO caution).
  • Chest: breathing pattern, chest expansion (eschar may restrict it), auscultation, cough and sputum.
  • Burn chart: sites, depth, TBSA, areas crossing joints, grafts and donor sites.
  • Oedema of the hands, face and limbs.
  • ROM with a goniometer (active first; passive only where allowed), muscle strength, sensation.
  • Function: bed mobility, transfers, walking, self-care, hand function.
  • Scar (rehabilitation phase): height, flexibility, colour and blood supply.

12PT management

Write it phase by phase. Papers ask for short-term and long-term management, which map onto these phases.

Phase 1: emergent or resuscitative (first 72 hours)

  • Respiratory care: head of bed raised (sit up at least 30°), deep breathing, thoracic expansion exercises, ACBT, assisted cough, suction if needed. If ventilated: positioning, secretion clearance, manual techniques where skin allows.
  • Oedema control: elevate burnt limbs above the heart (hands on pillows or slings), with active finger and ankle exercises.
  • Positioning and splinting in anti-deformity positions from day 1 (table below).
  • Range of motion: active exercises of every burnt joint, about every hour while awake. Use passive or active-assisted movement if the patient cannot move. Give analgesia before sessions.
  • Mobility: sitting out and walking as soon as the patient is stable.

Phase 2: acute (until the wounds are closed)

  • Continue ROM, positioning and splinting. Time exercise with dressing changes, when the wound is visible and analgesia is given.
  • After skin grafting: immobilise the grafted area, usually for about 3–5 days (the surgeon decides). Keep moving all other joints. Then restart gentle active ROM over the graft. Avoid friction and shear on new grafts.
  • Donor sites (often the thigh) hurt but can usually be moved early.
  • Lower-limb burns or grafts: wrap the leg in a compression bandage before standing, because dependent legs swell and grafts can fail.
  • Strengthening, walking, and self-care training (eating, washing) with built-up handles if grip is weak.

Phase 3: rehabilitation (after wound closure, while scar matures over 1–2 years)

  • Stretching: slow, sustained stretches across scar until it blanches, held 20–30 seconds or longer, several times a day.
  • Scar management: pressure garments, silicone gel sheets, scar massage once the skin is strong enough, moisturising.
  • Splints: night splints to hold gains; dynamic splints or serial casting for established contractures.
  • Strength and endurance: progressive resistance, aerobic training, hand therapy, fine motor practice.
  • Return to work: work-specific tasks, graded loading of the hands, sun protection outdoors.
  • Psychological support and peer support; cosmetic camouflage advice.

Role of splinting (S18): splints hold the anti-deformity position when the patient cannot, protect exposed tendons and new grafts, and keep the range gained by exercise. In the acute phase they are worn at rest and at night and removed for exercise. Check them daily for pressure and fit, because swelling changes.

Inhalation burns (7 marks in W22)

  • Upright positioning to reduce airway swelling and help breathing.
  • Humidified oxygen; high-flow oxygen for carbon monoxide.
  • Deep breathing, ACBT, huffing, assisted cough, suction. Mobilise early.
  • If ventilated: regular repositioning, airway clearance, careful bagging with suction.
  • Watch for worsening: stridor, rising breathing rate, falling oxygen. Call the team early.

Deep burns all the way round a limb can tighten like a band and cut off the blood supply. Check toe colour, warmth and pulses, and report quickly (the surgeon may release the eschar).

Burns in pregnancy (S24: full-thickness burns of the right leg, 6 months pregnant)

There are two patients: the mother and the baby. The baby does well only if the mother's oxygen, blood pressure and nutrition stay good, so involve the obstetric team and report any warning sign at once.

  • Position: from about 20 weeks, do not leave her flat on her back, because the uterus presses on the inferior vena cava and lowers her blood pressure. Use left side-lying or a wedge under the right hip (left tilt). With a right-leg burn, left side-lying also keeps the burnt leg on top, supported on pillows with the knee straight and the ankle at 90°.
  • DVT: pregnancy, burns and bed rest all raise the risk. Ankle pumps and active exercise of the good leg every hour, early walking, and anticoagulants as prescribed.
  • Chest: the uterus pushes the diaphragm up, so lung volumes fall. Deep breathing and thoracic expansion in side-lying or sitting.
  • Burnt leg: the same lower-limb plan as above, but no electrotherapy or heat over the abdomen, pelvis or lower back, and no shortwave diathermy.
  • Exercise: moderate effort, avoid breath-holding and straining. Stop and report bleeding, abdominal pain, contractions, leaking fluid or reduced baby movements.
  • Emotional support: she is crying and afraid for her baby. Explain each step, give treatment after analgesia, and involve her family.

Classic answer vs current evidence: pressure garments

Classic

Every deep burn gets a custom pressure garment, worn about 23 hours a day at about 20–25 mmHg, for 12–18 months until the scar matures.

Current (Cochrane 2024)

Still standard practice, but the evidence that garments improve scars is very uncertain. Comfort and wearing them regularly matter. Silicone and massage are used alongside.

Write in the exam

"Pressure garment therapy about 23 hours a day for 12–18 months, with silicone gel and scar massage."

Electrotherapy for wound healing (S16, S22) is covered on the diabetes and wound-healing page.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Fresh grafts: no movement of the grafted area until the surgeon allows; no shearing or friction.
  • Exposed tendons: no aggressive or combined stretching; splint the finger straight over an exposed extensor hood.
  • Heterotopic ossification: no forceful passive movement of a hot, painful, stiffening joint.
  • Haemodynamic instability in the first days: keep exercise gentle and monitored.
  • CO poisoning: pulse oximetry can be falsely normal.
  • Electrical burns: check ECG monitoring and cardiac stability before mobilising.
  • Infection control: wash hands, wear gloves and a gown, clean equipment between patients.
  • Associated injuries: fractures, rib fractures and head injury (W17 case) change the plan.
Stop and report if
  • Breathing becomes harder, stridor appears, or oxygen falls
  • Blood pressure falls, or the heart rate becomes fast or irregular
  • A graft bleeds, lifts or shears
  • A limb becomes tight, numb or very painful

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

Do's and don'ts for the patient

  • Do your stretches and exercises several times every day, even after discharge. Scar keeps tightening for 1–2 years.
  • Wear your splints and pressure garments as advised. Wash garments daily; get them refitted when they loosen.
  • Moisturise healed skin and massage the scar several times a day.
  • Protect healed skin from the sun with clothing and sunscreen; new skin burns and darkens easily.
  • Do not scratch itchy scars. Use moisturiser, cool compresses and medicines the doctor gives.
  • Protect numb areas from heat and injury.
  • Report any blister, wound or ulcer in a scar that does not heal.

Preventing burns at home

  • Cook on a raised platform, not on the floor. Never refuel a lit stove.
  • Wear close-fitting cotton clothes while cooking; tuck in the dupatta or saree end.
  • Keep children away from the kitchen, hot liquids and firecrackers.
  • Know first aid: stop, drop and roll; cool running water for 20 minutes.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

Prognosis is worse with larger TBSA, deeper burns, older age and inhalation injury. The revised Baux score estimates risk of death as age + %TBSA + 17 if there is an inhalation injury.

Discharge from the burn unit: wounds healed or manageable at home; independent or supported self-care and walking; family taught positioning, splints, exercises and skin care; follow-up arranged for scar and contracture review.

3 mWrite the rule of nine.

Head and neck 9%; each upper limb 9%; front of trunk 18%; back of trunk 18%; each lower limb 18%; perineum 1%. Total 100%. Draw the front and back figure.

3 mCharacteristics of deep partial-thickness burns.
  • Reaches the deep dermis.
  • Blotchy red and white, drier, blisters may be absent; slow or no blanching.
  • Less painful than superficial partial thickness; pressure is felt.
  • Heals in more than 3 weeks with a high risk of hypertrophic scar; often needs grafting.
7 mPositioning strategies to prevent deformities in burns.

Principle: the position of comfort is the position of contracture, so stretch the burnt surface. Then write the anti-deformity table: neck extension, shoulder abduction about 90°, elbow extension, safe hand position, hip extension with slight abduction, knee extension, ankle at 90°, mouth stretching. Add splints, the wearing schedule (rest and night, off for exercise) and daily checks.

LAQW22: 23-year-old mother, 40% burns of both thighs and legs. List the impairments with clinical reasoning.
  • Pain: exposed nerve endings in partial-thickness burns.
  • Oedema of the legs: capillary leak; worse when the legs hang down.
  • Risk of hip flexion, knee flexion and ankle plantarflexion contractures: position of comfort and scar contraction.
  • Reduced walking and standing tolerance: pain, oedema, weakness; grafts on the legs need compression before standing.
  • Risk of DVT and pressure sores: bed rest.
  • Weakness and fatigue: hypermetabolism.
  • Participation: cannot care for her young child or do housework; anxiety about appearance.
MCQA patient with dorsal hand burns should be splinted with:A. Wrist flexed, fingers flexedB. Wrist extended, MCP joints flexed, finger joints straight, thumb abductedC. Wrist neutral, all finger joints straightD. Hand in a fist around a roll

B. This "safe position" keeps the collateral ligaments of the MCP joints stretched and protects the extensor tendons over the PIP joints.

MCQAfter a kitchen fire, a patient's SpO₂ reads 99% but he is confused and has soot in his nose. What is the best next step?A. Reassure; oxygenation is normalB. Arterial blood gas with carboxyhaemoglobinC. Start a 6-minute walk testD. Begin chest percussion

B. Carbon monoxide makes the pulse oximeter read falsely high. A blood gas measures carboxyhaemoglobin directly.

MCQA split-thickness skin graft was placed over the elbow yesterday. What should physiotherapy do for that elbow?A. Full passive stretching twice dailyB. Keep it immobilised as the surgeon advises, and exercise other jointsC. Resisted elbow flexionD. Massage the graft

B. A new graft needs a few days without movement or shear to "take". Movement restarts after the surgeon clears it, usually around day 3–5.
