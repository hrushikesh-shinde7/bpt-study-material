Lung surgery, thoracotomy, chest drain and chest trauma

My revision

1Definition

Thoracic surgery is surgery on the lungs, pleura, chest wall, oesophagus or mediastinum. Physiotherapy matters because every chest operation reduces lung volumes and cough strength and stiffens the shoulder and trunk on the operated side.

An intercostal chest drain (ICD) is a tube passed between the ribs into the pleural space to remove air, blood, fluid or pus, connected to an underwater seal that lets air and fluid out but not back in.

Lung operations

  • Pneumonectomy: whole lung removed.
  • Lobectomy: one lobe removed (commonest for lung cancer); bilobectomy on the right.
  • Segmentectomy: one or more segments removed (S22: stage 1 lung cancer).
  • Wedge resection: a small wedge of lung.
  • Sleeve resection: a lobe with a section of main bronchus, rejoined.
  • Decortication: removal of a thick fibrous peel from the lung (chronic empyema).
  • Pleurectomy and pleurodesis: to stop recurrent pneumothorax or effusion.
  • Bullectomy, lung volume reduction, thoracoplasty (see the TB page), lung transplant.

3Indications

  • Lung cancer (commonest), carcinoid and other tumours; risk factors are smoking, occupational exposure (asbestos), air pollution and family history.
  • Localised bronchiectasis, lung abscess, aspergilloma, complications of TB.
  • Hydatid cyst (W16 case): a parasitic cyst from dog tapeworm, common in farming communities.
  • Empyema needing decortication; recurrent pneumothorax.
  • Chest trauma: rib fractures, flail chest, haemothorax, pneumothorax, lung contusion, penetrating wounds.

4Pathophysiology to impairments after thoracic surgery

  1. Anaesthesia and lying flat reduce functional residual capacity; small airways close at the lung bases.
  2. Pain from the incision, rib spreading and drains makes breathing shallow and stops deep breaths and effective coughing.
  3. Lung tissue is removed, so total lung capacity and exercise reserve fall; the remaining lung must expand to fill the space.
  4. Sputum is increased (surgery, smoking history) but cannot be cleared: sputum retention, then atelectasis and pneumonia.
  5. Cut shoulder-girdle muscles and guarding cause shoulder stiffness and a posture bent towards the operated side.
  6. Bed rest and drains reduce mobility and fitness and raise DVT risk.

5Clinical features after surgery

  • Incision and drain-site pain, worse on deep breathing and coughing.
  • Shallow, fast breathing; reduced chest expansion and breath sounds on the operated side; crackles if secretions are retained.
  • Weak, painful cough with sputum (W16: "cough with expectoration").
  • Drains in place; oxygen; drips; possibly an epidural or nerve-block catheter.
  • Stiff shoulder on the operated side, posture leaning towards the incision.
  • Fatigue and breathlessness on exertion.

7Medical and surgical management

Intercostal drain and underwater seal (7 marks in W20; part of W22)

  • Indications: pneumothorax, haemothorax, pleural effusion, empyema, after thoracic surgery.
  • Site: usually the "safe triangle": bordered by the front edge of latissimus dorsi, the side edge of pectoralis major, and a line at the level of the nipple (about the 5th intercostal space), with its apex below the axilla.
  • Underwater seal: the tube from the chest ends about 2 cm under water in the drainage bottle. Air and fluid can bubble out, but water blocks air from being sucked back. The bottle must stay below the level of the chest at all times.
  • Swinging of the water in the tube with breathing shows the drain is open and in the pleural space. Bubbling shows an air leak from the lung. No swing may mean the tube is kinked or blocked, or the lung has fully expanded.
  • Suction (low negative pressure) may be added to help re-expand the lung.

Other medical care

  • Pain control: epidural, paravertebral or intercostal nerve block, patient-controlled analgesia, regular oral analgesia.
  • Oxygen, nebulisers, antibiotics as needed.
  • Enhanced recovery programmes: early eating, early drain removal, walking on day 1.
  • For cancer: chemotherapy or radiotherapy after surgery where needed.

9PT assessment

  • Pre-op: lung function, exercise tolerance, smoking, sputum, shoulder range, occupation and hand dominance; teach the post-op plan.
  • Post-op chart: operation, incision, drains (number, site, swing, bubbling, amount and type of drainage), analgesia, oxygen, X-ray.
  • Respiratory: breathing pattern and rate, SpO₂, chest expansion, auscultation, cough and sputum.
  • Pain at rest and on coughing (VAS).
  • Musculoskeletal: shoulder range on the operated side, posture.
  • Function: bed mobility, sitting, standing, walking distance with SpO₂.
  • Outcome measures: 6MWT, shoulder ROM, pain scale, quality of life (for example EORTC questionnaire for cancer).

12PT management

Before surgery

  • Explain the operation, drains and the post-op routine; teach breathing exercises, ACBT, supported cough, bed mobility and shoulder exercises.
  • Stop smoking as early as possible.
  • Prehabilitation: aerobic and strength training for a few weeks before lung cancer surgery reduces post-op lung complications and hospital stay (Cochrane review).

After surgery (lobectomy or segmentectomy)

DayPhysiotherapy
Day 0–1
  • Sit upright; high side-lying alternating sides (after lobectomy either side is allowed)
  • Deep breathing with a 3-second hold, thoracic expansion to the operated side, ACBT
  • Supported huff and cough: hold a pillow or the patient's arm over the incision and drain
  • Ankle pumps; sitting out of bed and marching on the spot or walking with the drain
  • Active-assisted shoulder movement on the operated side within pain
Day 2–4
  • Progress walking distance 3–4 times a day; stairs before discharge
  • Active shoulder flexion and abduction, trunk side-bending away from the incision and rotation
  • Posture correction in a mirror
  • Continue airway clearance until the chest is clear
After drain removal and discharge
  • Home walking programme, increasing weekly
  • Shoulder stretches and strengthening; thoracic mobility
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation or exercise training for lung cancer survivors
  • Graded return to heavy work over about 6–8 weeks (no heavy lifting at first)
After pneumonectomy

Positioning is restricted: lie on the back or on the operated side (or a quarter turn towards it). Avoid lying fully on the remaining lung side, because fluid in the empty space could spill through a leaking bronchial stump into the only lung. Avoid head-down tilt. Breathing exercises, cough and early walking as above.

Physiotherapy with an intercostal drain

  • Check before every session: swing, bubbling, type and amount of drainage, tubing not kinked.
  • Keep the bottle upright and below chest level; carry it low when walking (or use a portable drain).
  • Never clamp a drain that is bubbling.
  • If the tube disconnects: put the chest end under sterile water or clamp briefly as per ward protocol, and call the nurse. If the drain falls out: cover the hole with a sealed dressing and call for help.
  • Support the drain site during coughing; do shoulder exercises gently near the drain.

Classic answer vs current evidence: incentive spirometry

Classic

Every patient after thoracic or abdominal surgery uses an incentive spirometer hourly.

Current (AARC 2011)

Not recommended alone or routinely. Deep breathing, airway clearance and early walking are the core. It can be an add-on to encourage deep breaths.

Write in the exam

"Deep breathing exercises, ACBT and early mobilisation; incentive spirometer as an adjunct."

Chest trauma (W17 electrician with 3 rib fractures, on ventilator)

  • Good pain control (nerve blocks, epidural) is the key to breathing and coughing.
  • On the ventilator: positioning, suction, airway clearance, careful manual techniques away from fractures, limb exercises.
  • After extubation: deep breathing, supported cough, early walking, shoulder exercises.
  • No percussion or vibration over fractured ribs; flail chest is covered on the chest-wall page.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Drain precautions as above; never lift the bottle above the chest.
  • After pneumonectomy: avoid full side-lying on the remaining lung, head-down tilt and vigorous manual techniques.
  • Fresh bronchial stump: avoid high-pressure techniques such as aggressive manual hyperinflation or high-level PEP.
  • No percussion over fractured ribs, incisions or drains.
  • Epidural: check leg power and BP before standing.
Stop and report if
  • Sudden breathlessness or chest pain (pneumothorax, PE)
  • Heavy fresh blood in the drain or coughed up
  • New irregular pulse or falling BP
  • Rapidly spreading surgical emphysema

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Keep doing deep breathing and huffing for a few weeks after going home.
  • Walk every day, increasing the distance gradually.
  • Do shoulder and trunk exercises until the range is full.
  • No heavy lifting for the first weeks; return to heavy work gradually as advised.
  • Stop smoking for good.
  • Report fever, more breathlessness, wound redness or discharge.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

Prognosis: most patients recover well after lobectomy or segmentectomy, especially after VATS. Lung function settles at a lower level depending on how much lung was removed. Cancer prognosis depends on stage.

Discharge: drains out; chest clear; walking independently and managing stairs; shoulder range near full; knows home exercises and warning signs.

3 mEnumerate the muscles incised in posterolateral thoracotomy.

Latissimus dorsi; serratus anterior (may be spared); trapezius (lower fibres); rhomboids (if extended up); intercostal muscles of the chosen space (usually the 5th).

7 mIntercostal drainage and underwater seal.

Define; indications; site (safe triangle); parts of the system with a diagram; principle of the underwater seal (tube about 2 cm under water, one-way); swing and bubbling meaning; suction; precautions (bottle below chest, never clamp a bubbling drain, no kinks); physiotherapy with a drain.

MCQA post-lobectomy patient's drain is bubbling continuously. During a walk, the best action is:A. Clamp the tube to stop the leakB. Keep the bottle upright below chest level and do not clampC. Lift the bottle onto the bedD. Avoid walking until the bubbling stops

B. Clamping a bubbling drain can cause a tension pneumothorax. Walking is encouraged with the bottle kept low.

MCQAfter a right pneumonectomy, which position should be avoided?A. SupineB. Lying fully on the left sideC. Quarter-turn towards the rightD. Sitting upright

B. Lying fully on the remaining (left) lung risks fluid from the empty space spilling into it through a leaking stump.

MCQWhich measure most reduces lung complications after lung cancer surgery?A. Routine incentive spirometry aloneB. Bed rest for 3 daysC. Pre-op exercise training, deep breathing and early mobilisationD. Head-down postural drainage

C. Prehabilitation and early mobilisation reduce complications; incentive spirometry alone is not recommended.
