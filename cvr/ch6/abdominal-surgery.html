Abdominal surgery: pre- and post-operative care

Abdominal surgery was asked in 5 papers as 3-mark incisions and 7-mark questions on pre-operative physiotherapy, gastrectomy and complications. The key idea: upper abdominal surgery stops deep breathing and coughing, so physiotherapy prevents lung complications, and one pre-operative session makes a big difference.

My revision

1Definition

Abdominal surgery includes operations on the stomach, intestines, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, spleen, kidneys and abdominal wall, done open (laparotomy) or by keyhole surgery (laparoscopy). Post-operative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are lung problems after surgery, such as atelectasis and pneumonia, that delay recovery.

Exam point

Upper abdominal incisions (upper midline, subcostal, rooftop) cause more pain on breathing and more lung complications than lower or laparoscopic ones, because they are close to the diaphragm.

3Risk factors for lung complications

  • Surgery: upper abdominal or thoracic site, open rather than laparoscopic, long operation, emergency surgery, nasogastric tube.
  • Patient: older age, smoking, COPD or asthma, obesity, poor fitness, poor nutrition, heart failure, sleep apnoea, poor general health.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. General anaesthesia relaxes the diaphragm and reduces functional residual capacity; small airways at the lung bases close.
  2. After surgery, wound pain stops deep breaths and coughing; the diaphragm works poorly for days after upper abdominal surgery.
  3. Opioid painkillers and lying flat reduce breathing further; secretions are retained.
  4. Result: atelectasis, low oxygen, and later pneumonia.
  5. Bed rest adds DVT risk, weakness and loss of fitness; abdominal muscles cut in the incision weaken trunk support.

5Clinical features after surgery

  • Wound pain, shallow fast breathing, weak cough, reduced breath sounds at the bases, low SpO₂ if atelectasis.
  • Drains, catheter, nasogastric tube, drips; nausea; bowel not working for a few days (ileus).
  • Fatigue and weakness, especially after major cancer surgery.

6Investigations

  • Pre-op: lung function in high-risk patients, fitness tests, haemoglobin, albumin.
  • Post-op: chest X-ray (basal atelectasis, raised diaphragm, effusion), ABG or SpO₂, blood counts, inflammatory markers.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Good pain control: epidural, wound catheters, regular analgesia.
  • Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS): pre-op education, short fasting, minimal drains, early eating, early mobilisation on the day of surgery or day 1, early removal of tubes.
  • DVT prophylaxis, antibiotics, nutrition.
  • Gastrectomy (S16): removal of part or all of the stomach (often for cancer) through an upper midline or rooftop incision: high risk of lung complications; small frequent meals afterwards.

9PT assessment

  • Pre-op: type and site of surgery, risk factors (smoking, lung disease, obesity, fitness), breathing pattern, cough, baseline mobility.
  • Post-op: chart (operation, incision, drains, analgesia), pain on coughing, breathing rate, SpO₂, chest expansion, auscultation, sputum, bed mobility, sitting and walking ability.
  • Outcome measures: incidence of chest complications, SpO₂ on air, walking distance, time to independent mobility, length of stay.

10PT diagnosis by ICF

Practice case built from past questions (not a single paper): a 58-year-old smoker, day 1 after open partial gastrectomy for cancer through an upper midline incision.

  • Impairments: incisional pain, shallow breathing and reduced lung bases (atelectasis risk), weak cough with sputum, fatigue, weakness, DVT risk.
  • Activity limitations: bed mobility, sitting up, walking, self-care.
  • Participation: work and family roles suspended during recovery and any chemotherapy.
  • Environmental factors: drains and tubes (barrier), ERAS ward routine and family support (facilitators).
  • Personal factors: smoking, age, anxiety about cancer.

11Problem list and goals

  • Short-term (days): no chest complications; effective supported cough; sitting out on day 1; walking in the ward on day 1–2.
  • Long-term (weeks): walking independently outdoors; back to daily activities; no hernia; return to work as advised.

12PT management

Pre-operative physiotherapy (7 marks in W16 and W20)

  • Assessment to find high-risk patients.
  • Education: what will happen after surgery, why breathing and early walking matter, pain relief.
  • Teach and practise: deep breathing with a 3–5 second hold, ACBT, supported cough (hands or pillow over the wound), log-roll bed mobility, ankle pumps, getting up from bed.
  • Prehabilitation for high-risk patients: aerobic and strength training, and inspiratory muscle training, for a few weeks before surgery.
  • Stop smoking as early as possible.
Exam point: the evidence for pre-op physio

In the LIPPSMAck-POP trial (441 patients having upper abdominal surgery), a single 30-minute pre-operative physiotherapy education and breathing-exercise session roughly halved post-operative pulmonary complications (adjusted hazard ratio 0.48). Quote this for "importance of pre-operative chest physiotherapy".

Post-operative physiotherapy

WhenPhysiotherapy
Day 0–1
  • Sit upright or high side-lying; avoid slumping
  • Deep breaths with a hold, 5–10 every hour while awake; ACBT if sputum
  • Supported huff and cough
  • Ankle pumps, quadriceps and gluteal sets
  • Log-roll and sit over the edge of the bed; sit out; walk with help
Day 2 to discharge
  • Increase walking 3–4 times a day; stairs if needed at home
  • Continue breathing exercises until fully mobile
  • Gentle trunk mobility; posture correction
After discharge
  • Daily walking, increasing gradually
  • Gentle abdominal (core) activation; progress after about 6 weeks
  • No heavy lifting for about 6 weeks

Classic answer vs current evidence: incentive spirometry

Classic

Incentive spirometry hourly for every upper abdominal surgery patient.

Current (AARC 2011)

Not recommended alone or routinely. Deep breathing, coughing and early mobilisation are the core; the spirometer can be an add-on.

Write in the exam

"Pre-op education and breathing exercises, then deep breathing, supported cough and early mobilisation; incentive spirometry only as an adjunct."

13Precautions

  • Always support the wound during coughing and moving.
  • Check drains, catheter and lines before moving; keep bags below the wound.
  • Epidural: check leg strength and BP before standing.
  • Watch for postural hypotension, bleeding, sudden breathlessness (PE).
  • No heavy lifting or strong abdominal exercises for about 6 weeks.
  • No percussion near fresh abdominal wounds and drains.

14Education and home programme

  • Keep walking every day; increase gradually.
  • Support the wound with a pillow when coughing or laughing.
  • Get out of bed by rolling onto your side first.
  • Avoid lifting heavy objects (such as water buckets or gas cylinders) for about 6 weeks.
  • Stop smoking; eat well for wound healing.
  • Report fever, wound discharge, calf pain or sudden breathlessness.

15Prognosis

Most patients recover well with good pain control and early mobilisation. Lung complications lengthen hospital stay and can be largely prevented by pre-operative teaching and early activity.

3 mAny three incisions for abdominal surgery.

Midline (laparotomy); Kocher's right subcostal (gallbladder); McBurney's gridiron (appendix); Pfannenstiel (caesarean, pelvic). Also paramedian, rooftop, transverse.

7 mImportance of pre-operative chest physiotherapy in major abdominal surgery.

Why lungs suffer after upper abdominal surgery; risk factors; content of the pre-op session (education, deep breathing, ACBT, supported cough, bed mobility, early mobilisation plan, prehab, smoking cessation); evidence (one session halved complications in LIPPSMAck-POP); benefits (fewer complications, shorter stay, less anxiety).

MCQWhich surgery carries the highest risk of lung complications?A. Laparoscopic appendicectomyB. Open upper abdominal gastrectomyC. Inguinal hernia repairD. Pfannenstiel caesarean section

B. Upper abdominal open incisions most affect the diaphragm and coughing.
