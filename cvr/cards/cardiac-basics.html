Cardiac anatomy and physiology for physiotherapists

My revision

1Anatomy: chambers, valves and coronary arteries

  • The heart lies in the middle mediastinum; the apex beat is normally in the left 5th intercostal space in the mid-clavicular line.
  • Right side (right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → lungs) pumps at low pressure; left side (left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → body) pumps at high pressure, so the left ventricular wall is the thickest.
  • Pericardium: a two-layered sac; fluid in it (effusion, tamponade) restricts filling.

Veins drain into the coronary sinus. Nerves: sympathetic (β1) speeds the heart and strengthens contraction; the vagus (parasympathetic) slows it.

3Cardiac cycle and heart sounds

  • Systole (contraction): isovolumetric contraction, then ejection. Diastole (relaxation): isovolumetric relaxation, then filling (rapid filling, then atrial contraction tops it up).
  • Typical pressures: left ventricle 120/0–10 mmHg, aorta 120/80; right ventricle 25/0–5, pulmonary artery 25/10.
  • S1 ("lub"): mitral and tricuspid valves close (start of systole). S2 ("dub"): aortic and pulmonary valves close (start of diastole); it splits on breathing in.
  • S3: rapid filling into a failing, dilated ventricle (heart failure; can be normal in the young). S4: atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle (hypertension, ischaemia).
  • The left ventricle's own blood supply flows mainly in diastole, because contraction squeezes the coronary vessels. At rest diastole is about two-thirds of each beat; a fast heart rate shortens it, so less blood reaches the heart muscle while it needs more. This is why tachycardia brings on angina and why beta-blockers help.

4Cardiac output and its regulation

TermMeaningTypical value
Cardiac output (CO)Heart rate × stroke volumeAbout 5 L/min at rest; about 20–25 L/min in maximal exercise in fit young adults
Stroke volume (SV)End-diastolic volume − end-systolic volumeAbout 70 mL at rest
Ejection fraction (EF)SV ÷ end-diastolic volumeAbout 55–70%; 40% or below is reduced (heart failure grades on the valve and heart failure page)
PreloadStretch of the ventricle before it contracts (filling)Raised by venous return: muscle pump, breathing, lying down
AfterloadResistance the ventricle pumps againstRaised by high BP and aortic stenosis
ContractilityStrength of contraction at a given stretchRaised by sympathetic drive; lowered by heart muscle damage
  • Frank–Starling law: the more the ventricle is filled (stretched), the more strongly it contracts, up to a limit. A failing heart sits on a flatter curve.
  • Blood pressure = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance. Mean arterial pressure ≈ diastolic + one-third of (systolic − diastolic); for 120/80, about 93 mmHg.
  • Baroreceptor reflex (carotid sinus and aortic arch): a fall in BP on standing quickly raises heart rate and narrows vessels. After bed rest, with some drugs and with autonomic damage it fails, giving postural hypotension.
  • Myocardial oxygen demand rises with heart rate, BP, wall tension and contractility. Heart rate × systolic BP (the rate–pressure product) is a bedside guide; angina appears at a fairly fixed product for each patient.

Test yourself

3 mWhy does tachycardia worsen myocardial ischaemia?

The heart muscle is perfused mainly in diastole; a fast rate shortens diastole, so supply falls while demand (rate × pressure) rises.

MCQST elevation in II, III and aVF usually means a block in the:A. LADB. Circumflex onlyC. Right coronary arteryD. Left main

C. Inferior wall, usually the right coronary artery.

MCQCardiac output at rest is about:A. 1 L/minB. 5 L/minC. 15 L/minD. 25 L/min

B. About 70 beats × 70 mL ≈ 5 L/min.
