Cardiac and respiratory drugs: what physiotherapists need to know

Patients in every long case are on several medicines, and each one changes what you see and do: beta-blockers flatten the heart-rate response, diuretics cause postural dizziness, anticoagulants make percussion risky, a bronchodilator before airway clearance makes it work better. This page is organised by what the drug means for physiotherapy, not by pharmacology.

1Heart and blood-pressure drugs

Group (examples)What it doesPhysiotherapy meaning
Beta-blockers (metoprolol, bisoprolol, atenolol, carvedilol)Slow the heart and lower BP; reduce angina and arrhythmiasLower resting and exercise heart rate: Karvonen targets overestimate, so use RPE and the talk test; fatigue; non-selective ones can trigger bronchospasm in asthma
ACE inhibitors and ARBs (ramipril, enalapril, losartan, telmisartan); sacubitril–valsartanLower BP; protect the heart and kidneys in heart failure and after MIPostural and post-exercise hypotension: longer cool-down; dry cough with ACE inhibitors
Diuretics (furosemide, torsemide, spironolactone, hydrochlorothiazide)Remove salt and water (heart failure, oedema, BP)Dehydration, dizziness on standing, low potassium (cramps, arrhythmias); urgency: plan sessions after the morning dose has worked
Nitrates (glyceryl trinitrate spray or tablet under the tongue, isosorbide)Widen veins and coronary arteries; relieve anginaPatient should carry GTN to sessions; take it sitting (it drops BP); headache; use before a known trigger if prescribed
Calcium-channel blockers (amlodipine; diltiazem, verapamil)Widen arteries; diltiazem and verapamil also slow the heartAnkle swelling (amlodipine), hypotension; slower heart-rate response (diltiazem, verapamil)
Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor)Stop platelets clumping; protect stents and graftsBruising and bleeding; never advise stopping them
Anticoagulants (heparin, enoxaparin, warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran)Prevent or treat clots (DVT, PE, atrial fibrillation, mechanical valves)Bleeding risk: gentle handling, care with percussion, suction and falls; check INR with warfarin (often 2–3; higher for mechanical valves)
Clot-dissolving drugs (streptokinase, tenecteplase, alteplase)Dissolve the clot in STEMI, stroke, massive PEHigh bleeding risk for the first day: no vigorous chest physiotherapy or injections
Statins (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin)Lower LDL cholesterol; stabilise plaqueMuscle aches or weakness: report new unexplained muscle pain
DigoxinSlows the rate in atrial fibrillation; mild boost to contractionToxicity (nausea, confusion, visual changes, slow or irregular pulse), especially with low potassium
Anti-arrhythmics (amiodarone)Control atrial and ventricular arrhythmiasSlow heart rate; long-term lung fibrosis and thyroid problems (new breathlessness or cough)
Inotropes and vasopressors (dobutamine, noradrenaline, adrenaline)Support BP and cardiac output in ICUHigh or rising doses: no active mobilisation (see the ICU safety table)

2Respiratory drugs

Group (examples)What it doesPhysiotherapy meaning
Short-acting β2-agonists (salbutamol, levosalbutamol)Quick relief of bronchospasm (reliever)Give 15–30 minutes before airway clearance or exercise if prescribed; keep the reliever at hand; tremor and fast heart rate
Long-acting β2-agonists (salmeterol, formoterol)12–24-hour bronchodilation (with an inhaled steroid in asthma)Not for sudden relief (except formoterol combinations in asthma, as prescribed)
Antimuscarinics (ipratropium short-acting; tiotropium, glycopyrronium long-acting)Bronchodilation, less mucus secretion (mainly COPD)Dry mouth; check inhaler technique
Inhaled corticosteroids (budesonide, fluticasone, beclometasone)Reduce airway inflammation (asthma preventer; COPD with frequent exacerbations)Rinse the mouth after use (thrush, hoarse voice); use a spacer
Oral steroids (prednisolone)Treat exacerbations; some chronic diseases (ILD, sarcoidosis)Long courses: osteoporosis (care with percussion and falls), muscle weakness, high sugar, thin skin, poor wound healing
Methylxanthines (theophylline, doxofylline)Weak bronchodilatorNarrow safety margin: palpitations, nausea, tremor, seizures if levels are high
Leukotriene antagonists (montelukast)Add-on in asthma and allergic rhinitisOccasional mood or sleep changes
Mucoactive drugs (N-acetylcysteine, carbocisteine; hypertonic saline; dornase alfa in cystic fibrosis)Thin or loosen sputumTime airway clearance after them; hypertonic saline can cause bronchospasm, so give a bronchodilator first
Antibiotics (oral, IV, inhaled)Treat infectionInhaled antibiotics go after airway clearance, so they reach clear airways
OxygenA drug: corrects hypoxaemiaPrescribed with a target; see the oxygen card

Usual order in cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis: bronchodilator → mucoactive drug → airway clearance → inhaled antibiotic or steroid.

3Other drugs common in these patients

Group (examples)Physiotherapy meaning
Insulin and sulfonylureas (glimepiride, gliclazide)Risk of low blood sugar during and hours after exercise: check sugar, carry glucose, avoid exercising at peak insulin action without food (see the diabetes page)
Metformin, gliptins, SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin)Low hypoglycaemia risk on their own; SGLT2 inhibitors also help heart failure but can cause dehydration
Anti-TB drugs (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol)Isoniazid: tingling and numbness of the feet (pyridoxine prevents it); rifampicin: orange urine and tears, many drug interactions; ethambutol: blurred vision; liver toxicity (jaundice, vomiting): report
Opioids and other painkillers (morphine, tramadol, paracetamol); epidurals and nerve blocksTreat after the dose works (often 30–60 minutes after oral doses); opioids cause drowsiness, slow breathing, nausea and constipation; epidurals can cause leg weakness and low BP when sitting up
Sedatives and paralysing drugs (ICU)No voluntary movement or cough under paralysis; daily sedation breaks are the time for active work
Anticholinesterases (pyridostigmine in myasthenia)Treat when the dose is working (often about an hour after it); too much causes secretions and weakness

4Timing physiotherapy around medicines

  • Bronchodilator 15–30 minutes before airway clearance or exercise in reactive airways.
  • Pain relief before post-operative chest physiotherapy and mobilisation.
  • Insulin and meals: exercise 1–2 hours after a meal; check sugar before and after.
  • GTN before a known angina trigger, if prescribed.
  • Diuretics: after the morning dose has worked, so that toilet trips do not interrupt the session.
  • Sedation in tetanus or ICU: coordinate with the nurse so the patient is comfortable but able to take part.

Test yourself

3 mHow do beta-blockers affect an exercise prescription?

They lower resting and peak heart rate, so heart-rate targets from formulas are too high; use RPE (11–13 or 12–16) and the talk test, or a heart rate from an exercise test done on the medicine.

MCQA patient with cystic fibrosis uses salbutamol, hypertonic saline and an inhaled antibiotic. The best order is:A. Antibiotic, airway clearance, saline, salbutamolB. Salbutamol, saline, airway clearance, antibioticC. Airway clearance, salbutamol, antibiotic, salineD. Saline, antibiotic, salbutamol, airway clearance

B. Open the airways, loosen the mucus, clear it, then deliver the antibiotic to clean airways.

MCQWhich drug most raises the bleeding risk of chest percussion?A. SalbutamolB. WarfarinC. AmlodipineD. Montelukast

B. Warfarin; check the INR and handle gently.
