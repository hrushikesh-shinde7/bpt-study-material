Dyspnoea, relieving positions, energy conservation and pacing

Breathlessness questions come in many forms: definition, mechanism and treatment (W25), positions that ease it (S22), energy conservation (W17, S19, S22, W22, S25) and pacing of activities (S20, W21). One idea links them: lower the demand, improve the efficiency, and calm the fear.

My revision

1Definition

Dyspnoea (breathlessness) is "a subjective experience of breathing discomfort that consists of qualitatively distinct sensations that vary in intensity" (American Thoracic Society, 2012). It is what the patient feels, so it is measured by the patient's own rating.

  • Different sensations: air hunger (cannot get enough air), work or effort of breathing, chest tightness (bronchospasm).
  • Two parts: how strong it is (sensory) and how distressing it is (emotional).
  • Measure: modified Borg (0–10) during activity; mMRC grade (0–4) for daily life; see the scales card.
  • Related terms: orthopnoea (breathless lying flat: heart failure), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (waking breathless at night), platypnoea (breathless sitting or standing, relieved by lying).

2How breathlessness is perceived (W25)

  • Motor drive and sense of effort: a copy of the motor command to the breathing muscles (corollary discharge) reaches the sensory cortex. Weak or overloaded muscles need a bigger command for the same breath, which is felt as effort.
  • Chemoreceptors: rising CO₂ and acidity (central, in the medulla) and low oxygen (peripheral, carotid and aortic bodies) increase the drive and produce air hunger.
  • Mechanoreceptors: stretch and irritant receptors in the airways and lungs, J receptors (stiff, wet lungs), and muscle spindles and tendon organs in the chest wall report how well the lungs and chest are moving.
  • Mismatch: when the drive is high but the lungs cannot respond (hyperinflation, stiff lungs, weak muscles), the cortex senses a mismatch: this is the main source of breathlessness.
  • Emotion: the limbic system adds fear and anxiety; anxiety speeds breathing, which worsens hyperinflation and breathlessness (the breathlessness–anxiety cycle).

Common clinical causes: higher demand (exercise, fever, acidosis, low oxygen), higher load (airway obstruction, stiff lungs or chest wall, hyperinflation), weaker muscles (neuromuscular disease, deconditioning), anxiety.

3Treatment of breathlessness (W25)

ApproachExamples
Treat the causeBronchodilators, treatment of heart failure, infection, anaemia; oxygen if hypoxaemic
Lower the work of breathingPositions of ease; pursed-lip breathing; breathing control; airway clearance; NIV in selected patients
Lower the demandEnergy conservation, pacing, weight loss, aids for daily tasks
Build capacityExercise training (pulmonary rehabilitation) desensitises to breathlessness and lowers ventilation at each workload; inspiratory muscle training if muscles are weak
Calm the brainEducation, relaxation, a "breathlessness plan"; a handheld fan blown on the face (reduces breathlessness through facial cool-air receptors); anxiety management
Medical palliationLow-dose opioids in advanced disease, as prescribed

4Positions to ease breathlessness (3 marks in S22)

  1. High side-lying: head and shoulders well supported on pillows, top arm on a pillow.
  2. Forward-lean sitting: forearms on the thighs, or on pillows on a table.
  3. Relaxed sitting: leaning back in a supportive chair with forearms on armrests.
  4. Forward-lean standing: forearms on a wall, window sill, rail or walking frame.
  5. Relaxed standing: back and hips against a wall, feet a little apart, shoulders relaxed.

Rationale: leaning forward lets the abdominal contents push a flattened diaphragm up into a better dome shape, so each contraction moves more air; fixing the arms lets the pectoral and other accessory muscles pull on the ribs; supported, relaxed positions cut muscle work and anxiety; upright positions increase lung volume.

5Energy conservation (S19, S22, W22; 3 marks W17, S25)

Memory hook

The 4 Ps: Plan, Pace, Prioritise, Position

PrincipleExamples
PlanSpread heavy jobs (washing clothes, cleaning, shopping) through the week; do them at the best time of day (often late morning, after airway clearance and medicines); gather everything before starting
PaceSlow, steady movement; rest before becoming very breathless; breathe out on the effort part ("blow as you go"): lifting, standing up, reaching
PrioritiseDrop or share tasks that are not essential; ask family to help with heavy work
Position and ergonomicsSit to bathe, dress, iron and cook; keep things used often between waist and shoulder height; avoid bending, lifting and working with the arms above the head; use a trolley or bag on wheels; long-handled sponge, shower stool, raised toilet seat
Breathing with activityPursed-lip breathing while walking; stairs one step per breath out, resting on landings
EnvironmentAvoid smoke, dust and extreme heat or cold; loose clothes; fan nearby

Ergonomic advice for one-sided lung disease (W22, left lung bronchiectasis with a normal right lung): rest and sleep lying on the right side: the healthy right lung is below, where it gets more blood and air, and the diseased left lung is on top, where it drains. Sit upright at work with the desk at elbow height; plan huffing breaks; avoid dust and smoke. Full answer on the bronchiectasis page.

6Activity pacing (3 marks in S20 and W21)

Pacing means doing activity in planned, manageable amounts with rests, so the patient stays below the level of breathlessness that forces a stop, and finishes the task without "boom and bust".

  • Break tasks into steps: make the bed in stages, sitting between them.
  • Alternate heavy and light tasks through the day.
  • Rest before exhaustion: "stop, rest, recover, restart" when breathlessness reaches a moderate level (Borg about 3–4).
  • Stairs: one step on each breath out, rest every few steps or at the landing.
  • Walking: interval walking (walk a set distance, rest, repeat) and slower speed with pursed-lip breathing.
  • Bathing and dressing: sit to wash and dress; rest between bathing and dressing; dressing gown instead of towelling off.

Test yourself

3 mGive the rationale of forward-lean sitting in a breathless COPD patient.

The abdominal contents push the flattened diaphragm up into a domed, longer position, so it contracts more effectively; fixing the arms lets accessory muscles help lift the ribs; the supported posture reduces muscle work and anxiety.

3 mWhat is activity pacing? Give two examples.

Doing activities in planned amounts with rests so breathlessness stays manageable. Examples: climbing stairs one step per breath out with rests at the landing; making the bed in stages with sitting breaks.

MCQThe main source of breathlessness when a hyperinflated COPD patient walks fast is:A. Low haemoglobinB. A mismatch between high drive and limited lung expansionC. Raised blood sugarD. Low heart rate

B. Neuromechanical dissociation: the drive rises but the hyperinflated chest cannot expand further.
