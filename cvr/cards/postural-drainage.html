Postural drainage, percussion, vibration and shaking

Postural drainage was asked as contraindications (S18, any six) and for a neonate (W18), and it belongs in every answer on airway clearance or bronchiectasis. Learn the positions by lobe, the head-down contraindications, and when percussion is not allowed.

My revision

1What it is

Postural drainage (gravity-assisted positioning) places the patient so that the bronchus of the affected segment points downwards towards the trachea, letting gravity help secretions drain to the large airways, where they are huffed or coughed out. It is almost always combined with a breathing technique (ACBT) and sometimes with percussion, vibration or shaking.

  • Classic: 11–12 positions, many with the foot of the bed raised (head-down tilt).
  • Modified postural drainage: the same side-lying and prone positions without head-down tilt, used when tilting is contraindicated or not tolerated (infants, reflux, breathlessness).

Dose: usually about 10 minutes in each productive position, with the whole session kept to about 20–40 minutes; once or twice a day for chronic sputum, more during infection. Not straight after meals.

Memory hook

Bad side up, bronchus down

Lie on the healthy side for a lateral segment; turn towards prone for posterior segments and towards supine for anterior segments; raise the foot for lower lobes and the middle lobe or lingula.

3Indications

  • Large amounts of sputum: bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, lung abscess (affected segment uppermost).
  • Retained secretions or collapse of a known lobe or segment (on X-ray or by auscultation).
  • Patients too weak or too young to do active techniques alone (infants, ventilated or unconscious patients) using modified positions.

4Contraindications (3 marks in S18)

To head-down tilt
  • Recent or active haemoptysis
  • Raised intracranial pressure, recent head injury or neurosurgery
  • Uncontrolled hypertension, unstable heart condition, arrhythmia, pulmonary oedema or heart failure
  • Severe breathlessness or bronchospasm
  • Gastro-oesophageal reflux, hiatus hernia, recent oesophageal surgery, just after meals or feeds
  • Aortic aneurysm, recent eye surgery
  • Gross obesity, abdominal distension, late pregnancy
  • Untreated pneumothorax, large pleural effusion or empyema, rib fractures, painful recent surgery

Use modified (flat side-lying or sitting) positions instead.

To percussion, vibration and shaking
  • Rib fractures, flail chest, osteoporosis, bone metastases, recent spinal surgery
  • Haemoptysis; low platelets or clotting problems
  • Unstable BP or heart rhythm; bronchospasm (percussion can worsen it)
  • Burns, fresh skin grafts, open wounds or a recently inserted pacemaker over the area; surgical emphysema
  • Pain the patient cannot tolerate

Newborns (W18, 7 marks): head-down tilt is avoided (reflux, intraventricular haemorrhage risk in preterm babies); handle minimally; see the neonatal chest physiotherapy page for the full list.

5Percussion, vibration and shaking

TechniqueHowEffect
Percussion (clapping)Rhythmic clapping with cupped hands and relaxed wrists over the affected segment, through a thin towel or clothing; for about 30 seconds to a few minutes, alternating with breathing control or thoracic expansionThe air cushion transmits an energy wave that loosens mucus from the airway walls
VibrationFine, rapid shaking from the therapist's tensed arms with gentle compression, during the breath out onlyIncreases expiratory flow and moves mucus towards larger airways
ShakingCoarser, rhythmic bouncing compression of the chest wall during the breath out, usually after a deep breath inStronger version of vibration; helps move thick mucus
  • Use them only when the patient has secretions and finds them helpful; they are added to, not instead of, a breathing technique.
  • Infants: gentle percussion with fingertips or a soft cup; no head-down tilt.
  • Ventilated patients: vibrations or shaking timed with expiration, often after manual or ventilator hyperinflation.

Classic answer vs current guideline: percussion and head-down tilt

Classic

Postural drainage with head-down tilt and percussion for every patient with sputum.

Current (BTS/ACPRC 2009)

Gravity-assisted positions help when secretions are in a known area, but active, independent techniques (ACBT, autogenic drainage, PEP) are preferred; head-down tilt is used only when it helps and is safe; percussion is not routine. Choose the method the patient will actually do.

Write in the exam

"Gravity-assisted positions for the affected lobe, combined with ACBT; modified positions without head-down tilt where contraindicated; percussion and vibration as adjuncts if helpful."

3 mGive six contraindications to head-down postural drainage.

Haemoptysis; raised intracranial pressure; uncontrolled hypertension or unstable heart disease; pulmonary oedema; severe breathlessness; gastro-oesophageal reflux or just after meals; aortic aneurysm; late pregnancy or abdominal distension; untreated pneumothorax (any six).
