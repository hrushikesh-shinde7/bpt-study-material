Exercise testing: contraindications, stop criteria, Bruce, cycle, walk and step tests

Exercise testing is in 9 of 20 papers. The single most repeated question is "absolute and relative contraindications to exercise testing" (W18, W20, W19, S23, S26). Add the Bruce protocol (S16), cycle ergometer (S22), 6MWT versus shuttle walk (W22) and the Harvard step test from the syllabus.

My revision

1Why test exercise capacity

  • Diagnosis: exercise-induced ischaemia, arrhythmia, desaturation or bronchospasm.
  • Prognosis and risk stratification: before cardiac rehab (see the IHD page), before surgery.
  • Functional capacity: how much the patient can do (METs, distance).
  • Exercise prescription: safe heart-rate range, walking speed, need for oxygen.
  • Outcome measure: response to rehabilitation or treatment.

Types: laboratory tests (treadmill and cycle ergometer, with ECG; cardiopulmonary exercise test with gas analysis) and field tests (six-minute walk, shuttle walks, step tests).

2Absolute and relative contraindications (7 marks in W18, W20, S26; 3 marks in W19, S23)

Absolute contraindications (do not test)
  • Acute myocardial infarction within 2 days
  • Ongoing unstable angina
  • Uncontrolled cardiac arrhythmia with symptoms or poor circulation
  • Active endocarditis
  • Symptomatic severe aortic stenosis
  • Decompensated (uncontrolled) heart failure
  • Acute pulmonary embolism, pulmonary infarction or deep vein thrombosis
  • Acute myocarditis or pericarditis
  • Acute aortic dissection
  • Physical disability that makes safe, adequate testing impossible
Relative contraindications (test only if benefit outweighs risk)
  • Known left main coronary artery stenosis
  • Moderate to severe aortic stenosis with uncertain symptoms
  • Fast heart rhythm with uncontrolled ventricular rate
  • Complete or advanced heart block
  • Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy with a severe resting gradient
  • Recent stroke or transient ischaemic attack
  • Mental impairment limiting co-operation
  • Resting BP above 200/110 mmHg
  • Uncorrected conditions: significant anaemia, electrolyte imbalance, overactive thyroid
Memory hook for the absolute list

"Acute heart, acute clot, tight valve"

Acute heart: MI within 2 days, unstable angina, arrhythmia, heart failure, myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis. Acute clot or tear: PE, DVT, aortic dissection. Tight valve: symptomatic severe aortic stenosis. Plus: unable to exercise safely.

4Treadmill and cycle tests

Bruce protocol (3 marks in S16)

A symptom-limited treadmill test in which speed and gradient both increase every 3 minutes, with ECG, BP and symptoms monitored. The result is the exercise time, the stage reached (METs), and any ischaemic changes, arrhythmia or BP problems.

Modified Bruce (for older, deconditioned or cardiac patients, early after MI): two gentler starting stages at 1.7 mph with 0% and then 5% gradient, before stage 1 of the standard protocol. It avoids the big jump in workload at the start.

5Field walking tests (3 marks in W22)

Six-minute walk test (6MWT)

  • Walk back and forth along a flat, straight course of at least 30 m, as far as possible in 6 minutes, at the patient's own pace; rests are allowed; standard encouragement every minute.
  • Record distance, SpO₂ and heart rate (before, during, after), Borg breathlessness and fatigue before and after, and any stops. Do a practice test where possible.
  • Do not test (ATS 2002): unstable angina or MI in the previous month. Caution: resting heart rate above 120, BP above 180/100 mmHg.
  • Stop for: chest pain, intolerable breathlessness, leg cramps, staggering, sweating, pale or ashen appearance.
  • Meaning of a change: in chronic respiratory disease, a change of about 25–33 m (often quoted as 30 m) is the smallest that matters to patients.

Shuttle walk tests

  • Incremental shuttle walk test (ISWT): walk around two cones on a 10 m course in time with audio beeps; speed increases every minute through 12 levels; the test ends when the patient cannot reach the cone in time or stops with symptoms. A maximal, symptom-limited test; distance relates closely to peak oxygen uptake.
  • Endurance shuttle walk test (ESWT): constant speed set from the ISWT result (a high percentage of peak); measures endurance time; sensitive to change after rehab.

6Harvard step test

  • Method (original): step up and down a 20-inch (about 50 cm) step at 30 steps a minute for 5 minutes or until exhausted (keeping time with a metronome). Lower steps are used in modified versions for women and patients.
  • Recovery pulse: count the pulse for 30 seconds at 1–1.5, 2–2.5 and 3–3.5 minutes after stopping.
  • Fitness index (long form) = (duration in seconds × 100) ÷ (2 × sum of the three pulse counts).
  • Short form = (duration in seconds × 100) ÷ (5.5 × the 1–1.5 minute pulse count).
  • Interpretation (long form): below 55 poor; 55–64 low average; 65–79 high average; 80–89 good; 90 or more excellent.
  • Use: a quick fitness test for healthy people; not suitable for most cardiac or respiratory patients (high, uncontrolled intensity).
Worked example

Completes 300 seconds; pulse counts 70, 60, 50. Index = (300 × 100) ÷ (2 × 180) = 30000 ÷ 360 = 83: good.

Test yourself

3 mList five absolute contraindications to exercise testing.

MI within 2 days; unstable angina; uncontrolled symptomatic arrhythmia; symptomatic severe aortic stenosis; decompensated heart failure; acute PE or DVT; acute myocarditis or pericarditis; active endocarditis; acute aortic dissection (any five).

MCQStage 1 of the Bruce protocol is:A. 1.7 mph at 0%B. 1.7 mph at 10%C. 2.5 mph at 12%D. 3.4 mph at 14%

B. 1.7 mph at 10%, for 3 minutes. 1.7 mph at 0% is the first stage of the modified Bruce.

MCQWhich is a relative, not absolute, contraindication?A. Acute pulmonary embolismB. Resting BP 210/115 mmHgC. Unstable anginaD. Acute aortic dissection

B. Resting BP above 200/110 is relative; the others are absolute.
