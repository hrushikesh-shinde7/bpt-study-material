My revision

1Why airway clearance is needed

Healthy airways clear mucus by the mucociliary escalator (cilia sweep a thin mucus layer upwards) and by coughing. Airway clearance techniques (ACTs) are needed when:

  • there is too much mucus, or it is too thick (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, COPD with chronic bronchitis, chest infection);
  • the cilia work poorly (smoking, cystic fibrosis, ciliary dyskinesia, dry gas through a tracheal tube);
  • the cough is weak or painful (neuromuscular disease, after surgery, sedation).

Aims: remove mucus, reduce infections and the "vicious cycle" of infection and inflammation, improve ventilation and gas exchange, reduce cough and breathlessness, and improve quality of life.

2Classification

  • Breathing techniques: active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT), forced expiration technique and huffing, autogenic drainage, directed or assisted cough.
  • Gravity and manual techniques: postural drainage, percussion, vibration, shaking.
  • Positive expiratory pressure devices: PEP mask, oscillating PEP (Flutter, Acapella), bottle PEP.
  • Mechanical devices: chest wall oscillation vest, intrapulmonary percussive ventilation, cough assist (insufflation–exsufflation), IPPB.
  • Exercise (increases ventilation and loosens mucus).
  • Adjuncts: hydration and humidification, nebulised saline (normal or hypertonic), mucolytics, bronchodilator before treatment.
  • Suction when the patient cannot cough secretions out.

4Choosing a technique

  • No single technique is best for everyone (BTS/ACPRC 2009); short-term studies show they all help clear sputum. Choose by the patient's age, condition, ability, preference and what they will actually keep doing.
  • Children: infants need modified postural drainage, gentle percussion and active play; older children PEP, bubble PEP and ACBT.
  • Unstable airways or wheeze with forced breaths: autogenic drainage, PEP.
  • Weak cough: assisted cough, cough assist, suction.
  • Order with medicines: bronchodilator → mucoactive drug (for example hypertonic saline) → airway clearance → inhaled antibiotic or steroid.
  • Review the technique regularly; adjust frequency during exacerbations.

5Mucus removal in chronic respiratory disease (7 marks in W19)

  1. Assess: daily sputum amount and colour, how easily it comes up, X-ray or CT (which lobes), breathlessness, previous techniques.
  2. Prepare the mucus: enough fluids; humidified or nebulised saline if sputum is thick; bronchodilator first in reactive airways.
  3. Daily routine: one main technique (ACBT, autogenic drainage or oscillating PEP), in a gravity-assisted position for the worst lobe if tolerated; 10–30 minutes, once or twice a day.
  4. Exacerbation plan: increase to 2–3 times a day; early medical review for antibiotics.
  5. Exercise: regular aerobic exercise as part of pulmonary rehabilitation.
  6. Pelvic floor: ask about urine leakage with coughing (common in women) and teach pelvic floor contraction before huffs and coughs.
  7. Review: outcome measures such as sputum weight, cough or bronchiectasis quality-of-life scores, exacerbation count.

6Indications of bronchial hygiene therapy (3 marks in W25)

  • Large amounts of sputum (classically more than about 25–30 mL a day) or difficulty clearing it.
  • Mucus plugging with lobar or segmental collapse.
  • Weak or painful cough: neuromuscular disease, after surgery, sedated or ventilated patients.
  • Chronic suppurative lung disease (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis) as a daily routine.

MCQWhich technique is most suitable for a 3-month-old with cystic fibrosis?A. Autogenic drainage done by the infantB. Modified postural drainage with gentle percussion and active playC. FlutterD. Incentive spirometry

B. Infants cannot do active techniques; drainage without head-down tilt, gentle percussion and play are used.
