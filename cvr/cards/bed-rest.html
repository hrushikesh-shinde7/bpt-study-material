Effects of bed rest and immobilisation, and early mobilisation

Bed rest was asked five times: as 3 marks (one effect on three systems, S19 and W21) and as 7 marks (complications of bed immobility in ICU, W17; complications on different systems, S24; effects on the cardiovascular and pulmonary systems, S26). Learn it system by system, with the mechanism and the prevention for each.

My revision

1Why bed rest harms

The body adapts to the load it carries. Lying flat removes two loads at once: gravity along the body (which keeps blood-pressure reflexes and fluid balance trained) and muscle work against gravity (which keeps muscle, bone and fitness). Within days every system starts to lose what it does not use. Older and critically ill patients lose it fastest.

Two classic numbers

3 weeks: fitness down about a quarter

In the Dallas bed rest study (1968), 3 weeks of bed rest cut young men's maximal oxygen uptake by about 27%. In critically ill patients the thigh muscle (rectus femoris) shrank by about 12.5% in the first week (Puthucheary, 2013).

ICU-acquired weakness (W17: complications of bed immobility in ICU)

Generalised, symmetrical limb weakness that develops during critical illness with no other cause. Diagnosed at the bedside with the MRC sum score (6 muscle groups each side, 0–5 each, total 60): below 48 = ICU-acquired weakness. Risk factors: sepsis, multi-organ failure, long ventilation, immobility, high blood sugar, steroids and paralysing drugs. It slows weaning and can affect walking and work for months, so prevention by early mobilisation matters.

4Prevention: early mobilisation

  1. Assess safety first (see the ICU safety criteria).
  2. Positioning: turns every 2–4 hours, head up, upright sitting in bed.
  3. Range of motion: passive when sedated, then active-assisted and active.
  4. In-bed exercise: bed cycling, bridging, resisted limb exercise.
  5. Sitting over the edge of the bed, then standing (tilt table if BP falls or the patient is weak).
  6. Transfer to a chair; marching on the spot; walking, with the ventilator if needed.
  7. Breathing: deep breathing, airway clearance, inspiratory muscle training.
  8. Monitor before, during and after: HR, BP, SpO₂, respiratory rate, symptoms.

Classic answer vs current evidence: rest after illness

Classic

Strict bed rest for days or weeks after a heart attack, surgery or serious illness.

Current

Bed rest is a treatment with side effects, used only when needed and for as short a time as possible. Early mobilisation is safe within criteria and shortens delirium and recovery; the amount is matched to the patient.

Write in the exam

"Bed rest affects every system within days; prevention is early, graded mobilisation with positioning, breathing exercises and ROM."

Memory hook for a 3-mark answer: one effect each

Heart, lungs, legs

Heart: postural hypotension. Lungs: basal collapse and pneumonia. Legs: muscle wasting and DVT. Add skin (pressure sores) or bone (osteoporosis) if asked for more.

Answer skeleton: 7 marks

  1. [1] One-line principle: unloading and lying flat cause every system to adapt downwards within days.
  2. [4] Table: system, effect, mechanism (cardiovascular and pulmonary in detail for S26).
  3. [2] Prevention: positioning, breathing exercises, ROM, early graded mobilisation, DVT and pressure care.

Test yourself

3 mWhy does a patient feel faint when first sat up after 10 days in bed?

Plasma volume has fallen (fluid shifts to the chest when lying flat and is passed as urine), the heart is less filled, and blood-pressure reflexes are out of practice. On sitting, blood pools in the legs and BP falls: postural hypotension. Sit up in stages, use ankle pumps and a tilt table.

MCQThe MRC sum score that defines ICU-acquired weakness is:A. Below 60B. Below 48C. Below 30D. Below 12

B. Below 48 out of 60.
