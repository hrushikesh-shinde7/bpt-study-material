9(d)Suctioning and humidification

"Hazards of suctioning" is one of the most repeated 3-mark questions (W16, S17, S18, W19, S26), and suction with bagging was asked as 7 marks twice (S22, S24). Learn the indications, the numbers (15 seconds, −200 mmHg, half the tube width) and a clean list of hazards.

My revision

1What it is

Suctioning removes secretions from the airway with a catheter attached to negative pressure, when the patient cannot clear them by coughing.

TypeRouteNotes
Endotracheal or tracheostomy, openDisconnect the ventilator and pass a sterile catheterLoses PEEP and oxygen during the pass
Closed (in-line)Catheter inside a sealed sleeve, attached to the circuitKeeps PEEP and FiO₂; less infection spread; preferred with high PEEP or FiO₂ and infectious patients
Nasopharyngeal or nasotrachealThrough the nose, in a patient without an artificial airwayUse a lubricated catheter or a nasopharyngeal airway; uncomfortable
OropharyngealMouth, with a Yankauer suckerClears mouth and throat secretions above the cuff

Shallow suction passes the catheter only to the length of the tube; deep suction goes until resistance is felt, then pulls back about 1 cm before suction. Use shallow suction routinely and deep suction only when shallow suction does not work (AARC 2022).

2Indications

Suction only when there are signs of secretions, not on a fixed schedule:

  • Secretions seen or heard in the tube; coarse crackles over the trachea and main bronchi.
  • A saw-tooth pattern on the ventilator's flow graph; rising peak pressure (volume modes) or falling tidal volume (pressure modes).
  • Falling SpO₂ or worsening blood gases that are likely due to secretions.
  • A weak or absent cough after other airway clearance has moved secretions up; suspected aspiration.
  • To take a sputum sample.

3Contraindications and precautions

Suction has no absolute contraindication when an artificial airway is blocked. Take extra care in:

  • Raised intracranial pressure: extra sedation as prescribed, short passes, few passes.
  • High PEEP or FiO₂, severe hypoxaemia: closed suction, pre-oxygenate.
  • Bleeding risk: low platelets or clotting problems, fresh haemoptysis: gentle, shallow suction.
  • Unstable heart rhythm or BP; spinal cord injury or GBS (vagal bradycardia).
  • Bronchospasm (suction can trigger it).
  • Recent lung resection: no deep suction near the bronchial stump.
  • Nasal route not used with a base-of-skull fracture or CSF leak, facial fractures, nasal bleeding or blocked nasal passages, and used with care in stridor (laryngospasm).

4Procedure (endotracheal, open)

The three numbers (AARC 2022)

15 s / −200 mmHg / half the tube

  • Each pass no longer than 15 seconds.
  • Suction pressure no stronger than −200 mmHg in adults (−120 mmHg in children and newborns).
  • Catheter fills less than half of the tube's inner width in adults and children (less than 70% in newborns). For an 8.0 mm tube, a 12 French catheter.
  1. Assess and explain: need for suction, vital signs, SpO₂; tell the patient (even if sedated) what you will do.
  2. Prepare: hand hygiene, apron, eye protection, sterile glove on the dominant hand; check the suction pressure; choose the catheter size.
  3. Pre-oxygenate if the patient drops their SpO₂ with suction (raise FiO₂ briefly, often for 30–60 seconds, using the ventilator's oxygen button).
  4. Insert the catheter without suction to the planned depth (shallow: tube length).
  5. Withdraw with suction applied, rolling the catheter between the fingers, in under 15 seconds.
  6. Reconnect the ventilator at once; let SpO₂ and heart rate recover before another pass; usually no more than 2–3 passes.
  7. Oral suction last, with a separate catheter or Yankauer (never go from the mouth into the tube).
  8. Reassess and record: breath sounds, SpO₂, amount, colour and thickness of secretions, any bleeding, how the patient tolerated it.
  • No routine saline down the tube: it does not thin secretions well and may raise infection risk. Thick secretions need better humidification and hydration.
  • With a closed system, advance the catheter through the sleeve, suction while withdrawing, then flush the catheter.

6Manual hyperinflation ("bagging") (7 marks in S22 and S24)

Manual hyperinflation (MHI) means disconnecting the ventilator and giving larger, slower breaths with a rebreathing bag, followed by a quick release. It imitates a cough.

Aims

  • Re-open collapsed alveoli (air passes behind secretions through collateral channels).
  • Move secretions towards the large airways: the quick release creates a fast expiratory flow.
  • Improve lung compliance and oxygenation for a short time.

Technique

  1. Two-person technique is common: one bags, one applies chest shaking or vibration.
  2. A 2-litre bag with oxygen at about 15 L/min, a pressure manometer and a PEEP valve if the patient needs PEEP.
  3. Patient in side-lying with the affected area uppermost (or supine).
  4. Slow, deep inspiration (larger than the ventilator breath), an inspiratory hold of about 2–3 seconds, then a quick release of the bag so the patient breathes out fast; add vibrations or shaking during that expiration.
  5. Keep the peak airway pressure at about 40 cmH₂O or less on the manometer.
  6. Repeat a few breaths in sets, suction when secretions reach the large airways, then reconnect and reassess.

Contraindications and precautions

  • Undrained pneumothorax (absolute); bullae; recent lung surgery with a fresh bronchial stump.
  • Unstable BP or arrhythmia (large breaths reduce venous return).
  • High PEEP (above about 10) or high FiO₂: disconnection collapses the lung.
  • Raised intracranial pressure not under control; severe bronchospasm; frank haemoptysis; pulmonary oedema.

Classic answer vs current evidence: bag or ventilator?

Classic

Bag every ventilated patient with secretions, by hand.

Current

A systematic review (Paulus, 2012) found short-term improvements in oxygenation, compliance and secretion clearance, but no proof of better long-term outcomes. Ventilator hyperinflation (larger breaths set on the ventilator) works about as well and avoids disconnection, so it is preferred with high PEEP or FiO₂.

Write in the exam

"Manual hyperinflation with a manometer, pressure kept at about 40 cmH₂O or less, with vibrations and suction; ventilator hyperinflation when PEEP or FiO₂ is high."

7Humidification for intubated and ventilated patients (W17)

The full notes on humidification and nebulisation are in 4(e).

Why intubated patients need it (W17)

An endotracheal tube or tracheostomy bypasses the nose and throat, so dry, cold gas reaches the trachea. This causes thick, sticky secretions, slows the cilia, damages the airway lining, blocks the tube, causes collapse and loses body heat and water. The AARC (2012) strongly recommends humidification for every invasively ventilated patient.

TypeHow it worksPoints
Heat and moisture exchanger (HME, "artificial nose")Passive: traps heat and moisture from each breath out and returns it on the next breath inShould deliver at least 30 mg H₂O/L; cheap, simple. Bubble humidifierOxygen bubbles through water at room temperatureFor oxygen; adds little real humidity

Uses (S17): intubated and tracheostomy patients; high-flow oxygen used for long periods; thick secretions; NIV comfort; dry upper airway.

Hazards: overheating and airway burns (heated systems); water collecting in the tubing (can be washed into the patient, spreads infection, triggers false breaths); infection from contaminated water; extra resistance and dead space with an HME, which can block with secretions.
