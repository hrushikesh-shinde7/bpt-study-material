My revision

•Neonatal chest physiotherapy: precautions and contraindications

Asked as 7 marks in W18. Learn this section first.

Precautions
  • Treat only when needed: retained secretions, collapse or a blocked tube; not as routine.
  • Minimal handling: cluster care with nursing; short sessions; let the baby rest between.
  • Monitor continuously: SpO₂, heart rate, breathing, colour and temperature; keep the baby warm.
  • Timing: before feeds or well after them, to avoid vomiting and aspiration.
  • Positioning: avoid head-down tilt; use modified (flat or head-up) positions. In infants with reflux, a 30° head-up position caused fewer reflux episodes than head-down (Cochrane).
  • Gentle techniques: very preterm babies have fragile ribs (osteopenia) and fragile brain blood vessels; vigorous percussion has been linked to injury, so use gentle, modified techniques only when clearly indicated.
  • Suction: correct catheter size and pressure, pre-oxygenate if needed, limit duration; watch for bradycardia and desaturation.
Contraindications (do not do chest physiotherapy, or only after senior review)
  • Unstable baby: hypotension, uncontrolled arrhythmia, severe hypoxia with handling
  • Recent or extending intraventricular haemorrhage, very preterm baby in the first days of life
  • Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (handling triggers desaturation)
  • Undrained pneumothorax or air leak
  • Rib fractures, osteopenia of prematurity, low platelets or bleeding disorder (for percussion and vibration)
  • Raised intracranial pressure; immediately after feeds; some post-operative states (for example after abdominal or cardiac surgery) without surgical clearance

iARDS: neonatal respiratory distress syndrome and paediatric ARDS

Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)

Definition and cause

Breathing failure in premature babies because the lungs lack surfactant, so alveoli collapse at the end of each breath. Commoner with more premature birth and maternal diabetes.

Key features

  • Within hours of birth: fast breathing, grunting, nasal flaring, rib and sternal recession, cyanosis.
  • X-ray: small lungs with a "ground-glass" appearance and air bronchograms.

Medical point

Antenatal steroids to the mother, CPAP, surfactant given into the airway, ventilation if needed.

PT management

  • Routine chest physiotherapy is not needed; the lungs are stiff, not full of secretions.
  • Positioning for comfort and oxygenation (prone positioning is used only under continuous monitoring in neonatal units), developmental positioning, minimal handling.
  • Airway clearance only if secretions build up later (for example on a ventilator).

Exam points

  • Surfactant deficiency; grunting; ground-glass X-ray; CPAP and surfactant; minimal handling.

Paediatric ARDS

Acute, severe lung inflammation in a child after pneumonia, sepsis, drowning or trauma, with low oxygen and bilateral shadows on X-ray, needing ventilation. Physiotherapy: positioning (prone positioning as directed by the ICU team), careful secretion clearance, minimal disconnection from the ventilator, early mobilisation and rehabilitation after recovery.

iiMeconium aspiration syndrome

Definition and cause

Breathing problems in a term or post-term baby who inhaled meconium (first stool) passed into the amniotic fluid, often after fetal distress.

Key features

  • Meconium-stained fluid and skin; breathing distress from birth.
  • Meconium blocks airways (with air trapping and pneumothorax risk), inflames the lungs (chemical pneumonitis) and inactivates surfactant; it can cause persistent pulmonary hypertension.
  • X-ray: patchy shadows and over-inflation; possible pneumothorax.

Medical point

Supportive: oxygen, CPAP or ventilation, surfactant, nitric oxide for pulmonary hypertension. Current resuscitation guidance (ILCOR) no longer recommends routine laryngoscopy and tracheal suction of non-vigorous babies born through meconium; start effective breathing support promptly.

PT management

  • Airway clearance (gentle positioning, modified drainage, suction) only when secretions are clearly retained.
  • Minimal handling, especially with pulmonary hypertension.

Exam points

  • Term or post-term; air trapping, pneumothorax, chemical pneumonitis, pulmonary hypertension.

iii–ivPneumonitis and pneumonia in children

Definition and causes

  • Pneumonitis: lung inflammation from aspiration of milk or stomach contents (reflux, poor swallowing), chemicals (kerosene ingestion in children is a classic Indian example), or infection.
  • Pneumonia: bacterial or viral infection; a major cause of death in children under 5 in India.
  • Bronchiolitis (viral, under 2 years) often looks similar: wheeze, crackles, feeding difficulty.

Key features

Fast breathing for age, chest in-drawing, fever, cough, poor feeding; low SpO₂ in severe cases.

  • Noises: grunting (the baby breathes out against a closed glottis to keep alveoli open), stridor, wheeze.
  • Effects: cyanosis and low SpO₂; fast heart rate; cannot feed or talk; sweating.
  • Danger signs (tiring): drowsiness or poor response, pauses in breathing (apnoea), slowing breathing in an exhausted child, silent chest. Call for urgent help.
Memory hook

60, 50, 40

Fast-breathing cut-offs fall by 10 as the child grows: under 2 months, under 1 year, under 5 years.

PT management

  • Bronchiolitis (Cochrane 2023): conventional chest physiotherapy (vibration, percussion) does not improve severity; forced expiratory techniques cause vomiting and breathing instability. Slow passive expiratory techniques may give a small benefit in moderate cases.
  • Pneumonia: no routine airway clearance; use positioning, mobilisation and play; airway clearance if secretions are retained (for example a collapsed lobe).
  • Aspiration: head-up positioning, feeding advice with speech and language therapy.

Exam points

  • Chest physiotherapy is not routine for bronchiolitis or uncomplicated pneumonia in children.

vChildhood asthma

Asthma itself (causes, attacks, drugs, assessment) is in 6(b)(iii). These are the parts of that page written for children.

Paediatric asthma rehabilitation (7 marks in S18; W22)

  • Assessment and goals with the child and parents.
  • Education: what asthma is, triggers, inhaler with spacer and mask, action plan, when to seek help.
  • Breathing exercises as games: blowing bubbles, windmills, cotton-ball races (gentle long exhalation), "smell the flower, blow the candle".
  • Exercise through play: warm-up games, interval play (short bursts with rest), skipping, cycling, swimming; 2–3 sessions a week, progressing time and intensity.
  • Strengthening (body-weight games), posture and flexibility.
  • Family and school involvement (counselling below).

viCystic fibrosis and chronic lung disease

Cystic fibrosis has its own page, 6(b)(iv): daily airway clearance starts from diagnosis in infancy and becomes play-based PEP and exercise in childhood. Chronic lung disease of prematurity is below.

Chronic lung disease of prematurity (bronchopulmonary dysplasia)

Definition and cause

Long-term lung damage in premature babies who needed oxygen and ventilation; usually defined by an ongoing oxygen need at 36 weeks after conception.

Key features

Fast breathing, oxygen dependence, recurrent wheeze and infections, poor growth.

PT management

  • Airway clearance during infections; positioning; developmental care (tummy time when awake and monitored, head control, motor milestones).
  • Parent education on home oxygen, signs of infection and feeding.
  • Later: active play and exercise as tolerated.

Answer skeleton: precautions and contraindications in a neonate

  1. Indications: retained secretions, collapse; not routine.
  2. Precautions: minimal handling, monitoring, warmth, timing with feeds, head-up or flat positions, gentle techniques, safe suction.
  3. Contraindications: instability, intraventricular haemorrhage, pulmonary hypertension, undrained pneumothorax, fragile ribs or bleeding, raised intracranial pressure.
  4. Modified techniques: positioning, gentle vibration, assisted breathing, suction.

Test yourself

MCQA preterm baby on CPAP has reflux. Best drainage position:A. Head-down tiltB. Flat or head-up (about 30°)C. Upside downD. No position change ever

B. Head-down positions increase reflux and are avoided in neonates.

MCQFor a 6-month-old with moderate bronchiolitis, which statement is true?A. Percussion and vibration shorten illnessB. Forced expiratory techniques are safe and effectiveC. Conventional chest physiotherapy does not improve severityD. Head-down drainage is first-line

C. Cochrane (2023) found no benefit from conventional techniques, and harm from forced expiratory techniques.
