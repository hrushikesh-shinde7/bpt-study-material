4(a)Bronchial hygiene therapy: postural drainage, forced expiration technique, ACBT, autogenic drainage

Bronchial hygiene is the most-asked skill in this paper: ACBT as 7 marks in W16, S23 and S26, its parts, huffing, FET and autogenic drainage as 3 marks, the overview of techniques (S19, W24), mucus removal (W19) and postural drainage contraindications (S18). Learn the overview first, then each technique with its principle, dose and contraindications.

My revision

1Why airway clearance is needed

Healthy airways clear mucus by the mucociliary escalator (cilia sweep a thin mucus layer upwards) and by coughing. Airway clearance techniques (ACTs) are needed when:

  • there is too much mucus, or it is too thick (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, COPD with chronic bronchitis, chest infection);
  • the cilia work poorly (smoking, cystic fibrosis, ciliary dyskinesia, dry gas through a tracheal tube);
  • the cough is weak or painful (neuromuscular disease, after surgery, sedation).

Aims: remove mucus, reduce infections and the "vicious cycle" of infection and inflammation, improve ventilation and gas exchange, reduce cough and breathlessness, and improve quality of life.

2Classification of airway clearance techniques

  • Breathing techniques: active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT), forced expiration technique and huffing, autogenic drainage, directed or assisted cough.
  • Gravity and manual techniques: postural drainage, percussion, vibration, shaking.
  • Positive expiratory pressure devices: PEP mask, oscillating PEP (Flutter, Acapella), bottle PEP.
  • Mechanical devices: chest wall oscillation vest, intrapulmonary percussive ventilation, cough assist (insufflation–exsufflation), IPPB.
  • Exercise (increases ventilation and loosens mucus).
  • Adjuncts: hydration and humidification, nebulised saline (normal or hypertonic), mucolytics, bronchodilator before treatment.
  • Suction when the patient cannot cough secretions out.

3Indications of bronchial hygiene therapy (3 marks in W25)

  • Large amounts of sputum (classically more than about 25–30 mL a day) or difficulty clearing it.
  • Mucus plugging with lobar or segmental collapse.
  • Weak or painful cough: neuromuscular disease, after surgery, sedated or ventilated patients.
  • Chronic suppurative lung disease (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis) as a daily routine.

5Choosing a technique

  • No single technique is best for everyone (BTS/ACPRC 2009); short-term studies show they all help clear sputum. Choose by the patient's age, condition, ability, preference and what they will actually keep doing.
  • Children: infants need modified postural drainage, gentle percussion and active play; older children PEP, bubble PEP and ACBT.
  • Unstable airways or wheeze with forced breaths: autogenic drainage, PEP.
  • Weak cough: assisted cough, cough assist, suction.
  • Order with medicines: bronchodilator → mucoactive drug (for example hypertonic saline) → airway clearance → inhaled antibiotic or steroid.
  • Review the technique regularly; adjust frequency during exacerbations.

6Mucus removal in chronic respiratory disease (7 marks in W19)

  1. Assess: daily sputum amount and colour, how easily it comes up, X-ray or CT (which lobes), breathlessness, previous techniques.
  2. Prepare the mucus: enough fluids; humidified or nebulised saline if sputum is thick; bronchodilator first in reactive airways.
  3. Daily routine: one main technique (ACBT, autogenic drainage or oscillating PEP), in a gravity-assisted position for the worst lobe if tolerated; 10–30 minutes, once or twice a day.
  4. Exacerbation plan: increase to 2–3 times a day; early medical review for antibiotics.
  5. Exercise: regular aerobic exercise as part of pulmonary rehabilitation.
  6. Pelvic floor: ask about urine leakage with coughing (common in women) and teach pelvic floor contraction before huffs and coughs.
  7. Review: outcome measures such as sputum weight, cough or bronchiectasis quality-of-life scores, exacerbation count.

7Postural drainage: what it is

Postural drainage (gravity-assisted positioning) places the patient so that the bronchus of the affected segment points downwards towards the trachea, letting gravity help secretions drain to the large airways, where they are huffed or coughed out. It is almost always combined with a breathing technique (ACBT) and sometimes with percussion, vibration or shaking.

  • Classic: 11–12 positions, many with the foot of the bed raised (head-down tilt).
  • Modified postural drainage: the same side-lying and prone positions without head-down tilt, used when tilting is contraindicated or not tolerated (infants, reflux, breathlessness).

Dose: usually about 10 minutes in each productive position, with the whole session kept to about 20–40 minutes; once or twice a day for chronic sputum, more during infection. Not straight after meals.

Memory hook

Bad side up, bronchus down

Lie on the healthy side for a lateral segment; turn towards prone for posterior segments and towards supine for anterior segments; raise the foot for lower lobes and the middle lobe or lingula.

9Postural drainage: indications

  • Large amounts of sputum: bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, lung abscess (affected segment uppermost).
  • Retained secretions or collapse of a known lobe or segment (on X-ray or by auscultation).
  • Patients too weak or too young to do active techniques alone (infants, ventilated or unconscious patients) using modified positions.

10Postural drainage: contraindications (3 marks in S18)

To head-down tilt
  • Recent or active haemoptysis
  • Raised intracranial pressure, recent head injury or neurosurgery
  • Uncontrolled hypertension, unstable heart condition, arrhythmia, pulmonary oedema or heart failure
  • Severe breathlessness or bronchospasm
  • Gastro-oesophageal reflux, hiatus hernia, recent oesophageal surgery, just after meals or feeds
  • Aortic aneurysm, recent eye surgery
  • Gross obesity, abdominal distension, late pregnancy
  • Untreated pneumothorax, large pleural effusion or empyema, rib fractures, painful recent surgery

Use modified (flat side-lying or sitting) positions instead.

To percussion, vibration and shaking
  • Rib fractures, flail chest, osteoporosis, bone metastases, recent spinal surgery
  • Haemoptysis; low platelets or clotting problems
  • Unstable BP or heart rhythm; bronchospasm (percussion can worsen it)
  • Burns, fresh skin grafts, open wounds or a recently inserted pacemaker over the area; surgical emphysema
  • Pain the patient cannot tolerate

Newborns (W18, 7 marks): head-down tilt is avoided (reflux, intraventricular haemorrhage risk in preterm babies); handle minimally; see the neonatal chest physiotherapy page for the full list.

11Percussion, vibration and shaking

TechniqueHowEffect
Percussion (clapping)Rhythmic clapping with cupped hands and relaxed wrists over the affected segment, through a thin towel or clothing; for about 30 seconds to a few minutes, alternating with breathing control or thoracic expansionThe air cushion transmits an energy wave that loosens mucus from the airway walls
VibrationFine, rapid shaking from the therapist's tensed arms with gentle compression, during the breath out onlyIncreases expiratory flow and moves mucus towards larger airways
ShakingCoarser, rhythmic bouncing compression of the chest wall during the breath out, usually after a deep breath inStronger version of vibration; helps move thick mucus
  • Use them only when the patient has secretions and finds them helpful; they are added to, not instead of, a breathing technique.
  • Infants: gentle percussion with fingertips or a soft cup; no head-down tilt.
  • Ventilated patients: vibrations or shaking timed with expiration, often after manual or ventilator hyperinflation.

Classic answer vs current guideline: percussion and head-down tilt

Classic

Postural drainage with head-down tilt and percussion for every patient with sputum.

Current (BTS/ACPRC 2009)

Gravity-assisted positions help when secretions are in a known area, but active, independent techniques (ACBT, autogenic drainage, PEP) are preferred; head-down tilt is used only when it helps and is safe; percussion is not routine. Choose the method the patient will actually do.

Write in the exam

"Gravity-assisted positions for the affected lobe, combined with ACBT; modified positions without head-down tilt where contraindicated; percussion and vibration as adjuncts if helpful."

12ACBT: what it is

The active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT) is a flexible cycle of three breathing exercises that the patient does independently to loosen and clear sputum: breathing control, thoracic expansion exercises and the forced expiration technique.

13ACBT components and the forced expiration technique: physiological principle (S23)

ComponentHow to do itPhysiological principle
Breathing control (BC)Gentle, relaxed tidal breathing with the lower chest; shoulders and upper chest relaxed; as long as the patient needs between the active partsRest; prevents bronchospasm, fatigue and desaturation that deep or forced breaths can cause
Thoracic expansion exercises (TEE)3–4 deep breaths in, with a 3-second hold (or a sniff) at the top, then a relaxed breath out; the therapist's or patient's hand on the chest wall over the area for feedback; percussion or shaking can be added during themA large lung volume lets air travel through collateral channels (pores of Kohn, canals of Lambert, channels of Martin) and get behind mucus; the hold evens out filling between lung units; expanding one area helps open its neighbours (interdependence)
Forced expiration technique (FET)1–2 huffs (forced breaths out through an open mouth and glottis, like steaming a mirror) from mid to low lung volume, followed by breathing control; a huff or cough from high volume clears secretions that have reached the large airwaysDuring a forced expiration the airways narrow downstream of the equal pressure point, and fast air there shears mucus towards the mouth. Starting from a lower lung volume moves the equal pressure point further out, so a longer huff clears more peripheral airways
What is the equal pressure point?

In a forced breath out, pressure inside the airways falls from the alveoli to the mouth. At some point it equals the pressure outside the airway (the pleural pressure): that is the equal pressure point. Beyond it the airway is squeezed narrower, the air speeds up, and mucus is carried along. At high lung volume this point is in the large central airways; as lung volume falls it moves towards smaller, more peripheral airways. That is why a long, medium-volume huff reaches further than a cough from a full breath.

14ACBT: indications and contraindications

Indications

  • Excess or retained bronchial secretions: bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, COPD with sputum, chest infection, asthma with mucus plugging.
  • Collapse (atelectasis) and prevention of chest complications after surgery.
  • To make coughing more effective and less tiring; patients who become breathless or wheezy with coughing.
  • Independent daily airway clearance at home (no equipment).

Contraindications and precautions

  • Few true contraindications. Not possible in patients who cannot follow instructions (unconscious, confused, very young children).
  • Bronchospasm: lengthen breathing control; stop if wheeze increases.
  • Haemoptysis: avoid vigorous huffing and added percussion; gentle breathing control.
  • Untreated pneumothorax, unstable heart, severe pain after surgery: modify (supported huffing) or avoid forced parts.
  • Fatigue and desaturation: shorter cycles, oxygen as prescribed.

Indications of bronchial hygiene therapy in general (3 marks in W25)

  • Large amounts of sputum (classically more than about 25–30 mL a day) or difficulty clearing it.
  • Mucus plugging with lobar or segmental collapse.
  • Weak or painful cough: neuromuscular disease, after surgery, ventilated patients.
  • Chronic suppurative lung disease (bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis) as a daily routine.

15ACBT: procedure and dose

  1. Explain the purpose; take a bronchodilator first if prescribed.
  2. Position: comfortable sitting, or a gravity-assisted position for the affected lobe if tolerated (see the postural drainage page).
  3. Start with breathing control until relaxed.
  4. 3–4 thoracic expansion breaths with a 3-second hold.
  5. Breathing control.
  6. 1–2 huffs from mid lung volume, then breathing control; when secretions are in the large airways, a high-volume huff or one cough.
  7. Repeat the cycle. Stop when the huff sounds dry and non-productive in two consecutive cycles, or when the patient tires.

Dose: usually 10–30 minutes, once or twice a day in chronic sputum production, more often during an infection. After surgery, a few cycles every hour or two while awake.

Rationale in asthma (S17): breathing control prevents bronchospasm and fatigue; thoracic expansion gets air behind plugs; huffing moves mucus without the coughing fits that narrow sensitive airways; the patient controls the pace and stops if wheeze increases.

Teaching a huff: medium breath in, then breathe out fast but not violently through an open "O"-shaped mouth, as if steaming up a mirror; the sound should be a breathy rush, not a cough. A mouthpiece or tube helps keep the glottis open. Support a wound with a pillow or hands.

17Autogenic drainage (7 marks in S20; phases 3 marks in W19)

Autogenic drainage (AD) (Chevaillier, Belgium) uses controlled breathing at different lung volumes to create the highest airflow the airways can take without collapsing, so mucus moves from the small airways to the large ones, where it is cleared with a gentle huff.

PhaseBreathingEffect
1. UnstickSmall breaths at low lung volume (in the expiratory reserve range)Loosens mucus in the peripheral airways
2. CollectBreaths at mid lung volume (around normal tidal breathing)Gathers mucus in the middle airways
3. EvacuateDeeper breaths at high lung volume; then a gentle huff or coughMoves mucus to the central airways and out

Technique points

  • Sitting upright (or lying); slow breath in through the nose; hold for 2–3 seconds; breathe out through an open mouth and open throat, actively but without forcing, like a sigh, as fast as possible without wheezing or airway squeezing.
  • Feel or hear the mucus crackle to judge which phase to move to.
  • Suppress the urge to cough until mucus reaches the large airways.
  • Sessions usually take 30–45 minutes; the patient learns by feedback over several sessions.

Indications, advantages and disadvantages

  • Indications: bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis (from about 8–12 years), COPD with sputum; patients whose airways collapse or wheeze with forced techniques; patients with cough-induced incontinence.
  • Advantages: independent, no equipment, gentle (less airway collapse, bronchospasm and desaturation), can be done anywhere quietly.
  • Disadvantages: hard to learn; needs concentration and good body awareness; time-consuming; not for young children or confused patients (a therapist-assisted form exists for infants).

Test yourself

3 mClassify airway clearance techniques with one example of each.

Breathing techniques (ACBT); gravity and manual (postural drainage with percussion); PEP devices (Acapella); mechanical devices (cough assist); exercise; adjuncts (nebulised hypertonic saline); suction.

MCQWhich technique is most suitable for a 3-month-old with cystic fibrosis?A. Autogenic drainage done by the infantB. Modified postural drainage with gentle percussion and active playC. FlutterD. Incentive spirometry

B. Infants cannot do active techniques; drainage without head-down tilt, gentle percussion and play are used.

3 mGive six contraindications to head-down postural drainage.

Haemoptysis; raised intracranial pressure; uncontrolled hypertension or unstable heart disease; pulmonary oedema; severe breathlessness; gastro-oesophageal reflux or just after meals; aortic aneurysm; late pregnancy or abdominal distension; untreated pneumothorax (any six).

MCQTo drain the right middle lobe, the patient lies:A. On the right side, turned a quarter forward, flatB. On the left side, turned a quarter backwards, foot raisedC. Prone, foot raisedD. Sitting upright

B. Left side down, turned back onto a pillow, foot of the bed raised about 15°.

3 mName the three components of ACBT with one purpose of each.

Breathing control: rest, prevents bronchospasm and fatigue. Thoracic expansion exercises: get air behind mucus through collateral ventilation. Forced expiration technique (huffs): moves mucus from smaller to larger airways.

3 mName the three phases of autogenic drainage and the lung volume of each.

Unstick (low lung volume), collect (mid, tidal volume), evacuate (high lung volume), followed by a gentle huff.

MCQA huff from low lung volume clears mainly:A. The tracheaB. Peripheral (smaller) airwaysC. The nasal passagesD. The pleural space

B. At low lung volume the equal pressure point lies further out, in the smaller airways.

MCQACBT should be stopped or changed when:A. The huff sounds wetB. Wheeze increasesC. The patient is sittingD. Breathing control lasts more than 20 seconds

B. Increase breathing control and stop forced breaths if wheeze increases.
