9(c)Mechanical ventilator modes

Ventilated patients now appear in long answers (GBS on SIMV in S23, ARDS on SIMV in S24, pneumonia on CMV in W24). You do not set the ventilator, but you must read its settings, know what each mode means for your treatment, and know when the patient is ready to come off it.

My revision

1What it is

Mechanical ventilation means a machine moves gas into the lungs with positive pressure when the patient cannot breathe well enough alone. It buys time while the cause is treated; it does not cure anything.

  • Invasive: through an endotracheal tube (ETT) or a tracheostomy.
  • Non-invasive (NIV): through a tight face or nasal mask: CPAP or bilevel pressure (BiPAP).

Warning signs that lead to ventilation: respiratory rate above about 35, exhaustion, falling SpO₂ despite oxygen, rising CO₂ with falling pH, drowsiness, and in neuromuscular disease a falling vital capacity.

3Modes (7 marks in S17 and W20)

ModeHow it worksUsed for
Controlled mandatory ventilation (CMV)Set volume (or pressure) at a set rate; in strict CMV the patient cannot add breathsDeeply sedated or paralysed patients (W24 pneumonia on CMV)
Assist-control (A/C)Set breath and a minimum rate; if the patient triggers, each extra breath is a full machine breath. Most modern "CMV" settings work this wayResting tired muscles early in illness. Fast breathing can cause over-breathing and air trapping
Synchronised intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV)A set number of machine breaths, timed with the patient's effort; between them the patient breathes alone, usually with pressure supportPartial support (S20, S23, S24 cases). Once used for weaning by lowering the rate, but weaning is slower than with pressure support or daily breathing trials
Pressure control (PCV)Set pressure and inspiratory time; the volume varies with lung stiffnessLimits pressure in stiff lungs (ARDS)
Pressure support (PSV)The patient starts every breath and controls rate and depth; the machine adds a set pressureComfortable partial support; weaning by lowering the support to 5–8 cmH₂O
CPAPOne continuous positive pressure; no machine breaths; the patient does all the breathingWeaning on the ventilator; mask CPAP for sleep apnoea, cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, post-operative collapse
Bilevel NIV (BiPAP)Higher pressure on breathing in (IPAP), lower on breathing out (EPAP), by maskCOPD exacerbation with acidosis, after extubation in high-risk patients, neuromuscular disease at night

Rescue modes in very severe ARDS, such as high-frequency oscillation, are run by the doctors; patients on them are not mobilised out of bed.

Why does the mode matter to a physiotherapist?

It tells you how much work the patient's own muscles do. On CMV or A/C at a high rate the muscles are resting and wasting, so you rely on positioning, passive movement and secretion clearance. On SIMV, PSV or CPAP the patient is breathing, so active exercise, breathing exercises and inspiratory muscle training become possible, and you watch for fatigue.

4CPAP and PEEP (3 marks in W21)

PointCPAPPEEP
What it isA mode: positive pressure through the whole breath in a patient who breathes on their ownA setting: positive pressure kept at the end of breathing out, added to any mode with machine breaths
BreathsAll spontaneous; no machine breathsMachine or supported breaths on top of it
How givenMask, nasal interface or ventilatorVentilator (or a PEEP valve on a bag)
Typical useSleep apnoea, cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, post-op collapse, weaningAlmost every ventilated patient (about 5 cmH₂O); higher in ARDS
Memory hook

CPAP = PEEP for a patient who breathes alone

Effects of both: raise the functional residual capacity (FRC), keep small airways and alveoli open, improve oxygenation and lung compliance, and reduce collapse. CPAP also lowers the work of breathing and, in heart failure, the load on the left ventricle.

Side effects of high levels: less blood returns to the heart, so BP can fall; over-stretching and pneumothorax (barotrauma); raised intracranial pressure; mask discomfort, air swallowing and pressure sores with CPAP.

Do not confuse with auto-PEEP (intrinsic PEEP): trapped air when the patient cannot breathe out fully before the next breath, common in COPD and asthma; it lowers BP and makes triggering harder.

5Complications (3 marks in S22 and W21)

Long-term ventilation (W21): tracheal stenosis or softening, repeated VAP, diaphragm wasting and ventilator dependence, ICU-acquired weakness, contractures, pressure injuries, and psychological effects. Patients who need the ventilator for more than about 1–2 weeks often get a tracheostomy, which is more comfortable, needs less sedation and makes weaning easier.

6Weaning (7 marks in W19)

Weaning is the process of freeing the patient from the ventilator. It starts with a daily check of readiness, then a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT), then extubation.

Ready to try? (daily screen)

  • The cause of respiratory failure is improving.
  • Oxygenation: SpO₂ 90% or more on FiO₂ 0.4–0.5 or less and PEEP 5–8 cmH₂O or less (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio above about 150–200).
  • Circulation: stable heart rate and BP, no or low-dose drugs to support BP, no active ischaemia.
  • Brain: awake or easily roused, follows commands; can cough and protect the airway.
  • Breathing: breathes on their own; pH 7.25 or more; few secretions; no fever, normal electrolytes.
Bedside weaning measures
MeasureFavours weaning
Rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI = rate ÷ tidal volume in litres)Below 105 breaths/min/L
Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP)Stronger than about −20 to −30 cmH₂O
Vital capacityMore than about 10 mL/kg
Minute ventilationBelow about 10 L/min
Why does a high RSBI predict failure?

A tiring patient breathes fast and shallow: rate goes up, tidal volume goes down, so the ratio rises. Example: 35 breaths of 0.25 L gives 140 (likely to fail); 20 breaths of 0.5 L gives 40 (likely to succeed).

The breathing trial

  • 30–120 minutes on low pressure support (5–8 cmH₂O) or a T-piece. The 2017 ACCP/ATS guideline suggests using low pressure support for the trial.
  • Stop the trial if: breathing rate above 35 for more than 5 minutes; SpO₂ below 90%; heart rate above 140 or a change of more than 20%; systolic BP above 180 or below 90 mmHg; agitation, sweating, use of accessory muscles or see-saw breathing.
  • Passed: the team considers extubation (with a cough and secretion check). High-risk patients may go onto NIV straight after extubation.
How weaning went (international consensus, 2007)
GroupMeaning
SimpleExtubated after the first trial
DifficultNeeded up to 3 trials or up to 7 days
ProlongedMore than 3 trials or more than 7 days

The physiotherapist's part in weaning

  • Keep the airways clear: positioning, manual techniques, hyperinflation and suction.
  • Sit the patient upright for trials: the diaphragm works better and lung volume is larger.
  • Inspiratory muscle training for patients who fail weaning: it strengthens the inspiratory muscles and may shorten weaning (systematic review, 2015).
  • Early mobilisation builds the strength and endurance needed to breathe alone.
  • After extubation: deep breathing, huffing and supported cough, early walking.

7Reading the settings in a case

Before treatment, note the mode, FiO₂, PEEP, set and actual tidal volume, rate, peak pressure, SpO₂ and the latest ABG, and whether these are getting better or worse. Rising FiO₂ or PEEP means the lungs are getting worse.

CaseSettingsWhat it tells you

Alarms during treatment
  • High-pressure alarm: coughing, secretions, biting the tube, kinked tubing, bronchospasm, the patient fighting the ventilator, or a pneumothorax.
  • Low-pressure or low-volume alarm: disconnection or a leak around the cuff.
  • If the cause is not obvious, or SpO₂ falls, stop and call the nurse or doctor. Never silence an alarm you do not understand.

Test yourself

3 mGive three differences between CPAP and PEEP.

CPAP is a mode, PEEP is a setting. CPAP applies pressure throughout the breath in a patient who breathes alone, with no machine breaths; PEEP is pressure kept at the end of expiration during machine or supported breaths. CPAP is often given by mask (sleep apnoea, pulmonary oedema); PEEP is given through the ventilator to almost every ventilated patient.

3 mA patient on a breathing trial breathes 32 times a minute with a tidal volume of 0.4 L. Calculate the RSBI and interpret it.

32 ÷ 0.4 = 80 breaths/min/L. Below 105, so it favours successful weaning (other criteria must also be met).

MCQIn which mode does the patient breathe spontaneously between a set number of machine breaths?A. CMVB. SIMVC. CPAPD. Pressure control

B. SIMV gives a set number of synchronised machine breaths; between them the patient breathes alone, usually with pressure support.

MCQDuring a spontaneous breathing trial, which finding means the trial should be stopped?A. SpO₂ 94%B. Breathing rate 38 for 6 minutesC. Heart rate rising from 90 to 100D. Systolic BP 130 mmHg

B. A rate above 35 for more than 5 minutes means the patient is tiring.
