9(e)Therapeutic intervention in ICU conditions

Four long answers since 2020 put the patient on a ventilator: COPD with pneumonia (S20), GBS (S23), ARDS (S24) and pneumonia (W24). The other 9(e) conditions have not been asked on their own, but a head injury or spinal cord injury case could come next. Each condition gives what it is, the medical point that matters for physiotherapy, treatment by problem and precautions.

•Physiotherapy for the ventilated patient

Physiotherapy for the ventilated patient (S17, S21, S26; goals W17)

Short-term goals for a patient on a ventilator (W17)

  • Keep the airways clear and the lungs expanded; improve oxygenation.
  • Prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia, collapse and aspiration.
  • Keep joint range; prevent contractures, pressure injuries and DVT.
  • Limit muscle wasting; start active exercise as soon as safe.
  • Support weaning; keep the patient oriented and able to communicate.

Chest physiotherapy on SIMV (7 marks in S26)

  1. Check: settings, vital signs, ABG, X-ray, sedation and safety criteria; time the session with pain relief and away from feeds.
  2. Positioning: head of bed 30–45°; side-lying with the affected area uppermost for drainage; turns every 2–4 hours; upright sitting as soon as possible.
  3. Manual techniques on expiration: chest shaking, vibrations, percussion where secretions are retained (avoid with fractures, low platelets, unstable patients).
  4. Hyperinflation by bag (manual) or by the ventilator to re-open collapsed areas and move secretions, then suction (see the suction page).
  5. Breathing exercises when awake on SIMV: deep breaths with a hold on the patient's own breaths; inspiratory muscle training during weaning.
  6. Limbs: passive ROM of all joints when sedated; active-assisted and active exercise when awake; splints for ankles and hands where needed.
  7. Mobilisation ladder: rolling → sitting up in bed → sitting over the edge → standing → marching on the spot → transfer to a chair → walking with the ventilator, as safety criteria allow.
  8. Reassess: breath sounds, SpO₂, secretions, ventilator pressures; record.
Precautions on the ventilator
  • High PEEP or FiO₂: avoid disconnecting the circuit; use closed suction and ventilator hyperinflation.
  • Undrained pneumothorax: no hyperinflation or other positive-pressure techniques.
  • Unstable BP or arrhythmia: limit to positioning and gentle treatment.
  • Raised ICP: head up 30°, neutral neck, short treatments, no head-down tilt.
  • Low platelets or clotting problems: no percussion; gentle suction.
  • Recent lung surgery: no deep suction or high pressures near the bronchial stump.

Early mobilisation: what the evidence says

Classic answer vs current evidence: how early, how much

Classic

Keep ventilated patients in bed; passive movements only until they come off the ventilator.

Current

Mobilising ventilated patients is safe within the safety criteria. Starting physiotherapy and occupational therapy during daily sedation breaks led to more patients walking and independent at discharge (59% vs 35%) and less delirium (Schweickert, 2009). A large 2022 trial (TEAM) found that pushing more early active mobilisation than usual care did not improve survival or days at home at 6 months and caused more adverse events, so the dose must suit the patient.

Write in the exam

"Early mobilisation as soon as safety criteria are met, progressed step by step, with monitoring before, during and after each session."

Mobilisation is one part of the ICU ABCDEF bundle: Assess and manage pain; Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials; Choice of sedation; Delirium care; Early mobility and exercise; Family involvement.

Other ventilated cases

  • W24: atypical pneumonia of both lungs on CMV, FiO₂ 0.6, PEEP 10: worked plan on the pneumonia page.
  • S20: COPD exacerbation with right lower lobe pneumonia on SIMV: COPD page.
  • W17: electrician with burns and 3 rib fractures on a ventilator: burns page and chest-wall page.

iTetanus and head injury

Tetanus

What it is

An illness caused by the toxin of Clostridium tetani, whose spores enter through wounds (puncture wounds, burns, injections, unclean delivery or cord care in newborns). The toxin travels up the nerves and blocks the inhibitory nerve cells in the spinal cord and brainstem, so muscles contract without control and the autonomic nervous system overacts. Symptoms usually start 3–21 days after the injury. It is prevented by vaccination; India was declared free of maternal and neonatal tetanus in 2015, but adult cases still occur after injuries.

What you see in the ICU

  • Lockjaw (trismus), a fixed grin (risus sardonicus), stiff neck, back and abdomen; arching of the back (opisthotonus).
  • Painful spasms set off by touch, noise, light or moving the patient; the patient stays fully conscious, which is very frightening.
  • Spasm of the larynx and a rigid chest wall cause respiratory failure; difficulty swallowing and aspiration.
  • Severe cases: unstable BP, fast or irregular heart rate, sweating, fever (autonomic instability).

Medical point that matters for physio

  • Wound cleaning, human tetanus immunoglobulin, metronidazole; sedation (benzodiazepines); magnesium sulphate reduces spasms and autonomic swings (trial, 2006); paralysing drugs with ventilation and an early tracheostomy in severe cases.
  • A dark, quiet room. The illness lasts several weeks. The patient needs vaccination after recovery, because having tetanus does not give immunity.

ICF summary

Impairments: rigidity and spasms, respiratory failure, retained secretions, aspiration, pain, autonomic instability, later weakness and deconditioning. Activity: fully dependent. Participation: off work for weeks to months. Context: often a manual worker with an untreated wound; fear and anxiety.

Physiotherapy by problem

  • Plan with the team: treat when sedation is adequate (often just after a dose); keep sessions short; no sudden touch or noise.
  • Chest: positioning and regular turns, gentle chest vibrations and shaking, suction with sedation; humidification for a tracheostomy.
  • Limbs: positioning and pressure care; slow, gentle passive movement only when spasms are controlled.
  • Recovery phase: as sedation and spasms settle: active exercise, jaw-opening exercises, sitting, standing, walking; build strength and endurance after weeks in bed; reassurance.

Precautions

  • Any stimulus can trigger spasms and laryngospasm: warn the patient, use firm, slow handling, and stop if spasms start.
  • Watch BP and heart rate (autonomic swings); avoid fatigue.
  • Strong spasms can fracture vertebrae: report new back pain.

Exam points

  • Toxin blocks inhibition; trismus, risus sardonicus, opisthotonus; spasms triggered by stimuli; dark, quiet room; coordinate physiotherapy with sedation.

Traumatic head injury

What it is

Brain injury from an external force (road accidents, falls, assault). Primary injury happens at impact (contusion, diffuse axonal injury, extradural or subdural haematoma). Secondary injury follows over hours and days from low oxygen, low BP, raised intracranial pressure (ICP), seizures, fever and high or low blood sugar. The whole ICU plan, including physiotherapy, is built to prevent secondary injury.

Severity by Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS, 3–15)
SeverityGCS
Mild13–15
Moderate9–12
Severe3–8 (usually intubated to protect the airway: "GCS 8, intubate")

What you see in the ICU

  • Reduced consciousness; ventilated and sedated; ICP monitor or external ventricular drain; sometimes part of the skull removed (decompressive craniectomy).
  • Abnormal posturing: decorticate (arms flexed, legs extended) or decerebrate (arms and legs extended); changing tone; seizures.
  • Episodes of fast heart rate, high BP, sweating, fever and posturing (paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity).
  • Chest problems: aspiration at the time of injury, ventilator-associated pneumonia, collapse; agitation and confusion as the patient wakes.

Medical point that matters for physio

  • ICP is treated when it rises above about 22 mmHg; cerebral perfusion pressure (MAP − ICP) is kept at about 60–70 mmHg (Brain Trauma Foundation, 2016).
  • Avoid low oxygen and low BP; keep CO₂ normal (high CO₂ widens brain vessels and raises ICP); head up 30°; sedation; drugs such as mannitol or hypertonic saline; surgery for haematomas.

ICF summary

Impairments: reduced consciousness, retained secretions, abnormal tone and posture, weakness, contracture and pressure-sore risk, later cognitive and behaviour changes. Activity: fully dependent. Participation: work, family and study roles, often for months. Context: young adults; family support and access to long-term rehab matter.

Physiotherapy by problem

  • Chest: head up 30° with the neck straight; side-lying turns as ICP allows; chest vibrations and compressions on expiration for secretions (in a study of sedated head-injury patients they did not change ICP or cerebral perfusion pressure); suction only when needed, after sedation and pre-oxygenation, with short passes. Suction raised ICP (about 20 to 26 mmHg) but it settled within about 10 minutes, and perfusion pressure was kept.
  • Tone and contractures: positioning out of the abnormal posture (for example side-lying with flexed hips and knees for extensor tone), ankle splints, gentle passive range of motion; serial casting later for fixed plantarflexion.
  • Mobilisation: once ICP is stable and not being actively treated: sitting up in bed, sitting over the edge, tilt table, standing; watch BP and ICP.
  • As the patient wakes: calm, consistent handling for agitation; orientation; family involvement; then neurological rehabilitation (see Neurophysiotherapy).

Precautions

  • ICP above target or being actively treated: no active mobilisation; minimal, clustered handling.
  • No head-down tilt; avoid neck flexion and rotation, tight tube ties and high-pressure bagging (all raise ICP).
  • Craniectomy: no pressure on the side without bone; helmet when mobilising.
  • External ventricular drain: clamp or level it as the team instructs before moving the patient.
  • Spine not yet cleared: log-roll and spinal precautions; check for other fractures.

Exam points

  • GCS grades; ICP 22 and perfusion pressure 60–70; head up 30°; suction raises ICP briefly, so sedate and keep it short; prevent contractures from day one.

iiPulmonary oedema

What it is

Fluid in the lung tissue and alveoli, so gas exchange fails.

  • Cardiogenic: a failing left ventricle (heart attack, heart failure, valve disease, arrhythmia) or fluid overload raises the pressure in the lung capillaries and pushes fluid out.
  • Non-cardiogenic: leaky capillaries with normal pressure: ARDS, near drowning, smoke inhalation, high altitude, after severe head injury (neurogenic).

What you see

  • Severe breathlessness, worse lying flat (orthopnoea), waking breathless at night; anxiety, sweating.
  • Frothy, pink sputum; fine crackles at both bases, sometimes wheeze; low SpO₂.
  • X-ray: haziness spreading from both hila ("bat's wing"), Kerley B lines at the edges, a large heart and pleural effusions in cardiogenic oedema.

Medical point that matters for physio

Sit upright, oxygen to target, diuretics, nitrates, treatment of the cause. CPAP or bilevel NIV by mask relieves breathlessness and acidosis quickly and reduces the need for intubation in cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (Cochrane, 2013).

Physiotherapy by problem

  • Breathlessness: high sitting with the legs down (less venous return), breathing control, reassurance; help apply and tolerate the CPAP mask.
  • Sputum: the frothy fluid is oedema, not secretions, so airway clearance does not help and only tires the patient. Treat secretions only if there is also a chest infection.
  • After it settles: early mobilisation, then heart failure rehabilitation (see the heart failure page).

Precautions

  • Never lie the patient flat or head-down; watch BP (diuretics and nitrates lower it) and fluid balance; do not exercise until breathing and oxygen are stable at rest.

Exam points

  • Cardiogenic vs non-cardiogenic; pink frothy sputum; bat's wing and Kerley B lines; sit up with legs down; CPAP; no airway clearance for oedema fluid.

iiiMultiple organ failure (MODS)

What it is

Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome: progressive failure of two or more organ systems in an acutely ill patient, so that normal balance cannot be kept without support. Common causes: sepsis, major trauma, burns, pancreatitis, major surgery, shock of any cause.

Severity is tracked with the SOFA score, which scores six systems: breathing (PaO₂/FiO₂), clotting (platelets), liver (bilirubin), circulation (MAP and BP-support drugs), brain (GCS) and kidneys (creatinine or urine output).

What you see

A ventilated, sedated patient on BP-support drugs, often on dialysis, with jaundice, bleeding tendency, swelling, confusion or coma. Long stays lead to ICU-acquired weakness.

Physiotherapy by problem

Failing organWhat it means for physiotherapy
Lungs (ARDS)See the ARDS plan: no disconnection, closed suction, positioning, prone help
CirculationMobilise only when MAP is stable on low support
Clotting (low platelets) and liverNo percussion; gentle suction; watch for bleeding and bruising
Kidneys (dialysis)Femoral dialysis lines do not stop exercise (green in the safety table), but secure them; watch fluid shifts and BP during dialysis
Brain (delirium, coma)Orientation, family, day–night routine; passive movement and positioning if unresponsive
Muscles (ICU-acquired weakness)Range of motion, positioning and splints early; active exercise and the mobilisation ladder as soon as safe

After the ICU: many survivors have post-intensive care syndrome: weakness, fatigue, breathlessness, memory and concentration problems, anxiety, depression and nightmares. Plan follow-up rehabilitation.

Exam points

  • Definition; causes; SOFA systems; organ-by-organ precautions; prevent ICU-acquired weakness; post-ICU follow-up.

ivNeuromuscular disease in the ICU

What it is

Weakness of the breathing and swallowing muscles from disease of the nerves, the neuromuscular junction or the muscles. Guillain–Barré syndrome is worked on the ICU physiotherapy page; the others in this section are:

  • Myasthenic crisis: myasthenia gravis (antibodies block acetylcholine receptors) worsening until breathing fails, often triggered by infection, surgery or some drugs.
  • Motor neurone disease (ALS): progressive loss of motor neurones; breathing failure is the usual cause of death.
  • Muscular dystrophies such as Duchenne: progressive muscle weakness, scoliosis, chest infections and night-time hypoventilation in the teens and twenties.
  • Critical illness polyneuropathy and myopathy: weakness that develops during critical illness itself (ICU-acquired weakness; see the bed rest page).
  • Neurotoxic snakebite (krait, cobra): see the poisoning section.

Warning signs of breathing failure

  • Falling vital capacity (below about 20 mL/kg is a warning sign), a weak cough, choking on food or saliva, a nasal voice (bulbar weakness).
  • Rising breathing rate, accessory muscle use, the abdomen moving in on inspiration (diaphragm weakness), breathlessness lying flat.
  • Morning headaches, daytime sleepiness and poor sleep (hypoventilation at night).
  • A rising PaCO₂ is a late sign; do not wait for it.
  • Peak cough flow: below about 270 L/min the cough may fail during a chest infection, so teach cough augmentation; below about 160 L/min the cough is ineffective.

Medical point that matters for physio

  • Myasthenic crisis: ventilation, IV immunoglobulin or plasma exchange, steroids. Too much anticholinesterase drug can cause a cholinergic crisis (weakness with excess secretions).
  • Motor neurone disease: non-invasive ventilation improves survival and quality of life in patients without severe bulbar weakness (trial, 2006); decisions about invasive ventilation are planned in advance with the patient.
  • Muscular dystrophy: night-time NIV, cough assist, scoliosis surgery, heart monitoring.

ICF summary

Impairments: weak breathing and cough muscles, retained secretions, swallowing weakness and aspiration risk, limb weakness, fatigue, contracture risk. Activity: mobility and self-care limited. Participation: depends on the disease course; work and family roles. Context: equipment (NIV, cough assist) at home, carers, advance care planning.

Physiotherapy by problem

  • Weak cough: manually assisted cough, cough assist, breath stacking (several breaths in without breathing out, with a bag and one-way valve, to reach a larger volume before coughing), suction when needed.
  • Low lung volume and night hypoventilation: support NIV (mask fit, timing); positioning; breath stacking for lung volume.
  • Secretions and aspiration: positioning, airway clearance; swallowing assessment by the speech and language therapist.
  • Limbs: range of motion and positioning against contractures; gentle, non-fatiguing strengthening in slowly progressive disease; energy conservation.
  • Myasthenia: short sessions with rests, timed when the drug is working (often about an hour after a pyridostigmine dose); stop at the first sign of fatigue.

Precautions

  • Avoid fatigue: overworked weak muscles get weaker.
  • Watch for the warning signs above and report early; aspiration risk with weak swallowing.
  • In progressive disease, respect the patient's choices about ventilation and comfort care.

Exam points

  • Warning signs of failure; peak cough flow 270 and 160 L/min; cough augmentation and breath stacking; NIV in motor neurone disease; avoid fatigue.

vSmoke inhalation

  • Heat injures the upper airway (swelling can close it within hours, so early intubation is common); smoke particles and chemicals injure the lower airways (bronchospasm, sloughing, casts, pneumonia, ARDS).
  • Carbon monoxide: binds haemoglobin; the pulse oximeter reads falsely normal, so a blood carboxyhaemoglobin level is needed; give high-flow oxygen through a reservoir mask. Cyanide from burning plastics also blocks oxygen use.
  • Physiotherapy: upright positioning, humidified oxygen, deep breathing, ACBT, suction and early mobilisation; details and precautions on the burns page (inhalation burns, 7 marks in W22).

viPoisoning

In India the commonest ICU poisonings are pesticides (organophosphates, often deliberate self-poisoning), snakebite, and drug overdose. Every self-poisoning patient needs a mental health assessment before discharge.

Organophosphate poisoning

Organophosphates block acetylcholinesterase, so acetylcholine builds up at every cholinergic junction.

  • Muscarinic, nicotinic and brain effects; bronchorrhoea needs suction; intermediate syndrome at 24–96 hours with neck flexor weakness; mental health assessment.

viiAspiration and near drowning

Aspiration

  • Aspiration pneumonitis: chemical injury from inhaled stomach contents (Mendelson's syndrome), causing sudden breathlessness and low oxygen within hours.
  • Aspiration pneumonia: infection from inhaled mouth bacteria, developing over days.
  • Who: reduced consciousness (head injury, overdose, alcohol, seizures), stroke and other swallowing problems, vomiting, tube feeding while flat, poor oral hygiene.
  • Where it goes: usually the right lung (the right main bronchus is wider and more vertical): the lower lobe basal segments when upright, the posterior segment of the upper lobe and the superior segment of the lower lobe when lying supine.
  • Physiotherapy: head up 30–45° and side-lying for at-risk patients; suction and airway clearance for the affected segments; mobilise; refer for a swallowing assessment; good oral care.

Drowning and near drowning

  • Drowning (WHO, 2002) is "the process of experiencing respiratory impairment from submersion or immersion in liquid"; the outcome may be death, survival with damage, or survival without damage. "Near drowning" is the older term for survivors.
  • Problems: severe hypoxaemia; aspirated water washes out surfactant, causing collapse and pulmonary oedema that can progress to ARDS; aspiration pneumonia; brain damage from lack of oxygen; hypothermia; neck injury in diving accidents.
  • Physiotherapy: as for ARDS and aspiration in the ventilated stage (positioning, careful airway clearance, no high pressures); spinal precautions until the neck is cleared; then neurological rehabilitation if the brain was injured.

viiiARDS

ARDS on SIMV (S24 Q3(b), 15 marks; acute phase, S25 Q2(c), 7 marks)

ARDS (Berlin definition, 2012): within 1 week of a known insult (pneumonia, sepsis, trauma, aspiration); white shadows in both lungs on X-ray; not fully explained by heart failure or fluid overload; PaO₂/FiO₂ on a PEEP of 5 or more: mild 201–300, moderate 101–200, severe 100 or less (mmHg). A 2024 global definition also includes patients on high-flow oxygen and allows SpO₂/FiO₂ when blood gases are not available.

  • Impairments (S24): poor oxygenation; stiff, low-volume lungs; possible secretions; inactive breathing muscles; reduced consciousness from sedation; limb stiffness and weakness; anxiety.
  • Likely complications: lung injury from the ventilator, pneumothorax, ventilator-associated pneumonia, ICU-acquired weakness, contractures, pressure injuries (especially if nursed prone), DVT and pulmonary embolism, delirium, and later reduced exercise capacity, anxiety and memory problems (post-intensive care syndrome).
  • Medical point: lung-protective ventilation (tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau pressure 30 or less), PEEP, careful fluids, and prone positioning for 16 hours or more a day when PaO₂/FiO₂ is below 150 (this lowered deaths in the PROSEVA trial).

Acute-phase physiotherapy (S25):

  • Minimal, clustered handling while oxygenation is poor; agree the plan with the ICU team.
  • No disconnection: closed suction only when secretions are present; ventilator hyperinflation rather than bagging; avoid high pressures (the lungs tear easily).
  • Positioning: head up 30–45°; regular turns; help the team with safe prone turning (protect eyes, face, shoulders, knees and lines).
  • Limbs: passive ROM and positioning against contractures and pressure injury.
  • As she improves (S24 settings, FiO₂ 0.4 and PEEP 5, are within the green zone): active exercise, sitting over the edge of the bed, standing and walking with the ventilator; inspiratory muscle training during weaning; after extubation, breathing exercises and a graded exercise programme, with follow-up rehabilitation.

ixShock

What it is

Circulatory failure: the tissues do not get enough oxygen. Signs: fast pulse, low BP (systolic below about 90 or MAP below 65 mmHg), cold clammy skin (warm in early septic shock), urine below 0.5 mL/kg/hour, confusion, raised blood lactate (above 2 mmol/L).

TypeCauseExamples
HypovolaemicToo little blood or fluidBleeding, burns, severe diarrhoea and vomiting
CardiogenicThe heart cannot pumpLarge MI, severe arrhythmia, end-stage heart failure
DistributiveVessels widen and leakSeptic shock, anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock after spinal cord injury (low BP with a slow pulse)
ObstructiveBlood flow is blockedTension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, massive pulmonary embolism

Sepsis (Sepsis-3, 2016): life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection. Septic shock: sepsis needing drugs to keep MAP at 65 mmHg or more, with lactate above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids.

Medical point that matters for physio

Fluids, drugs that raise BP (for example noradrenaline), antibiotics within an hour in sepsis, treatment of the cause; MAP usually kept at 65 mmHg or more (Surviving Sepsis Campaign, 2021).

Physiotherapy by problem

  • While unstable: no active mobilisation; positioning, pressure care and gentle passive movement only if BP allows; chest physiotherapy only for a clear need (retained secretions, collapse), kept short.
  • As support is weaned: follow the safety criteria (MAP above target on no or low support is green; high support or rising doses is a reason to wait) and start the mobilisation ladder (see the ICU safety table).
  • Recovery: survivors of sepsis are often very weak and tired for months; progressive strength and endurance training and follow-up.

Precautions

  • Turning and sitting up can drop BP sharply: monitor MAP during and after; stop if it falls below target, the pulse races or the patient becomes confused or clammy.

Exam points

  • Four types with examples; MAP 65; lactate above 2; sepsis and septic shock definitions; no active mobilisation while unstable.

xGuillain–Barré syndrome

GBS is the commonest neuromuscular disease seen in the ICU. Myasthenic crisis, motor neurone disease and muscular dystrophy are in (iv).

GBS on SIMV (S23 Q4(b), 15 marks)

The question

"A 45 year old school teacher is admitted as GBS and is on mechanical ventilator SIMV mode with PEEP 6 and FiO₂ of 0.4. Discuss his impairments and ventilator management."

Read the case: Guillain–Barré syndrome is an immune attack on peripheral nerves causing rapidly rising, symmetrical flaccid weakness. He needs a ventilator because his breathing muscles are weak, not because his lungs are diseased, so his FiO₂ and PEEP are modest.

Ventilator management, physiotherapy part:

  • Lungs: 2–4-hourly turns, head up 30–45°, manual or ventilator hyperinflation with vibrations, suction when secretions are present (watch the heart rate; pre-oxygenate).
  • Track strength: vital capacity and inspiratory pressure trends tell when weaning can start; a tracheostomy is common because recovery takes weeks.
  • Weaning: as strength returns, gradually more spontaneous breathing (lower SIMV rate, pressure support, breathing trials); inspiratory muscle training in short, non-fatiguing sets; upright sitting for trials.
  • Limbs: gentle passive ROM within pain, ankles at 90° with splints or pillows, hands in a functional position; then active-assisted and active exercise, avoiding fatigue (recovering muscles weaken further if overworked).
  • Other: pressure care, DVT prevention, tilt table for low BP on sitting up, a communication board, reassurance (most patients recover over months).

xiSpinal cord injury and other acute respiratory disorders

Acute spinal cord injury: the respiratory picture

What it is

Damage to the spinal cord, usually from trauma (falls from trees and roofs, road accidents, diving). The higher the level, the more breathing muscles are lost.

LevelBreathing muscles lostEffect
C1–C2Diaphragm and all othersNeeds a ventilator (or phrenic pacing) for life
C3–C5Diaphragm partly (phrenic nerve C3, 4, 5), intercostals, abdominalsOften ventilated at first; some wean as the diaphragm recovers
C6–C8Intercostals and abdominals; diaphragm worksLow vital capacity, very weak cough, paradoxical upper-chest movement
T1–T12Some intercostals and abdominals (abdominals T6–T12)Weak cough; the higher the level, the weaker
Memory hook

C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive

What you see in the ICU

  • Spinal shock: flaccid paralysis and no reflexes below the level for days to weeks.
  • Neurogenic shock (injuries at T6 and above): low BP with a slow heart rate.
  • More bronchial secretions (unopposed vagal activity), a weak cough, collapse and pneumonia (the commonest early complications); gut distension that pushes the diaphragm up.
  • Vital capacity falls sharply after a cervical injury and often improves over the following weeks to months as spinal shock settles.

Medical point that matters for physio

  • The spine is stabilised (collar, traction or surgery); until then strict spinal precautions and log-rolling.
  • BP support (a mean arterial pressure of about 85–90 mmHg is often targeted for the first week); ventilation and early tracheostomy in high injuries; DVT prophylaxis.

ICF summary

Impairments: paralysis and sensory loss, low vital capacity, weak cough, secretion retention, low BP, risk of pressure sores, contractures and DVT. Activity: dependent for all mobility and self-care. Participation: work, family and social life for life; major adjustment. Context: home access, carers, funding for equipment.

Physiotherapy by problem

  • Weak cough: manually assisted cough (an upward and inward push on the abdomen or lower ribs timed with the cough), cough assist (mechanical insufflation–exsufflation), suction.
  • Low lung volume: diaphragmatic breathing; lying flat or semi-reclined when breathless (the abdominal contents support the diaphragm, so vital capacity is often better lying than sitting); an abdominal binder when sitting up; respiratory muscle training (it improves vital capacity and respiratory muscle strength in tetraplegia, Cochrane 2013); manual or ventilator hyperinflation if ventilated.
  • Secretions: positioning and turns (log-roll), chest vibrations and shaking, assisted cough.
  • Limbs and skin: passive range of motion within spinal limits, positioning and splints, 2–3-hourly turns, pressure care.
  • Mobilisation once the spine is stable: sitting up in stages, tilt table for low BP, then wheelchair and rehabilitation.

Precautions

  • Spine not yet stable: no active mobilisation; log-roll only.
  • Suction or turning can cause severe bradycardia (atropine at hand).
  • Autonomic dysreflexia (injuries at T6 and above, after spinal shock): sudden very high BP, pounding headache, flushing and sweating above the level, slow pulse, usually triggered by a full bladder or bowel. Sit the patient up, loosen tight clothing, check the catheter and bowel, call for help.
  • Postural hypotension when sitting up; poor temperature control; no assisted cough over a fresh abdominal wound or ileus.

Exam points

  • Level decides breathing; C3, 4, 5; assisted cough; better vital capacity lying than sitting; abdominal binder; autonomic dysreflexia at T6 and above.

Test yourself

3 mList five safety criteria that make a ventilated patient "green" for out-of-bed mobilisation.

FiO₂ 0.6 or less; SpO₂ 90% or more; respiratory rate 30 or less; PEEP 10 cmH₂O or less; MAP above the lower target with no or low-dose support; calm, drowsy or restless (RASS −1 to +1); no active bleeding or unstable fracture.

3 mGive the Berlin severity grades of ARDS.

PaO₂/FiO₂ with PEEP 5 or more: mild 201–300, moderate 101–200, severe 100 or less (mmHg).

MCQA patient with GBS on SIMV becomes suddenly bradycardic during suction. The most likely reason is:A. PneumothoraxB. Autonomic instability with a vagal reflexC. Low PEEPD. Fever

B. GBS affects autonomic nerves; tracheal stimulation triggers a vagal slowing of the heart. Pre-oxygenate, keep passes short, and stop if the heart rate falls.

MCQAn MRC sum score below which value defines ICU-acquired weakness?A. 60B. 48C. 36D. 24

B. Below 48 out of 60.

3 mWhy does vital capacity often improve when a C6 tetraplegic patient lies flat?

With paralysed abdominal muscles, sitting lets the abdominal contents drop and the diaphragm flatten. Lying flat lets the abdominal contents push the diaphragm up into a domed, longer position, so it contracts more effectively. An abdominal binder copies this in sitting.

3 mGive the signs of autonomic dysreflexia and the first action.

Sudden very high BP, pounding headache, flushing and sweating above the level of injury, slow pulse, in an injury at T6 or above. Sit the patient up, loosen tight clothing, check the bladder catheter and bowel for the trigger, and call for help.

MCQIn severe head injury, which part of chest physiotherapy raises ICP the most?A. Chest vibrationsB. Positioning head up 30°C. Airway suctionD. Passive ankle movements

C. Suction raised ICP briefly in a study of sedated patients; vibrations did not. Sedate and pre-oxygenate, and keep suction short.

MCQA patient with tetanus has spasms whenever touched. Best approach:A. Vigorous percussion every 2 hoursB. Short, calm sessions timed with sedation in a quiet, dim roomC. No physiotherapy at allD. Early resisted exercise

B. Coordinate with sedation, minimise stimuli, and keep the chest clear and joints mobile.

3 mWhy is airway clearance not used for the pink frothy sputum of pulmonary oedema?

It is oedema fluid leaking into the alveoli from high capillary pressure, not retained secretions. It clears with treatment of the cause (diuretics, nitrates, CPAP). Airway clearance would only tire the patient.

3 mName the four types of shock with one example each.

Hypovolaemic (bleeding); cardiogenic (large MI); distributive (septic shock; anaphylaxis; neurogenic after spinal cord injury); obstructive (tension pneumothorax).

MCQOn day 3 after organophosphate poisoning, a patient cannot lift his head off the pillow. This suggests:A. RecoveryB. Intermediate syndromeC. Atropine excessD. Normal fatigue

B. Neck flexor weakness 24–96 hours after poisoning is the early sign; breathing muscles may follow, so report it at once.

MCQIn carbon monoxide poisoning, the pulse oximeter reading is usually:A. Falsely lowB. Falsely normalC. AccurateD. Zero

B. The oximeter cannot tell carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin.
