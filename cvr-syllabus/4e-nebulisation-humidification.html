4(e)Nebulisation and humidification, with oxygen therapy

Seven papers asked humidification and nebulisers (definitions, uses, jet and ultrasonic nebulisers, indications and types, inhalers) and four asked oxygen (low-flow oxygen, delivery methods, hazards, hypoxic drive). Oxygen is not a separate syllabus chapter; it sits here because the practical list puts it with nebulisation under medication delivery.

My revision

1Definitions (3 marks in S21)

  • Humidification: adding water vapour (a gas) to inspired air or oxygen, to warm and moisten it.
  • Nebulisation: turning a liquid (usually a drug solution) into an aerosol of fine droplets that can be breathed into the airways.
  • Aerosol: tiny liquid or solid particles suspended in a gas.

2Humidification (3 marks in S17 and W17)

Normal airway conditioning

The nose and upper airway warm and humidify inspired air so that, a few centimetres below the carina, it is at body temperature (37 °C) and fully saturated (100% relative humidity, about 44 mg of water per litre). This point is called the isothermic saturation boundary.

Why intubated patients need it (W17)

An endotracheal tube or tracheostomy bypasses the nose and throat, so dry, cold gas reaches the trachea. This causes thick, sticky secretions, slows the cilia, damages the airway lining, blocks the tube, causes collapse and loses body heat and water. The AARC (2012) strongly recommends humidification for every invasively ventilated patient.

TypeHow it worksPoints
Heat and moisture exchanger (HME, "artificial nose")Passive: traps heat and moisture from each breath out and returns it on the next breath inShould deliver at least 30 mg H₂O/L; cheap, simple. Uses (S17): intubated and tracheostomy patients; high-flow oxygen used for long periods; thick secretions; NIV comfort; dry upper airway.

Hazards: overheating and airway burns (heated systems); water collecting in the tubing (can be washed into the patient, spreads infection, triggers false breaths); infection from contaminated water; extra resistance and dead space with an HME, which can block with secretions.

3Nebulisation (7 marks in S20 and S24)

Particle size decides where the drug lands

Droplet sizeWhere it deposits
Larger than 10 µmMouth, nose and throat (swallowed)

Types of nebuliser

TypePrincipleAdvantagesDisadvantages
Jet (pneumatic)Compressed air or oxygen (about 6–8 L/min) rushes through a narrow opening; the low pressure (Bernoulli or Venturi effect) draws the liquid up a fine tube and shatters it into droplets; a baffle knocks out the large onesCheap, widely available, works with most drugs and suspensionsNoisy; slow (10–15 minutes); some drug left in the cup; needs a compressor or gas supply
UltrasonicA piezoelectric crystal vibrates at high frequency; waves on the liquid surface throw off dropletsQuiet; fast; large outputCan warm and damage some drugs; poor for suspensions (for example budesonide); costly
Vibrating meshA vibrating plate pushes the liquid through a mesh with thousands of tiny holesVery efficient; little drug wasted; quiet, small, battery-powered; used in ventilator circuitsExpensive; mesh needs careful cleaning

Indications (S24) and three conditions with purpose (S19)

  • Acute severe asthma or COPD exacerbation: bronchodilators (salbutamol, ipratropium) to relieve bronchospasm.
  • Cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis: hypertonic saline or dornase alfa to loosen sputum before airway clearance; inhaled antibiotics to control infection.
  • Ventilated patients: bronchodilators given in-line.
  • Children with croup: nebulised adrenaline or steroid as prescribed.
  • Sputum induction for TB and other tests (hypertonic saline).
  • Anyone who cannot use an inhaler: very young, very old, confused or very breathless patients.

Contraindications and precautions

  • Known allergy to the drug.
  • Hypertonic saline can cause bronchospasm: give a bronchodilator first and check peak flow or spirometry with the first dose.
  • Patients at risk of high CO₂ (COPD): drive the nebuliser with compressed air, not oxygen, and give oxygen separately by nasal cannula (BTS).
  • Side effects: fast heart rate and tremor (salbutamol), low potassium with frequent high doses.
  • Infection control: nebulising can spread infection (aerosol-generating); clean, dry and do not share equipment.

Technique

  1. Sit upright; fill the cup (usually 2–5 mL).
  2. Use a mouthpiece where possible (less drug lost to the face and eyes); a mask for children and very breathless patients.
  3. Breathe normally through the mouth, with an occasional slow deep breath.
  4. Continue until the nebuliser starts to splutter.
  5. Rinse the mouth after steroids; wash and dry the parts.

4Inhalers (3 marks in W25)

Metered-dose inhaler (MDI)

A pressurised canister holds the drug with a propellant; a metering valve releases a fixed dose with each press (for example 100 µg of salbutamol per puff).

  1. Remove the cap and shake the inhaler.
  2. Breathe out gently, away from the inhaler.
  3. Seal the lips around the mouthpiece.
  4. Start a slow, deep breath in (about 3–5 seconds) and press the canister once at the start.
  5. Hold the breath for up to 10 seconds, then breathe out slowly.
  6. Wait about 30–60 seconds before a second puff; rinse the mouth after a steroid inhaler.
  • Advantages: small, quick, cheap, many drugs available.
  • Disadvantages: needs hand–breath coordination; much of the dose hits the throat; cold spray can stop the breath.
  • Spacer (holding chamber): the puff goes into the chamber and is breathed in over a few breaths, so coordination matters less and less drug lands in the throat. With a face mask for young children. In acute asthma an MDI with a spacer works as well as a nebuliser (Cochrane, 2013).

Dry powder inhaler (DPI)

Drug powder released by the patient's own fast, deep breath in (for example Rotahaler with capsules, Accuhaler, Turbuhaler). No propellant and no coordination needed, but the patient must breathe in quickly and strongly, so it does not suit young children or a severe attack. Do not breathe out into it (moisture spoils the powder).

5Oxygen therapy (practical list, item 2): what oxygen is for

Oxygen is a drug given to correct or prevent hypoxaemia (low oxygen in the blood). It does not treat breathlessness itself, and it should be prescribed with a target saturation.

Target SpO₂ (BTS)
PatientTarget
Most acutely ill adults94–98%
At risk of high CO₂ (COPD, severe obesity, chest wall deformity, neuromuscular disease, cystic fibrosis with high CO₂)88–92%, adjusted with blood gases
Critically ill, peri-arrest, carbon monoxide poisoningHigh concentration first (reservoir mask 15 L/min), then titrate
  • Indications: low SpO₂ in acute illness (pneumonia, asthma, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, after surgery); critical illness; carbon monoxide poisoning; desaturation on exercise; long-term oxygen in chronic hypoxaemia (criteria on the COPD page).
  • Not routinely for breathless patients with normal saturation, or in heart attack when SpO₂ is 90% or more.

6Oxygen therapy: methods of delivery (3 marks in S23)

Devices are grouped by whether they supply all the air the patient breathes in. Low-flow (variable-performance) devices give less than the patient's inspiratory flow, so room air mixes in and the FiO₂ changes with the breathing pattern. High-flow (fixed-performance) devices supply all of it, so the FiO₂ stays fixed.

FiO₂ figures for low-flow devices are approximate and vary between sources, because they depend on the patient's breathing. Sources of oxygen: piped wall supply, cylinders, oxygen concentrators at home.

7Low-flow oxygen therapy (7 marks in S20)

  • Definition: oxygen given at a flow lower than the patient's own inspiratory flow, so the gas breathed is a mix of oxygen and room air and the FiO₂ varies.
  • Devices: nasal cannula, simple face mask, reservoir mask, tracheostomy mask (oxygen catheter in older texts).
  • Uses: mild to moderate hypoxaemia in stable patients; home and ambulatory oxygen; during exercise in patients who desaturate.
  • Advantages: simple, cheap, comfortable (cannula), patient can eat, talk and walk.
  • Disadvantages: FiO₂ is not known exactly (higher with slow, shallow breathing, lower with fast breathing), so it is less suitable when the CO₂ level must be controlled precisely; nasal dryness at higher flows.
  • Humidification: not needed for low-flow oxygen; consider it for high flows used for more than about 24 hours, for a tracheostomy, or if the nose and mouth feel dry.
  • Monitoring: SpO₂ against the prescribed target, breathing rate, drowsiness (rising CO₂), ABG in those at risk.

8Hazards of oxygen therapy (3 marks in S25)

HazardDetails
CO₂ retention (hypercapnic respiratory failure)In COPD and other at-risk patients, uncontrolled high oxygen raises CO₂ (worse V/Q matching and the Haldane effect; reduced drive plays a small part): drowsiness, confusion, acidosis. See hypoxic drive
Absorption atelectasisHigh FiO₂ washes nitrogen out of alveoli; the oxygen is absorbed and poorly ventilated alveoli collapse
Oxygen toxicityLung injury from prolonged high concentrations (FiO₂ above about 0.6 for long periods)
Retinopathy of prematurityHigh oxygen damages developing retinal vessels in preterm babies
FireOxygen feeds fire: no smoking, no flames or chulha nearby, no oil or grease on fittings; burns of the face in smokers using oxygen
Masking deteriorationA good SpO₂ on oxygen can hide worsening breathing; watch the rate and the patient
Local problemsDry nose and mouth, nosebleeds, pressure sores behind the ears and on the nose; masks cause claustrophobia and aspiration risk if vomiting
EquipmentTrips over tubing; empty cylinders during walks; heavy cylinders

9Oxygen therapy: the physiotherapist's role

  • Check the prescription and target before treatment; never stop or change oxygen without it.
  • Exercise: measure SpO₂ during walking; give oxygen (as prescribed) to keep SpO₂ above about 88–90% during training; use portable cylinders or concentrators for ambulatory oxygen.
  • Keep oxygen on during airway clearance and positioning when needed; watch SpO₂ after turns and suction.
  • Teach long-term oxygen users: at least 15 hours a day, safety (no smoking), cleaning the cannula, and pacing.
  • Pulse oximetry limits: wrong readings with cold fingers, poor circulation, movement, nail polish and carbon monoxide (falsely normal); oximeters can over-read in darker skin, so act on the whole clinical picture.

Test yourself

3 mWhy must gas be humidified for an intubated patient?

The tube bypasses the nose and upper airway that normally warm and humidify air. Dry, cold gas thickens secretions, slows the cilia, damages the airway lining, blocks the tube and causes collapse. Humidification restores near-normal conditions (heated humidifier 33–44 mg/L, or an HME giving at least 30 mg/L).

MCQThe droplet size best for reaching small airways is:A. Over 10 µmB. 5–10 µmC. 1–5 µmD. Under 0.1 µm

C. 1–5 µm is the respirable range.

MCQThe part of a jet nebuliser that removes large droplets is the:A. Piezoelectric crystalB. BaffleC. MeshD. Metering valve

B. Large droplets hit the baffle and fall back into the cup.

3 mList three low-flow and two high-flow oxygen devices with flows.

Low-flow: nasal cannula 1–4 L/min; simple face mask 5–10 L/min; reservoir mask 15 L/min. High-flow (fixed performance): Venturi mask (flow printed on the valve, FiO₂ 24–60%); high-flow nasal cannula (up to about 60 L/min).

MCQA COPD patient at risk of CO₂ retention needs oxygen. The best first choice is:A. Reservoir mask 15 L/minB. Simple face mask 3 L/minC. 24% or 28% Venturi mask, target SpO₂ 88–92%D. No oxygen

C. A fixed, low FiO₂ with the 88–92% target; a simple mask below 5 L/min risks rebreathing CO₂.

MCQWhich hazard of oxygen is caused by nitrogen washout?A. Absorption atelectasisB. Retinopathy of prematurityC. NosebleedsD. Fire

A. Without nitrogen to hold them open, poorly ventilated alveoli collapse as the oxygen is absorbed.
