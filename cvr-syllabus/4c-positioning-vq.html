4(c)Therapeutic positioning to improve ventilation and perfusion matching

Positioning is the cheapest treatment in the ICU and the papers keep asking about it: positioning in ICU (W22, W25), sitting upright (W21), zones of the lung (S25), causes of altered V/Q (S23) and positions that ease breathlessness (S22). One idea runs through all of them: gravity decides where air and blood go.

My revision

1Ventilation and perfusion in the upright lung

  • Ventilation (V) is the air reaching the alveoli (about 4 L/min at rest); perfusion (Q) is the blood flowing past them (about 5 L/min). The overall V/Q ratio is therefore about 0.8.
  • In upright lungs both ventilation and perfusion are greater at the base than the apex, but perfusion rises much more steeply, because gravity pulls blood down.
  • So V/Q is high at the apex (about 3) and low at the base (about 0.6).
  • Basal alveoli get more ventilation because they start smaller (the pleural pressure there is less negative), so they are on the steep, easily filled part of the pressure–volume curve.
If the question means the airway zones instead

Some books describe the conducting zone (trachea to terminal bronchioles, airway generations 0–16: no gas exchange; anatomical dead space about 150 mL in an adult) and the respiratory zone (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveolar sacs, generations 17–23: gas exchange). Write both if the wording is unclear.

2Causes of altered V/Q (3 marks in S23)

TypeMeaningCauses
Low V/Q (shunt when no ventilation)Blood passes alveoli that get too little airSecretions and mucus plugs; bronchospasm; collapse (atelectasis); consolidation (pneumonia); pulmonary oedema; ARDS; lying flat, obesity, pain-limited breathing after surgery
High V/Q (dead space when no perfusion)Air reaches alveoli that get too little bloodPulmonary embolism; low cardiac output or blood loss; emphysema (capillaries destroyed); high PEEP or over-stretched alveoli; pulmonary vascular disease

Both lower PaO₂. Low V/Q improves with oxygen and with positions that send air to the affected area; a true shunt (no ventilation at all) barely responds to oxygen until the alveoli are re-opened.

SupineAbdominal contents push the diaphragm up; FRC falls; the back of the lungs collapses; aspiration risk risesAvoid long periods; exception: tetraplegia, where vital capacity is often better lying than sitting (use an abdominal binder in sitting)

4Sitting upright as a therapeutic position (7 marks in W21)

SystemPhysiological application
RespiratoryDiaphragm descends and abdominal contents fall away: larger FRC and tidal volume; fewer collapsed basal alveoli; better V/Q and oxygenation; lower work of breathing; a deeper breath before a cough, so a stronger cough; less reflux and aspiration
CardiovascularKeeps the blood pressure reflexes working (prevents the fall in BP on standing that follows bed rest); with legs down it lowers venous return, which eases breathlessness in pulmonary oedema and heart failure (orthopnoea)
Neurological and otherWakefulness, orientation, communication and safer swallowing; head-up lowers intracranial pressure; first step towards standing and walking; relieves pressure on the back

Used in: post-operative patients, ICU and ventilated patients (including breathing trials), COPD and asthma (with forward lean), pulmonary oedema, neurological patients, older people on bed rest. Watch for sliding into a slumped position, which squashes the abdomen and loads the sacrum.

5Positioning in the ICU (7 marks in W22 and W25)

Physiological effects and benefits (W25)

  • Improves V/Q matching and oxygenation; increases lung volume; re-opens collapsed areas.
  • Drains secretions with gravity; lowers the work of breathing.
  • Head-up positions reduce aspiration and ventilator-associated pneumonia.
  • Regular turns prevent pressure injuries, contractures and nerve compression.
  • Upright positions keep the blood pressure reflexes and muscles working; they prepare for mobilisation and raise alertness.
  • Comfort, communication and dignity.

Before and after every turn

Check SpO₂, heart rate and BP; secure the tube, lines and drains; give the patient time to settle (oxygenation can dip for several minutes after a turn).

Test yourself

3 mWrite the pressure relationships of West's three zones.

Zone 1: alveolar > arterial > venous (no flow). Zone 2: arterial > alveolar > venous (flow depends on arterial minus alveolar). Zone 3: arterial > venous > alveolar (continuous flow).

MCQAn adult with right lower lobe pneumonia, breathing on his own, has SpO₂ 89%. Which position will most likely raise his SpO₂?A. Right side-lyingB. Left side-lyingC. Flat supineD. Head-down tilt

B. Good lung down: the healthy left lung below gets more ventilation and more blood.

MCQThe V/Q ratio at the apex of the upright lung is about:A. 0.6B. 0.8C. 1.0D. 3

D. Little blood reaches the apex, so ventilation exceeds perfusion.
