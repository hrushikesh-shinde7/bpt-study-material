6(b)(i)–(ii)Bronchitis and emphysema (COPD)

COPD was in 13 of 20 papers (2016–2026). The long cases (2016–2020) describe a long-time smoker, breathless on daily activities, living upstairs with no lift; newer papers ask short questions on PFT, X-ray, ABG, hypoxic drive and leg-muscle training. The answer is the same each time: ICF from the story, then acute-stage and long-term management with pulmonary rehab doses.

My revision

iChronic bronchitis

Chronic bronchitis (clinical definition): cough with sputum on most days for at least 3 months a year, for 2 years in a row, with other causes excluded.

  • Main problem: inflammation from smoke and dust narrows the small airways, enlarges the mucus glands and damages the cilia, so there is cough, sputum and repeated infection.
  • Classic picture: marked productive cough, moderate breathlessness; often overweight, cyanosed, with ankle oedema (cor pulmonale); low oxygen and high CO₂ early.
  • X-ray: often near-normal, or more prominent lung markings ("dirty chest") and thickened bronchial walls seen as tram lines.

Physiotherapy puts airway clearance first (4(a)), within the COPD plan below.

iiEmphysema

Emphysema (structural definition): permanent enlargement of the air spaces beyond the terminal bronchioles, with destruction of their walls.

  • Main problem: destroyed alveolar walls lose the elastic recoil that holds the small airways open. Airways collapse during breathing out, air is trapped, and the flattened diaphragm works at a mechanical disadvantage.
  • Classic picture: severe breathlessness early, little sputum; thin, pursed-lip breathing, barrel chest, accessory muscles; oxygen kept near normal until late; diffusing capacity (DLCO) reduced.
  • X-ray (3 marks in W18): hyperinflated, very black lung fields, low flattened diaphragms, a long narrow heart; full list in section 6.
  • Bullous emphysema: avoid high-pressure techniques (high PEP, aggressive manual hyperinflation): risk of pneumothorax.

Physiotherapy puts breathing control, pursed-lip breathing and positions of ease first, with pulmonary rehabilitation for the long term.

Most patients have features of both, so the two are managed together as COPD in sections 1 to 16 below. The classic comparison table is in section 2.

1Definition

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common, preventable lung condition with long-lasting breathlessness, cough and sputum, caused by damage to the airways (bronchitis, bronchiolitis) and the alveoli (emphysema). The airflow limitation is persistent and usually progressive.

The diagnosis needs spirometry: a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70.

  • Chronic bronchitis (clinical definition): cough with sputum on most days for at least 3 months a year, for 2 years in a row, with other causes excluded.
  • Emphysema (structural definition): permanent enlargement of the air spaces beyond the terminal bronchioles, with destruction of their walls.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Tobacco smoking: cigarettes, bidi, hookah. Risk rises with pack-years (packs a day × years smoked).
  • Household air pollution: cooking on wood, dung or crop waste in a poorly ventilated kitchen. A major cause in Indian women who have never smoked.
  • Occupational dust and fumes: sweepers, farm workers, miners, textile and construction workers (the W17 sweeper, S17 labourer, S20 farmer).
  • Outdoor air pollution.
  • Past tuberculosis, repeated childhood chest infections, poor lung growth.
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency: a rare genetic cause of early emphysema.

Effects of smoking on pulmonary function (3 marks in S17 and W22)

  • Paralyses and destroys cilia, so mucus clearance fails.
  • Enlarges mucus glands and goblet cells, so sputum increases.
  • Inflames the airways, narrowing them and making them more reactive.
  • Releases enzymes (proteases) that destroy alveolar walls: emphysema.
  • FEV1 falls faster than with normal ageing; diffusing capacity falls.
  • Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin and lowers oxygen carriage; infections become more common.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Inflammation from smoke and dust narrows the small airways, enlarges mucus glands and damages cilia: cough, sputum, infections.
  2. Destruction of alveolar walls (emphysema) removes the elastic recoil that holds small airways open. Airways collapse during breathing out.
  3. Air gets trapped: hyperinflation. The diaphragm is pushed down and flattened, so it works at a mechanical disadvantage. Accessory muscles take over.
  4. When breathing speeds up during exercise, there is less time to breathe out, so trapping worsens (dynamic hyperinflation). This is the main cause of breathlessness on exertion.
  5. Mismatched ventilation and blood flow lowers oxygen; in advanced disease CO₂ rises.
  6. Long-term low oxygen narrows the lung blood vessels, raising pressure: right heart failure (cor pulmonale).
  7. Breathlessness leads to inactivity, which weakens the limb muscles, which makes breathlessness worse: the breathlessness spiral.
Recall check. Explain in three sentences why a COPD patient becomes more breathless when he walks faster.

5Clinical features

  • Breathlessness on exertion that slowly worsens over years; chronic cough, often worse in the morning; sputum.
  • Wheeze, chest tightness, tiredness, weight loss in advanced disease.
  • Signs: barrel chest (increased front-to-back diameter), use of accessory muscles, pursed-lip breathing, tripod (forward-lean) posture, reduced chest expansion, hyper-resonant percussion, quiet breath sounds with prolonged expiration and wheeze.
  • Inward movement of the lower ribs on breathing in (Hoover's sign) from a flattened diaphragm.
  • Cyanosis, ankle oedema and raised neck veins in cor pulmonale.
Acute exacerbation

A sudden worsening of breathlessness, cough or sputum (more, or purulent) beyond day-to-day variation, usually triggered by infection or pollution. Look for breathlessness at rest, confusion or drowsiness (rising CO₂), and the use of accessory muscles. This is the picture in the W17 and S20 cases.

6Investigations

Pulmonary function tests in obstructive disease (3 marks in W20; PFT in COPD, W19)

  • FEV1 reduced; FVC normal or slightly reduced.
  • FEV1/FVC below 0.70 after bronchodilator.
  • Little improvement after bronchodilator (asthma shows large reversibility).
  • Peak expiratory flow reduced; flow–volume loop shows a "scooped" expiratory curve.
  • Lung volumes: residual volume (RV), total lung capacity (TLC) and RV/TLC raised (air trapping).
  • Diffusing capacity (DLCO) reduced in emphysema.

Chest X-ray in emphysema (3 marks in W18; X-ray in COPD, W24)

  • Hyperinflated, very black (hyperlucent) lung fields with fewer vessel markings at the edges.
  • Low, flattened diaphragms; more than 6 anterior or 10 posterior ribs visible above the diaphragm.
  • Ribs more horizontal, intercostal spaces wider.
  • Long, narrow ("tubular") heart shadow.
  • Increased air space behind the sternum on the lateral view; bullae may be seen.

Chronic bronchitis often shows a near-normal film, or more prominent lung markings ("dirty chest") and thickened bronchial walls seen as parallel lines (tram lines). With pulmonary hypertension the pulmonary arteries at the hila look large; with cor pulmonale the heart shadow enlarges.

7Medical management

  • Stop smoking and reduce dust and smoke exposure. This is the only measure that slows the loss of lung function.
  • Inhaled bronchodilators: long-acting LAMA and LABA, with reliever salbutamol. Inhaled steroids added in group E when blood eosinophils are high.
  • Vaccines: influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19 and others as advised.
  • Pulmonary rehabilitation for symptomatic patients and after a hospital admission.
  • Long-term oxygen therapy (ATS 2020) if resting PaO₂ is 55 mmHg or less (SpO₂ 88% or less), or 56–59 mmHg (SpO₂ 89%) with oedema, polycythaemia or right heart strain. Used for 15 hours a day or more.
  • Exacerbations: nebulised bronchodilators, a short course of oral steroids, antibiotics if sputum is purulent, controlled oxygen, and non-invasive ventilation (NIV) for acidotic hypercapnic respiratory failure. Intubation and ventilation if NIV fails (S20 patient on SIMV).
  • Surgery in selected patients: bullectomy, lung volume reduction, transplant.
Oxygen target

In COPD with a risk of CO₂ retention, aim for SpO₂ 88–92%, not 94–98%. Too much oxygen can raise CO₂ and make the patient drowsy.

Classic answer vs current understanding: hypoxic drive (3 marks in W25)

Classic

In chronic CO₂ retention the brain stops responding to CO₂, so breathing is driven by low oxygen (the hypoxic drive). Give high oxygen and this drive is removed, breathing slows and CO₂ rises.

Current (BTS emergency oxygen guideline)

Reduced drive plays only a small part. Most of the CO₂ rise comes from worse ventilation–perfusion matching: oxygen opens vessels in poorly ventilated lung that were narrowed by low oxygen, so more blood goes where little air goes. The Haldane effect (oxygenated haemoglobin carries less CO₂) adds to it. Never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient; control it instead.

Write in the exam

Define hypoxic drive, then: "Uncontrolled oxygen can cause CO₂ narcosis. Give controlled oxygen (Venturi mask or low-flow nasal cannula) to SpO₂ 88–92%, and check ABG."

9PT assessment

  • Subjective: breathlessness (mMRC), cough and sputum (amount, colour, morning load), wheeze, exacerbations in the last year, smoking pack-years, oxygen use, inhaler technique, sleep position, ADL that cause breathlessness, home (floor, lift, toilet), occupation and dust exposure, earning role.
  • Observation: posture (forward lean), chest shape, breathing pattern and rate, accessory muscles, pursed-lip breathing, cyanosis, clubbing (suggests another cause), oedema.
  • Palpation: chest expansion at three levels, trachea position.
  • Auscultation: breath sounds, wheeze, crackles.
  • Vital signs and SpO₂ at rest and on exertion.
  • Exercise capacity: 6MWT or incremental shuttle walk test with SpO₂ and Borg breathlessness.
  • Muscle strength: quadriceps, grip; respiratory muscle strength (PImax) if available.

Spot the ICF clues before you write

12PT management

Acute exacerbation (in hospital)

  • Positions that ease breathlessness: high side-lying; sitting leaning forward with forearms on a table or knees; standing leaning forward on a wall or railing. These lift the abdominal contents and let the flattened diaphragm work better.
  • Breathing control: gentle, relaxed breathing using the lower chest, shoulders relaxed; pursed-lip breathing during effort.
  • Airway clearance if sputum is a problem: ACBT with huffing; positive expiratory pressure devices (PEP, flutter) if helpful; postural drainage only if it clearly helps (see the classic-vs-current box).
  • Support NIV: help the patient tolerate the mask, position upright, clear secretions during breaks.
  • Early mobilisation: sit out, walk with oxygen if needed, keep SpO₂ 88–92%.
  • On a ventilator (S20, SIMV with right lower lobe pneumonia): regular repositioning (affected lung up for drainage), suction, manual techniques where appropriate, careful hyperinflation (risk of air trapping and pneumothorax), early sitting and limb exercises, inspiratory muscle training during weaning.
  • Before discharge: refer to pulmonary rehab to start within a few weeks.

Classic answer vs current guideline: postural drainage and percussion in COPD

Classic

Postural drainage with percussion and vibrations for every COPD patient with sputum.

Current (BTS/ACPRC 2009)

Use active techniques first (ACBT, PEP). Add postural drainage only if it clearly helps and causes no harm. Head-down tilt can worsen breathlessness.

Write in the exam

"ACBT and huffing as first choice; PEP devices if helpful; postural drainage only if effective and tolerated."

Pulmonary rehabilitation (long-term)

An individually prescribed programme of exercise training, education and behaviour change. Usually 6–12 weeks, with at least 2 supervised sessions a week plus home exercise. It improves breathlessness, exercise capacity and quality of life, and after a hospital admission it reduces readmissions (ATS 2023: strong recommendation).

OxygenGive oxygen during training if SpO₂ falls below about 88%
  • Why strengthen the leg and arm muscles (3 marks in W24): COPD weakens and wastes limb muscles (inactivity, low oxygen, inflammation, steroids, poor nutrition). Weak thighs tire early and produce lactic acid sooner, which drives breathing harder, so many patients stop exercise because of leg fatigue, not breathlessness. Strength training increases muscle strength and size, improves exercise tolerance and daily tasks (stairs, standing up, carrying), lowers breathlessness at the same workload, helps bone health and reduces falls. It also causes less breathlessness than endurance training, so very breathless patients can do it.
  • Inspiratory muscle training: for weak breathing muscles, at least 30% of maximal inspiratory pressure, about 30 breaths twice a day.
  • Education: disease, inhalers, airway clearance, energy conservation, action plan, smoking cessation, nutrition.
  • Maintenance: keep exercising at home or in community groups after the programme ends.

Pursed-lip breathing (3 marks in S20 and W22)

Technique: breathe in through the nose, then breathe out slowly through loosely pursed lips (as if blowing on hot tea), taking about twice as long to breathe out as to breathe in. Do not force the air out.

Rationale and effects:

  • Creates back-pressure in the airways, which splints the small airways open during breathing out.
  • Less air trapping, so less hyperinflation.
  • Slower, deeper breathing: lower breathing rate, better tidal volume and oxygen.
  • Reduces breathlessness and anxiety, and helps during walking and stair climbing.

Energy conservation (7 marks in S19)

  • Plan: spread heavy tasks across the week; do them at the time of day you feel best.
  • Pace: slow, steady movements; rest before you get very breathless; breathe out on effort.
  • Prioritise: drop or delegate tasks that are not essential.
  • Position: sit to wash, dress and cook; keep often-used things between waist and shoulder height; avoid bending and reaching overhead.
  • Use aids: trolley, long-handled sponge, shower stool, raised toilet seat.
  • Stairs: climb slowly, one step per out-breath, rest on landings; plan trips so he climbs to the 2nd floor fewer times.

Why it matters: it lowers the oxygen cost of daily tasks, so the patient stays independent and less breathless.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Keep SpO₂ at 88–92% in patients with high CO₂; do not give high-flow oxygen without a prescription.
  • Avoid head-down positions in breathless patients, and after meals.
  • Bullous emphysema: avoid high-pressure techniques (high PEP, aggressive manual hyperinflation); risk of pneumothorax.
  • Steroid-related osteoporosis: no vigorous percussion over fragile ribs.
  • Check heart disease, which is common in COPD.
  • Do not start exercise training during an acute exacerbation; manage the exacerbation first.
Stop exercise if
  • SpO₂ falls below about 85–88% despite oxygen
  • Severe breathlessness (Borg above 7), chest pain, dizziness, confusion
  • New irregular pulse, or a marked fall in BP

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Stop all tobacco; avoid second-hand smoke, chulha smoke and dust. Use masks at work where possible.
  • Learn correct inhaler and spacer technique; take medicines regularly.
  • Daily walking at moderate breathlessness, plus home strengthening exercises.
  • Daily airway clearance if you have sputum.
  • Use energy-conservation and pacing methods for housework and stairs.
  • Eat small, frequent meals; keep a healthy weight.
  • Get vaccinated against influenza and pneumonia.
  • Action plan: know the early signs of an exacerbation (more breathless, more or darker sputum) and when to start rescue medicines or see a doctor.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

Prognosis is worse with lower FEV1, frequent exacerbations, low BMI, low exercise capacity and continued smoking. Stopping smoking slows decline; pulmonary rehab improves symptoms and function; long-term oxygen improves survival when resting oxygen is severely low.

Discharge from hospital: stable on home medicines and oxygen if prescribed; independent with breathing control, airway clearance and inhalers; can walk and manage stairs with pacing; referred to pulmonary rehab.

Hyperlucent lungs with reduced peripheral markings; flattened low diaphragms (more than 6 anterior ribs above); horizontal ribs and wide spaces; narrow tubular heart; increased retrosternal air space; bullae.

7 mEnergy conservation techniques for COPD patients and their importance.

Define; the 4 P's (plan, pace, prioritise, position); breathing with activity (exhale on effort); aids; stairs strategy; examples for bathing, dressing, cooking; importance (less oxygen cost, less breathlessness, more independence, fewer exacerbation triggers).

MCQA COPD patient with PaCO₂ 60 mmHg is on oxygen with SpO₂ 98% and is becoming drowsy. The best action is:A. Increase oxygenB. Report and reduce oxygen to target 88–92% as prescribedC. Start a 6-minute walk testD. Head-down postural drainage

B. Too much oxygen in a CO₂-retaining patient can raise CO₂ further and cause drowsiness.

MCQWhich spirometry pattern confirms COPD?A. FEV1/FVC 0.85, FVC 55% predictedB. Post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC 0.60C. FEV1/FVC 0.75 that rises to 0.85 after salbutamolD. Normal FEV1, low DLCO only

B. A post-bronchodilator ratio below 0.70 confirms persistent airflow limitation. A is restrictive; C suggests asthma.

MCQThe main aim of endurance training in pulmonary rehab is to:A. Improve FEV1B. Reverse emphysemaC. Improve limb muscle function so daily tasks cause less breathlessnessD. Replace bronchodilators

C. Lung function does not change much, but trained muscles need less ventilation for the same work.
