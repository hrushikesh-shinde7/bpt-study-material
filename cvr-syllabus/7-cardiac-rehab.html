My revision

1Definition and aims (7a)

Cardiac rehabilitation is a coordinated, multi-part programme that helps people with heart disease recover their physical, psychological and social function, and that slows the underlying disease through exercise, education and risk-factor control (AHA/AACVPR). The WHO describes it as all the activities needed so that patients, by their own efforts, regain as normal a place as possible in the community.

  • Aims: safe return to daily activities, work and leisure; better exercise capacity; control of risk factors; fewer future heart attacks and admissions; better quality of life and mood; self-management for life.

2Indications: who should be referred (7b)

  • Myocardial infarction or acute coronary syndrome.
  • After coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or angioplasty with stent (PCI).
  • Stable angina.
  • Stable heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (and increasingly other heart failure).
  • After heart valve repair or replacement (including transcatheter valves).
  • Heart or heart–lung transplant.
  • Others who benefit: pacemaker or ICD, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease in adults, after cardiac surgery of any kind, peripheral arterial disease (supervised walking programme), people at high risk of coronary disease (diabetes, metabolic syndrome).

Older age, a low ejection fraction in stable heart failure, an ICD and diabetes are not reasons to exclude; they are reasons to stratify risk and supervise more closely.

3Contraindications to exercise in cardiac rehab, ACSM (7b)

Do not start (or stop) exercise training if
  • Unstable angina
  • Resting systolic BP above 180 or diastolic above 110 mmHg (decide case by case)
  • BP falls more than 20 mmHg on standing, with symptoms
  • Significant aortic stenosis
  • Uncontrolled atrial or ventricular arrhythmias; uncontrolled sinus tachycardia above 120
  • Uncompensated (decompensated) heart failure
  • Third-degree heart block without a pacemaker
  • Active pericarditis or myocarditis
  • Recent embolism (pulmonary or systemic); acute thrombophlebitis; aortic dissection
  • Acute illness or fever
  • Uncontrolled diabetes; other uncorrected metabolic problems (for example abnormal potassium)
  • Severe orthopaedic or psychological problems that prevent exercise

Stop criteria during sessions are on the IHD page and the exercise testing page.

6Exercise across heart conditions

ConditionKey adjustment

After PCINo sternal precautions; protect the radial or femoral puncture site for about a week; can progress faster
Heart failureStart low (RPE 11–13), interval training, longer warm-up and cool-down; watch weight gain and breathlessness (fluid); inspiratory muscle training if weak

Heart transplantThe heart has no nerve supply, so heart rate rises slowly and late: use RPE, long warm-up and cool-down
Atrial fibrillation, beta-blockersHeart rate is unreliable: use RPE and the talk test
Peripheral arterial diseaseSupervised walking to moderate claudication pain, rest, repeat (see the PAD page)

7Risk stratification

Before Phase II, patients are grouped as low, moderate or high risk for events during exercise (AACVPR), using ejection fraction, exercise-test results (functional capacity in METs, ischaemia, arrhythmias), symptoms, complications in hospital and depression. Risk sets the amount of ECG monitoring and supervision and how fast to progress. The full table (asked as 7 marks in S21) is on the IHD page.

Memory hook

EF 50 / 40, METs 7 / 5

Low risk: EF 50% or more and capacity 7 METs or more. High risk: EF below 40%, or symptoms below 5 METs, or complex arrhythmias, or a complicated course.

9Evidence and ways of delivering it

  • Benefit: compared with no programme, exercise-based cardiac rehab reduces heart attacks by about 28% and hospital admissions by about 35%, probably reduces deaths, and improves quality of life (Cochrane review update, 2026: 107 trials, 26,886 people).
  • Settings: home-based and digitally supported programmes are about as effective as centre-based ones, and suit patients who live far away or work.
  • Yoga-based rehab: the Indian Yoga-CaRe trial improved self-rated health and return to activities, but did not significantly reduce major events (see the yoga page).
  • The gap: few eligible patients join, especially in India and among women. Physiotherapists can refer at discharge, start Phase I early and offer home programmes.

Test yourself

3 mList six indications for cardiac rehabilitation.

MI or acute coronary syndrome; after CABG; after PCI; stable angina; stable heart failure with reduced EF; after valve surgery; heart transplant; pacemaker or ICD (any six).

3 mName three outcome measures used in cardiac rehabilitation.

6-minute walk test or exercise test (METs); MacNew or Seattle Angina Questionnaire; HADS; risk factors such as BP, lipids and HbA1c (any three).

MCQWhich is a contraindication to starting exercise training?A. Age 75B. Stable heart failure with EF 35%C. Unstable anginaD. An ICD

C. The others need risk stratification and closer supervision, not exclusion.
