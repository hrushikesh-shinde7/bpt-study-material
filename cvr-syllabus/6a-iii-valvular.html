6(a)(iii)Valvular heart disease

Valve disease was asked as cardiac rehabilitation after valve replacement (W21, 7 marks). Learn the four common valve lesions with their murmurs, why rheumatic heart disease still matters in India, which lesion makes exercise unsafe, and Phases 1 and 2 after valve surgery.

My revision

1Definitions

  • Valvular heart disease: a heart valve that does not open fully (stenosis) or does not close fully (regurgitation).
  • Rheumatic heart disease (RHD): permanent valve damage after rheumatic fever, which follows untreated streptococcal throat infection. Still common in India; the mitral valve is most often affected.
  • Valve replacement: a damaged valve replaced by a mechanical valve (lifelong warfarin) or a tissue valve, by open surgery through a sternotomy or through a catheter (TAVI).

Valve disease is one kind of acquired heart disease. It often ends in heart failure, so read it with 6(a)(v), which covers heart failure, cardiomyopathy and cardiac devices.

2Valve lesions and their murmurs

LesionMurmurExercise point
Mitral stenosis (commonest in RHD)Low rumbling mid-diastolic murmur at the apexFast heart rates shorten filling time: breathlessness, pulmonary oedema; atrial fibrillation common
Mitral regurgitationPansystolic murmur at the apex, spreading to the axillaFatigue, breathlessness; AF

Aortic regurgitationEarly diastolic murmur at the left sternal edgeUsually tolerates moderate exercise until the ventricle fails
Memory rule for murmurs

Open valves stenose, closed valves leak

A murmur happens when a valve that should be open is narrow (stenosis), or a valve that should be shut leaks (regurgitation). In systole the aortic valve is open and the mitral valve shut, so systolic murmurs are aortic stenosis or mitral regurgitation; in diastole it is the reverse.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Rheumatic fever (poverty, crowding, untreated sore throats): the commonest cause in India; the mitral valve is affected first.
  • Age-related calcification: aortic stenosis in older people.
  • Congenital: a bicuspid aortic valve.
  • Infective endocarditis destroying a valve.
  • Heart attack damaging the mitral valve's support, causing mitral regurgitation.

Rheumatic fever (Jones criteria): major features are carditis, migrating polyarthritis, chorea, a ring-shaped rash (erythema marginatum) and nodules under the skin; minor features are joint pain, fever, raised inflammatory markers and a prolonged PR interval.

4Clinical features and investigations

Features

  • Murmurs (table above); palpitations from atrial fibrillation.
  • Breathlessness and fatigue on exertion; when the heart fails, orthopnoea and ankle swelling.
  • Fainting on exertion in aortic stenosis; coughing blood in mitral stenosis.

Investigations

  • Echocardiogram: valve area and gradients, chamber size, EF (the key test).
  • ECG: atrial fibrillation, ventricular hypertrophy.
  • Chest X-ray: large heart, pulmonary congestion; mitral stenosis shows a large left atrium.
  • Exercise test: safe exercise range (not in symptomatic severe aortic stenosis).
  • INR for patients on warfarin (mechanical valves, atrial fibrillation).

5Medical and surgical management

  • Rheumatic heart disease: regular benzathine penicillin injections to prevent recurrence; balloon mitral valvotomy; valve repair or replacement.
  • Valve replacement: mechanical (lifelong warfarin) or tissue valve; open surgery through a median sternotomy, or a catheter valve (TAVI) for aortic stenosis.

6Complications

  • Heart failure and acute pulmonary oedema; atrial fibrillation and stroke; fainting and sudden death in severe aortic stenosis.
  • Infective endocarditis, valve thrombosis, bleeding on warfarin.
  • After valve surgery: atrial fibrillation is common, and heart block can follow aortic valve surgery (pacing wires, sometimes a permanent pacemaker).

7Cardiac rehabilitation after valve replacement (7 marks in W21)

Valve surgery is usually through a median sternotomy, so Phase I follows the CABG plan (sternal precautions, breathing exercises, early walking). Add these valve-specific points:

PhasePhysiotherapy
Phase 1 (in hospital, about 5–7 days)
  • Before surgery: education, breathing exercises, supported cough, baseline walking
  • Day 0–1: deep breathing with a hold, supported huff and cough, positioning, ankle pumps, sitting out of bed
  • Day 2 to discharge: walking 3–4 times a day from the room to the corridor, stairs before discharge; RPE below 13, HR no more than rest + 30
  • Monitor rhythm: atrial fibrillation is common, and heart block can follow aortic valve surgery (pacing wires, sometimes a permanent pacemaker)
  • Sternal precautions; posture and gentle shoulder movement
Phase 2 (outpatient, about 8–12 weeks)
  • Exercise test or 6MWT first; many patients were inactive for months before surgery, so start low
  • Aerobic training 3–5 days a week, building to 30–60 minutes at RPE 11–14; use RPE and the talk test when atrial fibrillation or beta-blockers make heart rate unreliable
  • Leg resistance work early; arm resistance once the sternum has healed (about 6–8 weeks), progressing slowly
  • Education: anticoagulation, infection prevention, symptoms to report, return to work and driving
  • Mechanical valve: lifelong warfarin with regular INR checks. Bruising and bleeding are easy, so handle gently, prevent falls, avoid contact sports and vigorous percussion, and report any bleeding.
  • Infective endocarditis: keep teeth and skin healthy; report fever, night sweats or new breathlessness early.
  • Mitral valve disease often leaves pulmonary hypertension and atrial fibrillation: progress more slowly.
  • Transcatheter valve (TAVI): no sternotomy, so no sternal precautions; older, frailer patients; groin-puncture care; walk within a day and follow the same Phase 2 plan.

8Exercise and precautions in valve disease

  • Moderate exercise is safe in mild to moderate lesions.
  • No exercise training in symptomatic severe aortic stenosis until it is treated: risk of fainting and sudden death.
  • Mitral stenosis: fast heart rates shorten filling time, causing breathlessness. Mitral disease often leaves pulmonary hypertension and atrial fibrillation, so progress more slowly.
  • After valve surgery through a sternotomy, follow the CABG sternal precautions.
  • Use RPE and the talk test when atrial fibrillation or beta-blockers make heart rate unreliable.
  • Warfarin: bruising and bleeding are easy, so handle gently, prevent falls, avoid vigorous manual techniques and contact sports, and report any bleeding.
Do not exercise if
  • Symptomatic severe aortic stenosis, uncontrolled arrhythmia, chest pain at rest
  • Signs of decompensated heart failure: breathless at rest, new orthopnoea, rapid weight gain (for example 2 kg in 3 days), increasing oedema

9Education

  • Rheumatic heart disease: never miss penicillin injections; keep teeth healthy.
  • Mechanical valve: take warfarin every day and keep regular INR checks.
  • Infective endocarditis: keep teeth and skin healthy; report fever, night sweats or new breathlessness early.
  • Keep active every day within your plan.

Test yourself

3 mWhich valve lesions cause a systolic murmur, and which a diastolic murmur?

Systolic: aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation, because in systole the aortic valve should be open and the mitral valve shut. Diastolic: mitral stenosis and aortic regurgitation.

MCQWhich lesion makes exercise training unsafe until it is treated?A. Mild mitral regurgitationB. Symptomatic severe aortic stenosisC. Mild aortic regurgitationD. A well-functioning tissue valve

B. The narrow valve stops output rising with exercise: fainting and sudden death.
