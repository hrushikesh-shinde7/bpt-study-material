1(a)Pulmonary anatomy and physiology

Chapter 1 is revision of earlier years, applied to physiotherapy. It is rarely asked on its own, but every technique answer rests on it: why a breath hold reopens alveoli, why huffing clears small airways, why the oxygen curve matters in a desaturating patient. Learn the numbers and the drawings that turn up again and again.

My revision

1Airways, lobes and segments

  • Upper airway (nose, pharynx, larynx) warms, humidifies and filters air. The trachea divides at the carina, at the level of the sternal angle (about T4–T5).
  • The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, so inhaled objects and aspirated fluid usually go right.
  • About 23 generations of branching: generations 0–16 are the conducting zone (no gas exchange; anatomical dead space about 150 mL); 17–23 are the respiratory zone (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and sacs), with hundreds of millions of alveoli.
  • Alveolar cells: type I (thin, for gas exchange) and type II (make surfactant, which lowers surface tension and stops small alveoli collapsing); alveolar macrophages clear particles.
  • Mucociliary escalator: cilia sweep a thin mucus layer upwards to the throat; slowed by smoking, dry gas and infection.
  • Lobes: right lung has 3 (upper, middle, lower; oblique and horizontal fissures), left has 2 (upper with the lingula, and lower; oblique fissure).

Surface markings (for hand placement and auscultation)

  • Oblique fissure: from about the T3 spinous process at the back, round the side, to the 6th costal cartilage at the front. So the lower lobes are mostly at the back, the upper lobes mostly at the front.
  • Horizontal fissure (right): along the 4th costal cartilage; the middle lobe lies between it and the oblique fissure, at the front.
  • Lower lung border in quiet breathing: 6th rib in the mid-clavicular line, 8th in the mid-axillary line, 10th at the back. The pleura reaches about two ribs lower (8th, 10th, 12th).

Nerves: vagus (bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion); sympathetic β2 (bronchodilation, the target of salbutamol); phrenic nerve C3–C5 to the diaphragm; intercostal nerves to the intercostals. Blood supply: the low-pressure pulmonary circulation (pulmonary artery pressure about 25/10 mmHg) for gas exchange, and bronchial arteries for the airway walls.

2Lung volumes and capacities

TermDefinitionAbout
Tidal volume (TV)Breathed in or out in a quiet breath0.5 L
Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)Extra air that can be breathed in after a quiet breath in3.0 L
Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)Extra air that can be breathed out after a quiet breath out1.1 L
Residual volume (RV)Air left after a maximal breath out1.2 L
Inspiratory capacity (IC)TV + IRV3.5 L
Functional residual capacity (FRC)ERV + RV: air left at the end of a quiet breath out2.3 L
Vital capacity (VC)IRV + TV + ERV4.6 L
Total lung capacity (TLC)All four volumes5.8 L

FRC matters most to physiotherapists: it falls after surgery, with pain, obesity and lying flat, and small airways at the bases then close (collapse). Upright positions, deep breaths and walking restore it. Minute ventilation = tidal volume × rate (about 6 L/min); alveolar ventilation subtracts the dead space.

3Gas exchange and transport

  • Oxygen and CO₂ cross the very thin alveolar–capillary membrane by diffusion, which slows if the membrane thickens (fibrosis, oedema) or its area falls (emphysema, lung resection).
  • Oxygen is carried about 98.5% bound to haemoglobin and 1.5% dissolved; 1 g of haemoglobin carries about 1.34 mL of oxygen, so anaemia lowers oxygen delivery even with a normal SpO₂.
  • CO₂ is carried as bicarbonate (about 70%), bound to proteins as carbamino compounds (about 23%) and dissolved (about 7%).
  • Bohr effect: CO₂ and acid make haemoglobin release oxygen in the tissues. Haldane effect: oxygen binding makes haemoglobin release CO₂ in the lungs (one reason high oxygen raises CO₂ in COPD).
