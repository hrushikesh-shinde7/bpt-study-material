6(h)Diabetic and vascular ulcers

Ulcers were asked as diabetic foot care (S19), pressure sores (W19), wound-healing stages and factors (S19, S25) and wound-healing modalities (S16, S22, S24). Learn the three kinds of leg ulcer, why the diabetic foot breaks down, and how physiotherapy helps a wound heal. Venous and arterial ulcers are treated with their vessels in 6(a)(vii) and 6(a)(viii).

1Ulcers in this syllabus item

An ulcer is an open break in the skin that is slow to heal. Syllabus 6(h) covers two groups:

  • Diabetic (neuropathic) foot ulcers: on this page.
  • Vascular ulcers: venous ulcers, whose full care is with varicose veins in 6(a)(vii), and arterial (ischaemic) ulcers, with peripheral arterial disease in 6(a)(viii).

Pressure sores (W19) and wound healing (S19, S25) are also on this page, because they apply to every ulcer. Amputation, the other half of 6(h), has its own page next.

2Diabetic foot: Wagner grades and risk categories

Diabetic foot ulcer: Wagner grades

GradeDescription
0At-risk foot, no open wound (callus, deformity)
1Superficial ulcer
2Deep ulcer reaching tendon, capsule or bone, no abscess
3Deep ulcer with abscess or osteomyelitis
4Gangrene of part of the foot (toes or forefoot)
5Gangrene of the whole foot

Foot risk categories (IWGDF)

  • 0, very low: no loss of protective sensation and no PAD. Screen yearly.
  • 1, low: loss of protective sensation or PAD. Screen every 6–12 months.
  • 2, moderate: two of: loss of sensation, PAD, foot deformity. Screen every 3–6 months.
  • 3, high: loss of sensation or PAD plus a past ulcer, past amputation or end-stage kidney disease. Screen every 1–3 months.

3Why the diabetic foot ulcerates

Why the diabetic foot ulcerates: three causes

  • Sensory neuropathy: loss of protective feeling, so injuries, tight shoes and burns are not noticed.
  • Motor neuropathy: weak small foot muscles cause claw toes and prominent metatarsal heads, so pressure under the forefoot rises and callus forms.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: no sweating, so dry, cracked skin lets bacteria in.
  • Ischaemia (PAD) slows healing; high sugar weakens immunity, so infection spreads fast.

Repeated pressure under a callus causes bleeding and breakdown beneath it: a neuropathic ulcer. Add infection and ischaemia, and the result can be gangrene and amputation.

5Wound healing: stages and factors

Stages of wound healing (3 marks in S19)

Haemostasis (minutes to hours) → inflammation (about days 1–5) → proliferation (about day 4 to week 3) → remodelling (week 3 to 1–2 years). See the full table on the burns page. Diabetes slows every stage, especially inflammation and proliferation.

Factors affecting wound healing (7 marks in S25)

  • Local: infection; poor blood supply (arterial disease, pressure, tight dressings); oedema; dead tissue or foreign bodies; repeated shear, friction or pressure; a wound that is too dry or too wet; a large or deep wound; previous radiotherapy.
  • General: older age; poor nutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc); diabetes with high sugar; smoking; anaemia and low oxygen; obesity; drugs (steroids, chemotherapy); kidney or liver failure; reduced immunity; stress and poor sleep.

In a diabetic foot the main ones are high sugar, ischaemia, unnoticed pressure from neuropathy, infection and smoking. Answer S25 with this list plus the modalities in the electrotherapy section below.

6Diabetic foot care (7 marks in S19)

Diabetic foot care (7 marks in S19: "what is diabetic foot, how to take care of it, and why")

Seek help earlyAny blister, cut, redness or swellingSmall wounds become ulcers in days
  • With an active plantar ulcer: off-load the foot (cast, boot, crutches); switch to non-weight-bearing exercise such as cycling with the other leg, arm ergometry or swimming once the wound allows.
  • Neuropathy without ulcer: moderate weight-bearing walking is safe with proper footwear and daily checks.

7Electrotherapy for wound healing

Electrotherapy for wound healing (3 marks in S16 and S22; 7 marks in S24 and S25)

Typical textbook parameters (vary between sources; follow local protocol)
ModalityTypical parametersEffect
High-voltage pulsed current (electrical stimulation)Twin-peak monophasic pulses, about 100 pulses per second, sensory-level voltage (often 100–150 V), 45–60 minutes, daily or 5 days a week; negative pole on an infected wound, positive to promote healingAttracts repair cells, improves blood flow, antibacterial effect
Therapeutic ultrasoundPulsed 20%, about 0.5 W/cm², 3 MHz, 5–10 minutes on the skin around the wound (not into an open wound without a sterile coupling)Stimulates inflammation to move on, collagen formation
Ultraviolet (UVC)Short-wave UV (about 254 nm), short exposures by test dose, protecting surrounding skin and eyesKills bacteria in infected wounds
Low-level laserLow dose, about 1–4 J/cm² to wound edgesCell stimulation, healing

Exact parameters differ between electrotherapy books and trials. Write a range with the effect, and never use heat or electrotherapy over an ischaemic or numb area without checking circulation.

8Pressure sores (pressure injuries)

Pressure sores (pressure injuries) (7 marks in W19)

Damage to skin and the tissue under it from pressure, or pressure with shear, usually over a bony point: sacrum, heels, hips (greater trochanter), ischial tuberosities, back of the head, elbows. Patients at risk: bed- or chair-bound, numb skin (diabetes, spinal cord injury), ICU patients, poor nutrition, incontinence, older age.

Stages (NPIAP, 2016)
StageWhat you see
Stage 1Intact skin with redness that does not fade (blanch) when pressed
Stage 2Partial-thickness skin loss: shallow pink wound or blister; fat not visible
Stage 3Full-thickness skin loss: fat visible; no bone, tendon or muscle seen
Stage 4Full-thickness skin and tissue loss: muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage or bone exposed or felt
UnstageableBase covered by slough or eschar, so depth cannot be seen
Deep tissue pressure injuryIntact or broken skin with a persistent deep red, maroon or purple area

Risk assessment: Braden scale (sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, friction and shear; total 6–23). A score of 18 or less means the patient is at risk; the lower the score, the higher the risk.

Physiotherapy

  • Relieve pressure: reposition on a schedule set for that patient; 30° tilted side-lying rather than lying straight on the hip; head of bed no higher than 30° unless breathing needs more; heels floated off the bed with a pillow under the calves.
  • Surfaces: high-specification foam or alternating-pressure air mattress; pressure-relieving cushion in the chair; teach chair users to shift weight (push-ups or leaning) regularly.
  • Move: bed mobility, sitting out and walking as early as possible; active and passive exercise to keep circulation and joint range.
  • Avoid shear: lift, do not drag; use slide sheets.
  • Help the wound heal: electrical stimulation has the best evidence of the physical modalities for stage 2 sores that are slow to heal and for stage 3–4 sores (parameters as in the table above); wound cleaning and dressing by the team; nutrition and continence care.
  • Teach: daily skin checks, weight shifts, never sit or lie on a red area.

Classic answer vs current guideline: turning

Classic

Turn every patient every 2 hours.

Current (international guideline, 2019)

Set the turning frequency for each patient from their risk, skin checks, comfort and the mattress they are on.

Write in the exam

"Reposition at least every 2 hours, or as the patient's skin and support surface require."

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mThree electrotherapeutic modalities with parameters for wound healing.
  1. High-voltage pulsed current: about 100 pps, sensory level, 45–60 min, negative pole for infected wound.
  2. Pulsed ultrasound: 20%, about 0.5 W/cm², 3 MHz, 5–10 min around the wound.
  3. UVC about 254 nm, short test-dosed exposure for bacterial kill. (Laser 1–4 J/cm² also accepted.)
MCQWhich footwear advice is correct for a diabetic with numb feet?A. Walk barefoot at home to strengthen the feetB. Tight shoes for supportC. Soft, roomy closed shoes with socks, checked inside before wearingD. Hot-water soaks every night

C. Numb feet cannot sense injury; protection and daily checks prevent ulcers.

MCQA patient with a leg ulcer has ABPI 0.45. What is correct?A. Apply full 40 mmHg compressionB. Avoid compression and refer to a vascular surgeonC. Elevate the leg high all nightD. Apply a hot pack

B. Severe arterial disease: compression could cut off the blood supply, and elevation worsens arterial pain.

MCQA shallow, moist ulcer above the inner ankle with brown pigmentation and good pulses is most likely:A. ArterialB. VenousC. NeuropathicD. Pressure ulcer

B. The gaiter-area site, pigmentation and present pulses are typical of a venous ulcer.
