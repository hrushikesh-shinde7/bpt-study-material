6(a)(v)Acquired heart disease: heart failure, cardiomyopathy, pacemakers and ICDs

My revision

1Definitions

Acquired heart disease develops after birth, unlike congenital heart disease. It includes coronary disease (6(a)(ii)), valve disease (6(a)(iii)), heart failure and cardiomyopathy. This page covers heart failure, cardiomyopathy and the devices used in them.

  • Heart failure: a condition in which the heart cannot pump enough blood for the body's needs, or can do so only at high filling pressures, causing breathlessness, fatigue and fluid retention.
  • Cardiomyopathy: disease of the heart muscle itself (dilated, hypertrophic or restrictive).
  • Pacemaker: a device that sends electrical impulses to keep the heart rate from going too low. ICD (implantable cardioverter-defibrillator): a device that detects dangerous fast rhythms and treats them with pacing or a shock.

2Classification: ejection fraction and NYHA classes (3 marks in S21)

Heart failure by ejection fraction (universal definition 2021)

  • Reduced EF: 40% or less.
  • Mildly reduced EF: 41–49%.
  • Preserved EF: 50% or more (stiff ventricle that fills poorly).

NYHA functional classes (3 marks in S21)

ClassSymptoms
INo limitation; ordinary activity causes no undue breathlessness, fatigue or palpitations
IISlight limitation; comfortable at rest, ordinary activity causes symptoms
IIIMarked limitation; comfortable at rest, less than ordinary activity causes symptoms
IVSymptoms at rest; any activity increases discomfort

3Causes and risk factors

  • Heart failure: coronary disease and MI, hypertension, valve disease, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, diabetes, alcohol, some chemotherapy.
  • Cardiomyopathy: genetic, viral myocarditis, alcohol, pregnancy (peripartum), unknown.
  • Pacemaker: slow heart rhythms (heart block, sick sinus syndrome). ICD: survivors of cardiac arrest, or people at high risk (for example EF 35% or less with heart failure symptoms despite treatment).

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. The weak or overloaded heart cannot raise its output with exercise, so muscles get less oxygen: fatigue and low exercise tolerance.
  2. Back-pressure into the lungs causes breathlessness, worse lying flat (orthopnoea) and at night; back-pressure into the body causes leg oedema and an enlarged liver.
  3. The body retains salt and water to compensate, which worsens congestion.
  4. Skeletal muscles weaken and change (fewer mitochondria), so even daily tasks become hard: the main target of exercise training.
  5. Arrhythmias (AF, ventricular tachycardia) cause palpitations, stroke risk and sudden death.

5Clinical features

  • Breathlessness on exertion, orthopnoea, waking breathless at night; fatigue; ankle swelling; weight gain from fluid.
  • Fast pulse, low BP in advanced disease, raised neck veins, basal crackles, third heart sound.
  • Palpitations from arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia); fainting.

6Investigations

  • Echocardiogram: EF and chamber size (the key test).
  • ECG: rhythm (AF), conduction blocks, ventricular hypertrophy; Holter monitoring.
  • Chest X-ray: large heart, pulmonary congestion, effusions.
  • BNP or NT-proBNP: raised in heart failure.
  • Exercise test or CPET: peak oxygen uptake and a safe exercise range.

7Medical and device management

  • Heart failure with reduced EF: four main drug groups (ACE inhibitor/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, mineralocorticoid antagonist, SGLT2 inhibitor), diuretics for congestion; devices (ICD, cardiac resynchronisation therapy); exercise training.
  • Pacemaker or ICD: implanted under the skin below the collarbone (usually left), with leads passed through a vein into the heart.

8Complications

  • Acute pulmonary oedema, arrhythmias, stroke (AF), sudden death, kidney impairment.
  • Device: lead displacement (early, from arm movement), pocket haematoma or infection, pneumothorax at insertion, inappropriate ICD shocks, anxiety after shocks.

9PT assessment

  • Diagnosis, EF, NYHA class, medicines, device type and settings (pacing mode, rate-response, ICD detection rate), recent shocks.
  • Symptoms at rest and on exertion; daily weight trend; oedema; orthopnoea.
  • HR and rhythm, BP, SpO₂, RR at rest and with activity; auscultation for crackles.
  • Wound and shoulder range on the device side.
  • Exercise capacity (6MWT with Borg), muscle strength, function.
  • Outcome measures: 6MWT, NYHA class, Minnesota Living with Heart Failure or Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire, HADS.

11Goals

  • Short-term (first 4–6 weeks): wound healed; no lead displacement; shoulder kept mobile below shoulder height; walking daily; knows device precautions and shock plan.
  • Long-term (3 months): full shoulder range; better 6MWT and NYHA class; regular exercise below the ICD detection rate; back to teaching.

12PT management

Pre-implantation

  • Assessment as in section 9; baseline shoulder range and 6MWT if stable.
  • Education: what the device does, arm precautions after implantation, what to do if the ICD shocks.
  • Breathing exercises and early mobilisation plan; reduce anxiety.

Post-implantation

  • First 4–6 weeks: no lifting of the device-side arm above shoulder height and no heavy lifting with that arm, so the leads can fix in place. Gentle active movement of the shoulder below shoulder height, elbow, wrist and hand from day 1 to prevent a stiff shoulder.
  • Early walking from the day after implantation; breathing exercises.
  • After 4–6 weeks: restore full shoulder range; start structured exercise training.

Exercise training in heart failure and cardiomyopathy

  • Exercise training is recommended for all stable heart failure patients who can exercise (ESC 2021, Class I): it improves exercise capacity and quality of life and reduces hospital admissions.
  • Aerobic: walking or cycling, 3–5 days a week, building to 20–60 minutes; start low (RPE 11–13), progress slowly; interval training suits low-fitness patients.
  • Resistance: light loads, 2–3 days a week, 10–15 repetitions, breathe out on effort.
  • Inspiratory muscle training for weak breathing muscles.
  • With an ICD: keep training heart rate at least 10 beats below the ICD's programmed detection rate (ask the device clinic for the setting).
  • With a pacemaker: if the heart rate cannot rise (chronotropic incompetence) or the device is fixed-rate, use RPE and BP to guide intensity.
  • Valve disease: moderate exercise in mild to moderate lesions; no exercise training in symptomatic severe aortic stenosis until it is treated; after valve surgery follow the CABG sternotomy plan.

If the ICD shocks

  • Stop, sit or lie down.
  • One shock and feeling well: inform the device clinic.
  • More than one shock, or feeling unwell (chest pain, breathlessness, fainting): call emergency services.

13Precautions and contraindications

Devices
  • Avoid shortwave and microwave diathermy in patients with pacemakers or ICDs.
  • Avoid TENS, interferential and other electrical stimulation near the device or across the chest; use only after device-team advice.
  • Do not apply therapeutic ultrasound directly over the device.
  • Keep strong magnets away from the device.
Do not exercise if
  • Symptoms of decompensated heart failure: breathless at rest, new orthopnoea, rapid weight gain (for example 2 kg in 3 days), increasing oedema
  • Symptomatic severe aortic stenosis, uncontrolled arrhythmia, chest pain at rest
  • Recent ICD shocks until reviewed
  • Warfarin: gentle handling; avoid vigorous manual techniques.
  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: avoid dehydration and very heavy straining; exercise plans are individual.

14Education

  • Weigh yourself every morning; report a sudden gain.
  • Follow salt and fluid advice; take medicines regularly.
  • Keep active every day within your plan.
  • Rheumatic heart disease: never miss penicillin injections; keep teeth healthy.
  • Device: carry the device card; keep mobile phones away from the device pocket; tell staff before any procedure; follow driving rules after ICD implantation.

15Prognosis

Modern drugs and devices have greatly improved survival in heart failure. Exercise training improves symptoms and quality of life. Valve disease has a good outlook when treated before the heart muscle is damaged.

Test yourself

3 mGrades of NYHA scale.

I no limitation; II slight limitation, ordinary activity causes symptoms; III marked limitation, less than ordinary activity causes symptoms; IV symptoms at rest.

MCQTwo days after a left-sided pacemaker, which is correct?A. Full overhead pulley exercises for the left shoulderB. Gentle left shoulder movement below shoulder height; no overhead lifting for 4–6 weeksC. Complete immobilisation of the left arm for 3 monthsD. Shortwave diathermy for shoulder pain

B. Early gentle movement prevents stiffness while protecting the leads; diathermy is contraindicated.

MCQAn ICD is set to detect ventricular tachycardia at 170 bpm. The training heart rate should stay below about:A. 180B. 160C. 175D. 200

B. At least 10 beats below the detection rate avoids inappropriate shocks.
