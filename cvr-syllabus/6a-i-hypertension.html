6(a)(i)Hypertension

Hypertension was asked on its own as a 7-mark question (S18) and as a long case (S23: a teacher admitted with chest pain and hypertension), and it appears in many other long cases as a risk factor. Know the classification, the exercise dose, the lifestyle changes with their effect, and when not to exercise.

My revision

1Definition

Hypertension is persistently raised arterial blood pressure, measured correctly on two or more occasions. Most guidelines use 140/90 mmHg or more (office reading) as hypertension; the ACC/AHA uses 130/80 or more.

2Classification

ACC/AHA categories (2017, kept in 2025)
CategorySystolicDiastolic
NormalBelow 120and below 80
Elevated120–129and below 80
Stage 1 hypertension130–139or 80–89
Stage 2 hypertension140 or moreor 90 or more
Hypertensive crisisAbove 180and/or above 120 (emergency if organ damage)
  • ESC 2024: non-elevated below 120/70; elevated BP 120–139/70–89; hypertension 140/90 or more.
  • ISH 2020 and Indian guidelines: hypertension 140/90 or more; grade 1 140–159/90–99; grade 2 160/100 or more.
  • Primary (essential): about 90% or more, no single cause. Secondary: kidney disease, hormonal causes, sleep apnoea, drugs.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Age, family history, obesity (especially abdominal), high salt intake (pickles, papad, packaged snacks), low potassium intake, physical inactivity, alcohol, stress, smoking, diabetes, sleep apnoea.
  • Secondary causes: chronic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, hyperaldosteronism, thyroid disease, coarctation of the aorta, pregnancy, steroids and NSAIDs.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  • BP = cardiac output × peripheral resistance. Most hypertension comes from stiff, narrowed small arteries (raised resistance), with salt and water retention and nervous system overactivity.
  • Long-term high pressure damages target organs: heart (left ventricular hypertrophy, heart failure, coronary disease), brain (stroke, dementia), kidneys, eyes (retinopathy) and arteries (PAD, aneurysm).
  • Physio-relevant impairments: reduced exercise tolerance, risk during straining, drug side effects.
Why does regular exercise lower BP?

Training improves the function of the artery lining, relaxes small arteries, lowers sympathetic nervous activity and helps weight loss, so resting resistance falls. A single session also lowers BP for several hours afterwards (post-exercise hypotension).

5Clinical features

Usually no symptoms (the "silent killer"). Sometimes headache, dizziness or nosebleeds. Very high BP can cause severe headache, blurred vision, chest pain, breathlessness or confusion (an emergency).

6Investigations

  • Correct BP measurement: seated, back supported, arm at heart level, correct cuff size, rested 5 minutes, two readings; home or 24-hour readings to confirm.
  • Blood and urine tests for kidney function, sugar, lipids; ECG for left ventricular hypertrophy; echo; eye examination.

7Medical management

  • Lifestyle change for everyone (section 14).
  • Drugs: ACE inhibitors or ARBs, calcium channel blockers, thiazide-type diuretics; beta-blockers for specific indications. Often two drugs in one tablet.
  • Targets: below 130/80 for most adults (ACC/AHA 2025); ESC 2024 aims for a systolic of 120–129 if tolerated.

8Complications

Stroke, heart attack, heart failure, kidney failure, retinopathy, aortic aneurysm and dissection, PAD, vascular dementia. Good BP control lowers all of these.

9PT assessment

  • BP both arms at rest (correct cuff), HR, medicines and timing, target-organ disease (heart, kidney, eyes), other risk factors.
  • Activity level, diet and salt intake, alcohol, smoking, stress, sleep.
  • BMI, waist; fitness (6MWT or step test) with BP before and after.
  • Outcome measures: resting BP (home readings), weight and waist, 6MWT, activity minutes a week.

10PT diagnosis by ICF

Real paper (S23, 15 marks): schoolteacher, 50, chest pain and hypertension, treated conservatively

Functional diagnosis: a 50-year-old schoolteacher with hypertension and chest pain of probable cardiac origin (angina), managed with medicines, with reduced exercise tolerance and fear of exertion, limiting walking, stairs and standing to teach, and restricting her work and family roles.

  • Before treating: confirm the cause of the chest pain with the doctor (ECG, troponin); resting BP below 180/110; no chest pain at rest.
  • Plan: treat it like stable angina with hypertension: education on BP and angina, GTN use and warning signs; graded walking at RPE 11–13 (moderate), building to 30 minutes on most days, kept below the heart rate that brings on angina; light resistance training with breathing out on effort; relaxation and stress management; a low-salt diet rich in fruit and vegetables, and weight control; regular medicines and home BP checks. The angina sections of the IHD page give the doses and stop criteria.
  • Participation: pacing at school (sitting for part of the class, planning stair use), return to full duties as symptoms settle.

Practice case (not from a paper): a 52-year-old bank clerk with stage 2 hypertension, BMI 28, sedentary, high salt intake and work stress. Impairments: raised BP, low fitness, excess weight. Activity: limited brisk walking and stair climbing. Participation: at risk of losing work through stroke or heart disease. Environment: desk job and fried canteen food (barriers); family and a nearby park (facilitators). Personal: stress, low awareness.

11Goals

  • Short-term (4 weeks): correct home BP measurement; walking 30 minutes on most days; salt reduced.
  • Long-term (3–6 months): BP at target; 5% or more weight loss; regular aerobic, resistance and isometric training; stress management habits.

12PT management (7 marks in S18)

Exercise prescription for hypertension
TypeDose
AerobicBrisk walking, cycling, swimming; moderate intensity (talk test, RPE 12–13); about 30 minutes on most days. ACC/AHA 2025: 75–150 min a week of aerobic and/or resistance exercise.
Dynamic resistance2–3 days a week; major muscle groups; moderate loads (about 50–80% of 1RM), about 3 sets of 10; breathe out on effort
IsometricHandgrip 4 × 2 minutes at 30–40% of maximum grip, 1–3 minutes' rest between, 3 days a week; or wall squats in the same pattern. Gave the largest BP fall in a 2023 analysis of 270 trials (about 8/4 mmHg).
Flexibility and relaxationStretching; yoga with slow breathing; relaxation training for stress
  • Warm-up and a longer cool-down (10 minutes) to avoid a sudden BP drop.
  • Progress duration first, then intensity.
  • Combine with weight loss and diet change for the best effect.

13Precautions and stop criteria

Do not start exercise if
  • Resting BP is above about 180/110: recheck after rest and refer before exercising.
  • There are symptoms of a hypertensive emergency (severe headache, chest pain, blurred vision, confusion).
Stop exercise if
  • Systolic rises above about 250 or diastolic above about 115 mmHg during exercise
  • Systolic BP falls as the workload rises
  • Chest pain, severe breathlessness, dizziness or headache
  • Avoid breath-holding (Valsalva), very heavy lifting and long head-down positions.
  • Isometric holds: keep breathing throughout; avoid in uncontrolled hypertension.

15Prognosis

Treated, well-controlled hypertension greatly reduces stroke and heart disease. Lifestyle change alone can control stage 1 hypertension in many people and reduces the number of drugs needed in others.

7 mPhysiotherapy management of hypertension.

Define and classify; assessment; aims; exercise prescription (aerobic, resistance, isometric with doses); warm-up and cool-down; precautions and stop criteria; drug effects; lifestyle advice table; outcome measures.

MCQResting BP before a session is 196/112. You should:A. Start with light exerciseB. Recheck after rest; do not exercise and refer if it stays above about 180/110C. Do isometric handgripD. Do a maximal exercise test

B. Uncontrolled high BP is a contraindication to starting exercise.

MCQA patient on an alpha-blocker feels dizzy after stopping exercise suddenly. Best prevention:A. Longer, gradual cool-downB. Exercise harderC. Hold the breath when liftingD. Lie head-down after exercise

A. Vasodilating drugs plus post-exercise vasodilation can drop BP; a gradual cool-down prevents it.
