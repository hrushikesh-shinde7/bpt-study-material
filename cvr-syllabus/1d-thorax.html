1(d)Biomechanics of the thorax (revision)

How the ribs, diaphragm and accessory muscles move air, revised for physiotherapy. It explains why a flattened diaphragm fails in COPD, why posture changes breathing, and why some positions make breathing easier.

1Mechanics of breathing and biomechanics of the thorax

How the chest gets bigger on breathing in
MovementRibsDiameter increased
Pump handleUpper ribs (about 1–6): the front ends rise and the sternum moves forwards and upFront-to-back (anteroposterior)
Bucket handleLower ribs (about 7–10): the middle of each rib swings up and outSide-to-side (transverse)
CaliperFloating ribs (11–12): swing outwardsSide-to-side, lower chest
Diaphragm descentDome moves downVertical (the biggest share of quiet breathing)
  • The diaphragm is the main muscle of quiet breathing (often quoted as about three-quarters of the tidal volume). Where it lies against the inside of the lower ribs (the zone of apposition), its contraction, with the abdominal contents as a fulcrum, also lifts and widens the lower ribs.
  • In hyperinflation (COPD) the diaphragm is flat and short, the zone of apposition shrinks, and contraction can pull the lower ribs in (Hoover's sign). This is why forward-lean positions, which push the diaphragm back into a dome, ease breathlessness.
  • Posture: slumped sitting and lying flat reduce lung volume; kyphosis, scoliosis and a stiff chest wall (ankylosing spondylitis) restrict it.
Muscles of breathing
PhaseMuscles
Quiet inspirationDiaphragm; external intercostals and parasternal internal intercostals; scalenes
Forced inspiration (accessory)Sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, upper trapezius, pectoralis major and minor (with arms fixed), serratus anterior, back extensors
Quiet expirationNone: passive elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall
Forced expiration and coughAbdominals (rectus, obliques, transversus), internal intercostals, latissimus dorsi
  • Pressures: pleural pressure is about −5 cmH₂O at rest and about −7.5 cmH₂O at the end of a quiet breath in; alveolar pressure is about −1 cmH₂O during inspiration (air flows in) and +1 cmH₂O during expiration.
  • Compliance (stretchiness: volume change per pressure change): about 200 mL/cmH₂O for the lungs alone and about 110 mL/cmH₂O for lungs and chest wall together. Low in fibrosis, oedema, obesity and chest wall deformity; high lung compliance in emphysema (loss of elastic recoil).
  • Airway resistance sits mainly in the medium-sized bronchi; it rises with bronchospasm, mucus and airway collapse.
  • Work of breathing takes only about 3–5% of the body's energy at rest, but much more in lung disease, so breathing muscles compete with leg muscles for oxygen during exercise.

2Exam corner

Test yourself

3 mDifferentiate the pump-handle and bucket-handle movements of the ribs.

Pump handle: upper ribs, front ends rise and the sternum moves forwards and up, increasing the front-to-back diameter. Bucket handle: lower ribs, the middle of each rib swings up and out, increasing the side-to-side diameter.
