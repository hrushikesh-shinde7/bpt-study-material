3(a)Nutrition (bioenergetics): the energy systems (7 marks in S24)

Muscles run on ATP, but they store only enough for a few seconds. Three systems remake it, overlapping all the time; which one leads depends on how hard and how long the effort is.

SystemFuel and processLeads forExamples
ATP–PC (phosphagen)Creatine phosphate quickly remakes ATP; no oxygen neededMaximal efforts up to about 10 seconds100 m sprint, a jump, a heavy single lift, a fast bowler's run-up and delivery
Anaerobic glycolysis (lactic acid system)Glucose and glycogen broken down without oxygen to lactate; fast but little ATPHard efforts of about 10 seconds to 2 minutes400 m run, 100 m swim, climbing several flights of stairs fast
Aerobic (oxidative)Carbohydrate and fat (some protein) fully broken down with oxygen in mitochondria; slow but large ATP yieldEfforts longer than about 2 minutes, and all restWalking, jogging, cycling, marathon, most daily activities

Fuel mix: fat dominates at rest and low intensity; carbohydrate takes over as intensity rises. The respiratory quotient (CO₂ produced ÷ O₂ used) is about 1.0 for carbohydrate and 0.7 for fat.

3(b)Total energy expenditure: the MET (3 marks in W22, S24, S25)

One metabolic equivalent (MET) is the energy used at rest, sitting quietly: about 3.5 mL of oxygen per kg of body weight per minute (roughly 1 kcal per kg per hour). An activity of 4 METs uses four times the resting energy.

Uses: grading activity intensity (light below 3 METs, moderate 3–5.9, vigorous 6 or more); Phase I cardiac rehab goes up to about 3 METs; a good functional capacity is 7 METs or more; sexual activity is reasonable when 3–5 METs are tolerated.

3(d)Complications of bed rest and immobilisation, and prevention

Why bed rest harms

The body adapts to the load it carries. Lying flat removes two loads at once: gravity along the body (which keeps blood-pressure reflexes and fluid balance trained) and muscle work against gravity (which keeps muscle, bone and fitness). Within days every system starts to lose what it does not use. Older and critically ill patients lose it fastest.

Two classic numbers

3 weeks: fitness down about a quarter

In the Dallas bed rest study (1968), 3 weeks of bed rest cut young men's maximal oxygen uptake by about 27%. In critically ill patients the thigh muscle (rectus femoris) shrank by about 12.5% in the first week (Puthucheary, 2013).

ICU-acquired weakness (W17: complications of bed immobility in ICU)

Generalised, symmetrical limb weakness that develops during critical illness with no other cause. Diagnosed at the bedside with the MRC sum score (6 muscle groups each side, 0–5 each, total 60): below 48 = ICU-acquired weakness. Risk factors: sepsis, multi-organ failure, long ventilation, immobility, high blood sugar, steroids and paralysing drugs. It slows weaning and can affect walking and work for months, so prevention by early mobilisation matters.

Prevention: early mobilisation

  1. Assess safety first (see the ICU safety criteria).
  2. Positioning: turns every 2–4 hours, head up, upright sitting in bed.
  3. Range of motion: passive when sedated, then active-assisted and active.
  4. In-bed exercise: bed cycling, bridging, resisted limb exercise.
  5. Sitting over the edge of the bed, then standing (tilt table if BP falls or the patient is weak).
  6. Transfer to a chair; marching on the spot; walking, with the ventilator if needed.
  7. Breathing: deep breathing, airway clearance, inspiratory muscle training.
  8. Monitor before, during and after: HR, BP, SpO₂, respiratory rate, symptoms.

Classic answer vs current evidence: rest after illness

Classic

Strict bed rest for days or weeks after a heart attack, surgery or serious illness.

Current

Bed rest is a treatment with side effects, used only when needed and for as short a time as possible. Early mobilisation is safe within criteria and shortens delirium and recovery; the amount is matched to the patient.

Write in the exam

"Bed rest affects every system within days; prevention is early, graded mobilisation with positioning, breathing exercises and ROM."

3(f)Principles of exercise prescription

Principles of training: FITT-VP

  • FITT-VP: Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type, Volume, Progression.
  • Principles: overload (work above usual levels), specificity (you improve what you train), progression, individuality, reversibility (gains fade within weeks of stopping), warm-up and cool-down.

Methods of measuring exercise intensity (7 marks in S23)

ACSM intensity categories
Intensity% heart-rate reserve% maximum heart rateRPE (6–20)METs
Light30–39%57–63%9–112.0–2.9
Moderate40–59%64–76%12–133.0–5.9
Vigorous60–89%77–95%14–176.0 or more
  1. Heart-rate reserve (Karvonen) method (W25): target HR = resting HR + intensity × (maximum HR − resting HR). Maximum HR is estimated as 220 − age (or 208 − 0.7 × age, Tanaka). Example: age 60, resting HR 70: maximum 160, reserve 90; 50% = 70 + 45 = 115.
  2. Percentage of maximum heart rate: simple but less precise.
  3. Oxygen uptake: % of VO₂max or VO₂ reserve, from a cardiopulmonary exercise test (the gold standard).
  4. METs: choose activities by their MET value; on a treadmill or cycle, set the workload in METs or watts.
  5. Rating of perceived exertion: Borg 6–20 (moderate 12–13) or CR10 breathlessness (moderate to strong, 3–5 in pulmonary rehab).
  6. Talk test: moderate if the patient can talk but not sing; vigorous if only a few words.
  7. Field-test speed: walking at about 80% of the speed reached on the 6MWT or shuttle test.
  8. Thresholds: ventilatory or lactate threshold; ischaemic threshold (train at least 10 beats below the heart rate where angina or ST change appears).
  9. Strength training: % of one-repetition maximum (1RM) or the number of repetitions possible.

Use RPE and the talk test when heart rate is unreliable: beta-blockers, atrial fibrillation, pacemakers, heart transplant.

Cardio-respiratory changes with ageing and a fitness programme (7 marks in S16)

SystemChanges with age
Heart and vesselsLower maximum heart rate; stiffer arteries, so higher systolic BP; slower heart-rate response; VO₂max falls (faster in inactive people)
Lungs and chest wallLungs lose elastic recoil; chest wall stiffens; residual volume rises and vital capacity falls; small airways close earlier; weaker breathing muscles; slightly lower PaO₂
Muscle and boneLoss of muscle mass and strength (sarcopenia), especially fast fibres; bone loss; stiffer joints; poorer balance

Fitness programme for older adults (WHO 2020): 150–300 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity (or 75–150 minutes vigorous); muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days; varied activity that emphasises balance and strength on 3 or more days a week to prevent falls; start low and progress slowly; longer warm-up and cool-down; check medicines and heart conditions first. Regular exercise slows the loss of fitness, strength and independence with age.

Heart, lungs, legs

Heart: postural hypotension. Lungs: basal collapse and pneumonia. Legs: muscle wasting and DVT. Add skin (pressure sores) or bone (osteoporosis) if asked for more.

Test yourself

3 mDefine a MET and give the METs of two daily activities.

One MET is the resting energy use, about 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min. Eating sitting 1.5 METs; showering 2.0; dressing 2.8; walking at a moderate pace 3.8 (any two).

3 mA 50-year-old with a resting HR of 80 trains at 60% intensity. Find the target HR by Karvonen.

Maximum HR = 220 − 50 = 170. Reserve = 170 − 80 = 90. Target = 80 + 0.6 × 90 = 80 + 54 = 134 beats per minute.

MCQA fast bowler's single delivery mainly uses:A. Aerobic systemB. ATP–PC systemC. Fat oxidationD. Lactic acid system only

B. A maximal effort of a few seconds runs on stored ATP and creatine phosphate.

3 mWhy does a patient feel faint when first sat up after 10 days in bed?

Plasma volume has fallen (fluid shifts to the chest when lying flat and is passed as urine), the heart is less filled, and blood-pressure reflexes are out of practice. On sitting, blood pools in the legs and BP falls: postural hypotension. Sit up in stages, use ankle pumps and a tilt table.

MCQThe MRC sum score that defines ICU-acquired weakness is:A. Below 60B. Below 48C. Below 30D. Below 12

B. Below 48 out of 60.
