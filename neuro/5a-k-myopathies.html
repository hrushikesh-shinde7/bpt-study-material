5A(k)Myopathies and myasthenia gravis: features, crises and physiotherapy

Myasthenia gravis came up twice: physiotherapy for 7 marks (W19) and three features for 3 marks (W24). Its hallmark is weakness that grows with use and recovers with rest, so physiotherapy is timed to the medicine, paced with rests and built up gradually; the other muscle diseases on this page follow the same rule of moderate exercise without exhaustion.

My revision

1Myasthenia gravis

Definition and types

  • Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction: antibodies block and destroy acetylcholine (ACh) receptors on the muscle, so muscles weaken with repeated use and recover with rest.
  • By antibody: ACh receptor antibodies in about 85% of generalised MG (about half of purely ocular MG); MuSK antibodies in some of the rest; a few have none detectable.
  • By extent: ocular MG (eyes only) or generalised MG. Ocular weakness is the commonest first symptom, and when it generalises it usually does so within 2 years.
MGFA clinical classification

Classes II–IV are split into a (mainly limb and trunk) and b (mainly swallowing and breathing muscles).

Causes and key features (3 marks in W24)

  • Who: any age; peaks in younger women and in older men. About 10–15% have a thymoma, and many younger patients have an enlarged (hyperplastic) thymus.
  • Fatigable weakness: worse with repeated use and towards evening, better after rest.
  • Eyes: drooping eyelids (ptosis) and double vision (diplopia).
  • Bulbar: slurred or nasal speech, difficulty chewing and swallowing, a weak "snarling" smile.
  • Neck and limbs: neck weakness (head drop), proximal arm and leg weakness (combing hair, climbing stairs).
  • Breathing: breathlessness, especially lying flat; it can fail suddenly (myasthenic crisis).
  • Reflexes, sensation and bladder are normal; wasting is unusual.
  • Triggers: infection, overexertion, heat, emotional stress, surgery, pregnancy, and some drugs (certain antibiotics, magnesium, beta-blockers, botulinum toxin).

Medical point that matters for physiotherapy

  • Diagnosis: antibody tests; repetitive nerve stimulation (falling responses) and single-fibre EMG; the ice-pack test (ptosis improves with cold); CT of the chest for thymoma.
  • Pyridostigmine (an anticholinesterase) improves strength for a few hours; its effect peaks about 1–2 hours after a dose, so plan exercise then.
  • Long-term: steroids and other immunosuppressants; newer antibody-based drugs; thymectomy (sternotomy precautions after surgery).
  • Crisis: IVIG or plasma exchange, and ventilation if needed.

Classic answer vs current guidance: exercise in myasthenia

Classic

Rest and avoid exercise, because effort makes the weakness worse.

Current

In stable MG, regular aerobic and strength training is safe and improves strength and daily function; tailor the intensity and keep training to keep the gains (Gilhus, 2021).

Write in the exam

"Graded, moderate aerobic and strength training when the disease is stable, timed after pyridostigmine, with rests and no exhaustion."

Precautions

  • No exercise during a flare, an infection or a crisis; stop if breathing, speech or swallowing worsen.
  • Avoid heat (hot packs, hot pools, hot rooms) and exhausting sessions.
  • Watch for crisis: breathlessness lying flat, a weak cough, choking, a falling vital capacity.
  • Tell the team before any injection of botulinum toxin for spasticity.

Exam points

  • Three features: fatigable weakness, ptosis and diplopia, bulbar weakness (with normal reflexes and sensation).
  • For 7 marks: aims, then exercise (type, timing, dose, rests), breathing care, energy conservation, aids, education about crisis and triggers.

2Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome

  • Antibodies against calcium channels on the nerve ending reduce ACh release; about half are linked to small-cell lung cancer.
  • Proximal leg weakness, reduced reflexes that briefly return after exercise, a dry mouth and other autonomic symptoms.
  • Unlike MG, strength and reflexes improve for a short time with repeated effort.
  • Physiotherapy: as for MG, plus care around cancer treatment.

3Inflammatory myopathies (myositis)

Definition and key features

  • Dermatomyositis: proximal weakness with a rash (violet eyelids, scaly patches over the knuckles); in adults, screen for cancer.
  • Polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (sometimes after statins): symmetrical proximal weakness, high CK.
  • Inclusion body myositis: over 50; slow, often asymmetrical weakness of the quadriceps and finger flexors; falls; poor response to drugs.
  • Swallowing difficulty and lung fibrosis can occur.

Medical point

Diagnosed by CK, myopathic EMG, MRI and muscle biopsy; treated with steroids and immunosuppressants (steroids themselves can weaken muscles).

Physiotherapy

  • Exercise has been found safe in all types and stages studied; start at low intensity under supervision and progress with regular review (Alexanderson, 2018).
  • Active phase: range of motion, positioning, gentle active and isometric work, breathing exercises.
  • Stable phase: progressive resistance and aerobic training; functional training (sit to stand, stairs); falls prevention (inclusion body myositis).

Other causes of muscle weakness in adults: steroid and thyroid myopathy (proximal weakness), statin-related myopathy, and weakness acquired in intensive care.

5Exercise rules for muscle disease

  • Moderate aerobic and strength training appears safe in slowly progressive muscle diseases, but the trials are small and their quality is low (Cochrane, 2019).
  • Avoid eccentric, high-intensity and exhausting exercise.
  • Stop and review if pain, cramps or weakness last into the next day, or if the urine turns dark after exercise (muscle breakdown).
  • Screen the heart and breathing (vital capacity sitting and lying) where the disease can affect them.
  • Prevent contractures and falls; use orthoses and aids early; teach energy conservation.

3 mEnlist three features of myasthenia gravis. (W24)

(1) Fatigable weakness that worsens with use and towards evening and improves with rest. (2) Ptosis and diplopia, usually the first symptoms. (3) Bulbar weakness: nasal speech, difficulty chewing and swallowing. Also proximal limb and neck weakness and breathing weakness, with normal reflexes and sensation.

3 mDifferentiate myasthenic crisis from cholinergic crisis.

Myasthenic crisis is a flare of the disease (infection, missed dose, trigger drug) with rapidly increasing weakness and breathing failure. Cholinergic crisis is an overdose of anticholinesterase, with weakness plus sweating, salivation, cramps, diarrhoea, small pupils and twitching. Both are emergencies.

7 mWrite physiotherapy management for myasthenia gravis. (W19)

MG is an autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction with fatigable weakness. Assess strength before and after repetition, endurance, breathing (vital capacity, cough), swallowing and function. Aims: maintain and improve strength and endurance, protect breathing, manage fatigue, keep independence. Treatment: graded moderate aerobic and resistance training in stable disease, timed 1–2 hours after pyridostigmine, in a cool room, with short sets and rests; inspiratory muscle training and breathing retraining; airway clearance and assisted cough; energy conservation and pacing; collar for head drop, eye patch for diplopia, falls prevention; after thymectomy, chest physiotherapy and sternal precautions. Precautions: no exercise in a flare or infection, avoid heat and exhaustion, recognise crisis and refer, avoid trigger drugs. Education on triggers and warning signs.

LAQPractice: a 32-year-old teacher with generalised myasthenia gravis (MGFA class III) has ptosis, double vision, and arm and leg weakness that is worse by evening, and is breathless climbing stairs. Write ICF with clinical reasoning, goals and physiotherapy. (5 + 3 + 7)

ICF (5): body functions: fatigable proximal weakness, ptosis, diplopia, reduced breathing strength, fatigue; activities: stairs, writing on the board, standing to teach, reading; participation: full teaching day; environment: heat in classrooms, stairs; personal: young, worried about crises. Goals (3): short term: knows pacing and timing of exercise, starts an exercise and breathing programme; long term: teaches a full day with planned rests, climbs stairs without breathlessness. Physiotherapy (7): as in the 7-mark answer, adapted to work (sit to teach, lessons with the hardest tasks in the morning after a dose).
