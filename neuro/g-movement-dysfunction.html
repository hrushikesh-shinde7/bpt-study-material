GMovement dysfunction in daily tasks: bed mobility, lying to sitting, standing up, walking, balance, reaching and manipulation

The syllabus asks you to know how movement fails in seven everyday tasks after damage to the nervous system. For each task learn three things: its essential components, the common problems and compensations (stroke is the usual example), and how to practise it; this is the backbone of every management answer.

My revision

1How movement fails

  • Impairments: weakness and slowness, loss of dexterity and coordination, altered tone, sensory loss, shortened soft tissues, poor balance.
  • Adaptations (compensations): the person uses the stronger side, the arms, a wider base or a stiff trunk to get the task done. Some compensations are useful; others block recovery (learned non-use of the weak arm, all weight on the strong leg).
  • Training rule: analyse the task, find the missing components, practise them and then the whole task many times in varied real settings, and measure the result (see motor relearning and motor learning).

2Bed mobility (rolling and bridging)

  • Essential components: turning the head and reaching the arm across; flexing the top hip and knee; rotating the trunk; bridging (lifting the pelvis) to move up the bed and onto a bedpan.
  • Common problems: cannot bring the weak arm or leg across; pulls on the bed rail with the strong arm; cannot bridge because of weak hip extensors.
  • Training: practise rolling to both sides with the weak limbs placed to help; bridging with the weak foot loaded; reduce reliance on the rail.

3Lying to sitting and sitting to lying

  • Essential components: roll to side lying, lower the legs over the edge, push up through the lower arm while side-flexing the neck and trunk upwards; the reverse to lie down.
  • Common problems: pulls straight up with the strong arm; legs stay on the bed; poor sitting balance at the end.
  • Training: practise each part (side lying, legs off, push up), then the whole task; vary bed height and side; practise lying down with control.

4Standing up and sitting down

  • Essential components: feet placed back (behind the knees); trunk moves forward by flexing at the hips with a straight spine; knees move forward over the feet; then hips and knees extend together to stand; sitting down reverses this with controlled lowering.
  • Common problems: weight on the strong leg; weak foot left forward; not enough forward lean; pushes up with the arms; drops into the chair when sitting down.
  • Training: foot placement with the weak foot back; practise with a higher seat, then lower; repetitions in sets; step-ups and leg strengthening; time it (for example the five times sit-to-stand test).

5Walking

  • Essential components: stance: hip extension, a controlled small knee bend at loading then extension, forward roll over the ankle, push-off; swing: knee bending, hip flexion and ankle dorsiflexion to clear the foot; a small side-to-side shift of the pelvis.
  • Common problems: short stance on the weak leg, knee hyperextension or buckling, little push-off, stiff knee and foot drop in swing, circumduction, slow speed.
  • Training: walking practice with intensity and variety, treadmill, stepping drills, strengthening, an AFO when needed; measure speed and distance (see gait training).

6Balance

  • Essential components: keeping the centre of mass over the base of support while still and while moving; anticipatory adjustments before a movement; reactive steps or strategies after a push; using vision, vestibular and body sensation.
  • Common problems: wide base, weight kept on the strong side, stiffening, holding on with the arms, fear of falling.
  • Training: reaching beyond arm's length in sitting and standing, stepping in all directions, practice on varied surfaces, perturbations, dual tasks; measure with Berg, TUG or Mini-BESTest (see balance).

7Reaching

  • Essential components: shoulder flexion with the scapula rotating upwards and moving forward, elbow extension, a wrist ready for grasp, and trunk movement only for targets beyond arm's length.
  • Common problems: the shoulder hitches up instead of the scapula rotating; the elbow stays bent; the trunk leans forward to make up for a short reach.
  • Training: reaching to targets at different heights and distances; limiting forward trunk lean during practice (trunk restraint improved reaching in a trial after stroke; Michaelsen et al., 2006); strengthening shoulder and elbow; task practice with real objects.

8Manipulation (grasp, release and hand use)

  • Essential components: wrist extension during grasp; opening the hand to match the object; thumb opposition; grip force scaled to the object; release; moving objects within the hand.
  • Common problems: weak wrist extensors so the grip is weak; the hand cannot open (flexor tone or weak extensors); poor thumb opposition; too much or too little grip force; poor release.
  • Training: graded object tasks (size, weight, texture), wrist-extension practice in function, constraint-induced movement therapy or intensive bimanual practice for suitable patients, splints only for a stated aim (see orthoses).

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite the essential components of sit to stand.

Feet placed back behind the knees; trunk moves forward by hip flexion with a straight spine; knees move forward over the feet; hips and knees then extend together to upright standing.

3 mList common compensations during reaching after stroke.

Shoulder hitching (scapular elevation) instead of upward rotation; elbow kept flexed; forward trunk lean to extend the reach; using the other hand instead.

7 mDescribe the training of standing up and sitting down in a hemiplegic patient.

Analyse the task (foot placement, forward lean, knee advancement, extension, controlled descent) and identify problems (weight on the strong side, weak foot forward, too little lean, arm pushing). Train: place the weak foot back, practise forward lean with reaching, start from a higher seat and lower it, repeated practice in sets, discourage arm use, practise sitting down slowly, strengthen hip and knee extensors with step-ups; vary seats (chair, bed, toilet, car); measure with a timed sit-to-stand test.

LAQPractice: a 62-year-old man 6 weeks after a left MCA stroke cannot get out of bed alone, stands up only by pulling on a rail, and reaches with a shoulder hitch. Analyse these three tasks and plan training. (15)

Lying to sitting: pulls straight up; train rolling to side lying, legs over the edge, push up through the left arm, with the right leg helped and then not. Sit to stand: weight on the left, right foot forward, pulls on rail; train right foot back, forward lean, raised seat, repetitions, then lower seat, no rail. Reaching: shoulder hitch, trunk lean; train reaching to targets within arm's length with trunk kept back, scapular upward rotation and elbow extension practice, then functional reaching with objects. Measure: timed sit to stand, reach distance, independence in bed mobility.
