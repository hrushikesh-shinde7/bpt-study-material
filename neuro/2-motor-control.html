2Motor control: the theories behind neurological physiotherapy

"Explain any two theories of motor control" was a 7-mark question in three papers (W19, S21, S23). Each theory answers one question: how does the nervous system organise movement? The older answers (reflexes, a hierarchy) produced the neurophysiological approaches; the newer ones (systems, dynamic systems, ecological) produced task-oriented training. Learn each theory as idea, limitation and clinical use.

My revision

1What motor control means

  • Motor control is the ability to regulate or direct the mechanisms essential to movement.
  • Movement emerges from three things acting together: the individual (perception, cognition, action systems), the task and the environment.
  • A theory of motor control matters because it decides what the therapist treats: reflexes, tone, or the person practising a task in a real setting.

2The theories

TheoryKey ideaLimitation and clinical use
Reflex theory (Sherrington)Reflexes are the building blocks of movement; complex movement is a chain of reflexes triggered by sensory inputLimitation: cannot explain movement without sensory input, fast movements, or new movements. Use: assess reflexes; use sensory stimuli to bring out or inhibit movement (Rood)
Hierarchical theory (Hughlings Jackson, Magnus)Control is top-down: the cortex controls the brainstem, which controls the spinal cord. A lesion of higher centres releases primitive reflexes; development follows a fixed sequenceLimitation: lower levels can also control higher ones (spinal reflexes can dominate), and not every adult movement goes through a hierarchy. Use: the neurophysiological approaches (Bobath, Brunnstrom, Rood) that inhibit abnormal reflexes and follow developmental sequences
Motor programming theoryMovement can be produced by a stored central program or central pattern generator, even without sensory input (for example the spinal pattern generator for stepping)Limitation: a program cannot alone explain how movement adapts to body and environment. Use: retrain whole movement patterns, not single muscles; treadmill stepping uses spinal pattern generators
Systems theory (Bernstein)The body is a mechanical system with many degrees of freedom; movement results from many systems working together, controlled through synergies, with forces from the body and environmentLimitation: less detail on how the nervous system and environment interact. Use: assess and treat impairments in all systems (strength, range, sensation, perception) as well as the nervous system; train functional synergies
Dynamic systems theoryMovement self-organises from interacting parts; preferred patterns are stable "attractor states", and a change in one variable (a control parameter such as speed) can shift behaviour to a new pattern (walk to run)Limitation: can underplay the role of the nervous system. Use: find the control parameter that shifts an abnormal pattern (speed, support, task demand); practise at transition points
Ecological theory (Gibson)Perception and action are linked: people perceive the environment in terms of what it allows them to do (affordances), and act to achieve goalsLimitation: less on the nervous system's organisation. Use: practise real tasks in varied real environments; teach the person to read the environment (kerbs, crowds)

Current practice draws on several theories at once: the systems approach sees movement as arising from the person, the task and the environment, which is the basis of task-oriented training.

3 mWhat is the dynamic systems theory?

Movement self-organises from many interacting parts; stable preferred patterns are attractor states; changing a control parameter (such as speed) can shift behaviour to a new pattern. Clinically, find and change the parameter that moves a patient out of an abnormal pattern.

3 mGive two limitations of the reflex theory.

It cannot explain movement without sensory input (deafferented animals still move), fast movement sequences that are too quick for reflex chains, or the creation of new movements.

7 mExplain any two theories of motor control.

Hierarchical theory: top-down control from cortex to brainstem to spinal cord; higher lesions release primitive reflexes; limitation: lower levels also influence higher ones; use: Bobath, Brunnstrom, Rood. Systems theory (Bernstein): the body as a mechanical system with many degrees of freedom, controlled through synergies, with movement arising from many systems and external forces; use: assess and treat all systems and train functional tasks. Close with: current practice combines theories in task-oriented training.

LAQExplain how your choice of motor control theory changes the treatment of a patient with hemiplegia. (Practice question)

Under a hierarchical view, treatment inhibits abnormal reflexes and tone and follows developmental sequences (Bobath, Brunnstrom). Under a systems or task-oriented view, treatment assesses all impairments (strength, range, sensation, balance), practises real tasks (sit to stand, reaching, walking) with many repetitions, varies the task and environment, and uses feedback. Current evidence favours task-oriented practice, borrowing techniques from older approaches when they help.
