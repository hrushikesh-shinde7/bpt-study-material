GNeurological assessment and investigations: UMN vs LMN, reflexes, sensation, cranial nerves, EMG and nerve conduction

Upper vs lower motor neuron lesions came up in 6 papers as a 3-mark question, and the same skills are needed to assess every long case. Decide first whether the problem is in the upper or the lower motor neuron, because that decides the treatment: tone and movement control for an upper motor neuron lesion, and strengthening, protection and sensory care for a lower motor neuron lesion.

My revision

1The order of a neurological assessment

  1. History: chief complaints; onset (sudden or gradual), duration and progression; past, family, personal and social history; drugs; red flags.
  2. General examination: vital signs, level of consciousness (GCS), built, posture, attitude of the limbs, deformities, skin and pressure areas, aids in use.
  3. Higher functions: orientation, memory, speech, cognition (MMSE or MoCA), perception (see cognition and perception).
  4. Cranial nerves (section 6).
  5. Motor system: bulk (girth), tone, power, reflexes, coordination, involuntary movements (sections 3 and 4).
  6. Sensory system: superficial, deep and combined sensations; dermatomes (section 5).
  7. Balance and gait (see balance and coordination).
  8. Function: bed mobility, lying to sitting, sit to stand, walking, reaching and manipulation; ADL (Barthel index).
  9. Outcome measures for the condition (see scales) and the investigations (section 7).
  10. Sum up: problem list, ICF and functional diagnosis, goals and plan (see ICF).

2Upper vs lower motor neuron lesions

Asked for 3 marks in S16, S20 and W21 (differences) and in W16, W22 and S24 (signs or features of a UMN lesion).

  • The upper motor neuron (UMN) runs from the motor cortex down the corticospinal tract to the anterior horn of the spinal cord (or to the cranial nerve nuclei).
  • The lower motor neuron (LMN) runs from the anterior horn cell (or cranial nerve nucleus) through the root, plexus and peripheral nerve to the muscle.

Memory hook

UMN goes up, LMN goes down

  • UMN: tone up, reflexes up, toes up (Babinski).
  • LMN: tone down, reflexes down, muscle bulk down.

Features of a UMN lesion (W16, W22, S24)

  • Negative features (what is lost): weakness in a pyramidal pattern, loss of fine and skilled movements, slowness and early fatigue.
  • Positive features (what is released): spasticity, brisk reflexes with spread and clonus, the Babinski sign and Hoffmann's sign, flexor or extensor spasms, associated reactions and mass synergy patterns.
  • Loss of the abdominal and cremasteric reflexes; little wasting and no fasciculation.
  • Exception: straight after an acute lesion (cerebral or spinal shock) the limb is flaccid with absent reflexes; spasticity appears over days to weeks.

Why it changes physiotherapy

  • UMN: manage tone, train movement control and tasks, prevent contracture, and strengthen (strength training does not increase spasticity).
  • LMN: strengthen each muscle according to its grade, protect weak or denervated muscles from overstretching with splints, care for numb skin, and watch for reinnervation.
Recall check. Give six differences between UMN and LMN lesions without looking at the table.

3Motor examination

  • Bulk: look for wasting and fasciculation; measure girth at fixed distances from a bony point on both sides.
  • Tone: passive movement at slow and fast speeds; spasticity (velocity-dependent, clasp-knife), rigidity (lead-pipe or cogwheel), hypotonia; grade spasticity with the Modified Ashworth Scale (see tone).
  • Power: MRC grades for LMN and muscle disease. In UMN lesions, isolated muscle testing is less useful; record voluntary control and synergy (Brunnstrom stage, Fugl-Meyer) instead.
  • Coordination: finger–nose, heel–shin, rapid alternating movements, rebound (see ataxia).
  • Involuntary movements: tremor (at rest in Parkinson's disease; on action or intention in cerebellar disease), chorea, athetosis, dystonia, ballismus, myoclonus, tics.

Memory hook

1, 2, buckle my shoe

  • S1–2 buckle my shoe: ankle.
  • L3–4 kick the door: knee.
  • C5–6 pick up sticks: biceps and brachioradialis.
  • C7–8 lay them straight: triceps.

Grading: 0 absent; 1+ reduced (trace); 2+ normal; 3+ brisk (can be normal); 4+ very brisk with clonus. Compare the two sides, and use reinforcement (clenching the teeth, or pulling the hooked fingers apart: Jendrassik's manoeuvre) before calling a reflex absent.

Superficial reflexes

  • Abdominal (about T8–T12): stroke each quarter towards the umbilicus; the umbilicus moves towards the stroke. Lost in UMN lesions.
  • Cremasteric (L1–L2): stroke the inner thigh; the testis rises.
  • Plantar (S1): stroke the outer sole from the heel towards the little toe and across; the toes normally curl down.
  • Anal (S2–S4): touching the perianal skin contracts the anal sphincter; important in spinal cord injury.

Pathological signs

  • Babinski sign: the big toe extends and the other toes fan out on plantar stimulation (normal in infants).
  • Hoffmann's sign: flicking the nail of the middle finger makes the thumb and index finger flex.
  • Clonus: a quick, sustained dorsiflexion of the ankle produces rhythmic beats; sustained clonus means a UMN lesion.
  • Primitive reflexes reappearing in adults (grasp, sucking, palmomental) suggest frontal lobe damage.

Test with the eyes closed, compare with the normal side, and move from the numb area towards normal skin.

Patterns of sensory loss

  • Glove and stocking: polyneuropathy (diabetes, alcohol).
  • One dermatome: root lesion (disc prolapse).
  • One nerve territory: peripheral nerve injury.
  • A level on the trunk: spinal cord lesion.
  • One half of the body: brain lesion (thalamus, internal capsule, cortex).
  • Pain and temperature lost, touch kept: syringomyelia, anterior cord syndrome; Brown-Séquard: power and joint sense lost on one side, pain and temperature on the other (see spinal cord).
  • Saddle anaesthesia with bladder or bowel change: cauda equina (an emergency).

Dermatomes: key sensory points (ISNCSCI)

  • Arm: C5 outer side of the elbow crease; C6 thumb; C7 middle finger; C8 little finger; T1 inner side of the elbow crease; T2 apex of the axilla.
  • Trunk: T4 nipple line; T6 xiphisternum; T10 umbilicus; T12 middle of the inguinal ligament.
  • Leg: L2 middle of the front of the thigh; L3 inner knee (medial femoral condyle); L4 medial malleolus; L5 top of the foot at the third toe joint; S1 outer heel; S2 middle of the back of the knee; S3 ischial tuberosity; S4–5 around the anus.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite three differences between UMN and LMN lesions. (S20, W21)

Tone: increased (spasticity) in UMN, reduced (flaccid) in LMN. Reflexes: brisk with clonus in UMN, reduced or absent in LMN. Plantar: extensor (Babinski) in UMN, flexor or absent in LMN. Also: wasting slight in UMN, marked in LMN; fasciculation absent in UMN, may be present in LMN.

3 mWrite the clinical features of a UMN lesion. (W22, S24)

Weakness in a pyramidal pattern with loss of fine movements; spasticity (clasp-knife); brisk reflexes and clonus; extensor plantar (Babinski) and Hoffmann's sign; lost abdominal and cremasteric reflexes; little wasting and no fasciculation; associated reactions and synergies. Early after onset there may be a flaccid shock phase.

7 mDescribe the examination of the sensory system in a neurological patient.

Preparation (quiet room, eyes closed, compare sides, numb to normal). Superficial: light touch (cotton), pain (pin), temperature (tubes). Deep: joint position sense, vibration (128 Hz fork), deep pressure. Combined: two-point discrimination, stereognosis, graphaesthesia, localisation, extinction. Chart the findings on a dermatome map, name the pattern (glove and stocking, dermatomal, nerve territory, sensory level, hemisensory, dissociated) and give its meaning for physiotherapy: skin protection, balance training with vision, sensory re-education.

LAQPractice from the S16 ALS case: a 36-year-old dentist has wasting of the small hand muscles, fasciculations in the arm, hyperreflexia in both arms, hyporeflexia in both legs and bilateral Babinski signs. Sort the findings into UMN and LMN signs and write the assessment you would do. (5 + 10)

UMN signs (5): hyperreflexia in the arms, Babinski signs. LMN signs: wasting, fasciculations, reduced leg reflexes. Both together point to ALS. Assessment (10): history (onset, progression, falls, cramps, swallowing, breathing at night, work); bulk, girth and fasciculations; tone; MRC grades of key muscles, grip and pinch; deep, superficial and pathological reflexes; sensation (expected normal); coordination and fine hand tasks; balance and gait; breathing (vital capacity, cough); function (ALSFRS-R, Barthel); EMG and nerve conduction reports.
