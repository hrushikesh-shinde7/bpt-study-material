5A(l)Disorders of the autonomic nervous system: Horner's syndrome, blood pressure problems and autonomic dysreflexia

Horner's syndrome was a 3-mark question in S23, and autonomic dysreflexia, asked in most papers, is covered fully on the spinal cord page. The autonomic system matters in every session with a neurological patient, because blood pressure, heart rate and temperature decide whether it is safe to sit up, stand and exercise.

2Horner's syndrome (3 marks in S23)

  • Definition: loss of the sympathetic supply to one side of the face and eye.
  • Clinical features (same side): mild ptosis (drooping upper lid), miosis (small pupil, most obvious in dim light), anhidrosis (no sweating of the face), and an apparently sunken eye (enophthalmos); the pupil still reacts to light.
The three-neuron pathway and causes
NeuronCourseCauses
First order (central)Hypothalamus down the brainstem and cervical cord to T1Lateral medullary (brainstem) stroke, syringomyelia, cervical cord injury, MS
Second order (preganglionic)T1 root over the lung apex to the superior cervical ganglionT1 root avulsion in a lower brachial plexus (Klumpke's) injury, lung apex (Pancoast) tumour, neck trauma or surgery
Third order (postganglionic)Along the internal carotid artery to the eyeCarotid artery dissection, cluster headache, cavernous sinus lesions

Why physiotherapists care: in a brachial plexus injury, Horner's syndrome means the T1 root is torn from the cord (preganglionic), which carries a poor outlook for natural recovery of the hand (see obstetric plexus and nerve injuries).

3Orthostatic (postural) hypotension

  • Definition: a sustained fall in systolic blood pressure of at least 20 mmHg, or diastolic of at least 10 mmHg, within 3 minutes of standing or head-up tilt (consensus, 2011).
  • Neurological causes: spinal cord injury at or above T6, Parkinson's disease and multiple system atrophy, diabetic and other autonomic neuropathies, GBS, long bed rest, and drugs (blood-pressure tablets, levodopa, diuretics).
  • Symptoms: dizziness, blurred or greying vision, weakness, yawning, nausea, fainting; in tetraplegia symptoms may be few despite large falls.

Physiotherapy

  • Measure blood pressure lying and after sitting or standing; record symptoms.
  • Gradual uprighting: head-of-bed elevation, then tilt table or sitting, raising the angle in small steps as blood pressure allows (see spinal cord).
  • Reduce pooling: abdominal binder, compression stockings, leg exercises (ankle pumps) before rising.
  • Slow position changes, sitting on the bed edge before standing; good fluid intake (as the medical team advises); avoid heat and large meals before exercise.
  • Physical counter-manoeuvres: crossing the legs, tensing the leg and buttock muscles, or squatting raise blood pressure for a short time.
  • If symptoms start: stop, recline or lower the tilt, raise the legs, and check blood pressure.

4Hypertension: autonomic dysreflexia

A sudden, dangerous rise in blood pressure in people with spinal cord injury at or above T6, triggered by a stimulus below the lesion (most often a full bladder): pounding headache, flushing and sweating above the lesion, a slow pulse. Sit up, loosen clothing, check the bladder and bowel, and monitor blood pressure. The full answer with the flowchart is on the spinal cord page.

5Other autonomic problems

  • Temperature control: after high cord injury the person cannot sweat or shiver below the lesion, so body temperature follows the room (poikilothermia): avoid heat and cold, cool rooms for exercise, dress for the weather.
  • Heart rate: slow heart rate in acute tetraplegia, especially with suction or turning; irregular heart rhythm and blood pressure swings in GBS (dysautonomia).
  • Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity after severe brain injury: episodes of fast heart rate, high blood pressure, fever, fast breathing, sweating and posturing; calm handling, and treatment by the medical team.
  • Bladder, bowel and sexual function: see bladder and bowel.
  • Complex regional pain syndrome (sympathetic features of pain, swelling, colour and sweating change): see neuropathies.

3 mClinical features of Horner's syndrome. (S23)

Same-side mild ptosis, miosis (small pupil, reacting to light), anhidrosis of the face and apparent enophthalmos, from loss of the sympathetic supply. Causes: central (lateral medullary stroke, syringomyelia, cord injury), preganglionic (T1 root avulsion in Klumpke's injury, Pancoast tumour), postganglionic (carotid dissection).

3 mDefine orthostatic hypotension and list three ways to manage it in physiotherapy.

A sustained fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing or head-up tilt. Management: gradual uprighting (tilt table in small steps), abdominal binder and stockings, leg exercises before rising, slow position changes, counter-manoeuvres; stop and recline if symptoms start.

7 mPhysiotherapy considerations for autonomic problems in a patient with C6 tetraplegia.

Orthostatic hypotension: binder and stockings, gradual tilt, blood pressure monitoring, counter-measures. Autonomic dysreflexia: know the signs and the steps (sit up, loosen, bladder, bowel, other triggers), avoid tight straps, check the catheter before sessions. Temperature: avoid overheating and cold, cool room, fluids. Heart: slow heart rate with suction or turning in the acute stage. Bladder and bowel routines before sessions. Educate the patient and carers to recognise and act.

LAQPractice: a 68-year-old man with Parkinson's disease faints twice when getting out of bed in the morning. Assess, explain the cause, and plan physiotherapy and advice. (5 + 3 + 7)

Assess (5): blood pressure and pulse lying and at 1 and 3 minutes standing, symptoms, timing with meals and levodopa, fluid intake, falls and injuries, other autonomic signs (constipation, urinary urgency). Cause (3): orthostatic hypotension from autonomic failure in PD, worsened by levodopa, morning dehydration and heat. Plan (7): sit on the bed edge and do ankle pumps before standing; stand slowly with support; counter-manoeuvres; compression stockings or an abdominal binder; fluids as advised; avoid hot baths and big meals before activity; exercise lying or seated at first; report to the doctor for a medicine review; teach the family what to do if he feels faint.
