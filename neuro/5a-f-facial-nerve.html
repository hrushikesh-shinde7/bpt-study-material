5A(f)Facial (VII) nerve palsy, Bell's palsy, trigeminal nerve and trigeminal neuralgia

The facial and trigeminal nerves came up in 9 papers: a long case on Bell's palsy, physiotherapy for Bell's palsy for 7 marks three times, trigeminal neuralgia twice, and short notes on Bell's phenomenon, synkinesis and nerve assessment. Most people with Bell's palsy recover, especially with early steroids; physiotherapy protects the eye, keeps the face moving symmetrically and limits the unwanted movements that can follow regrowth.

My revision

1Definition

  • Bell's palsy is an acute, one-sided, lower motor neuron (LMN) facial nerve palsy with no identifiable cause, developing over less than 72 hours. It is the commonest cause of facial palsy.
  • It is thought to follow viral inflammation (often herpes simplex) that makes the nerve swell inside its narrow bony canal.

2Classification and grading

UMN vs LMN facial weakness

House-Brackmann grading

GradeDescription
INormal
IIMild: slight weakness on close look; symmetry at rest; eye closes fully with little effort
IIIModerate: obvious but not disfiguring weakness; eye closes fully with effort; synkinesis may be present
IVModerately severe: disfiguring asymmetry; eye does not close fully
VSevere: barely perceptible movement
VITotal paralysis

The Sunnybrook Facial Grading System (resting symmetry, voluntary movement and synkinesis, 0–100) is more detailed and sensitive to change.

3Causes of facial palsy

  • LMN: Bell's palsy (idiopathic); Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster: painful vesicles in the ear, worse outlook); trauma (temporal bone fracture, facial wounds); middle ear infection and cholesteatoma; parotid tumours or surgery; Guillain-Barré syndrome (often both sides); leprosy; diabetes; Lyme disease.
  • UMN: stroke, tumour, MS affecting the corticobulbar fibres.
  • Risk of Bell's palsy is higher in pregnancy, diabetes and after viral infections.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Inflammation makes the nerve swell inside the narrow facial canal of the temporal bone, so the nerve is compressed: mostly conduction block (neurapraxia), sometimes axon loss.
  2. Because all the facial muscles on one side lose their supply, the face droops, the eye cannot close and the mouth cannot seal.
  3. Because branches inside the canal can be involved, taste (front of the tongue), tear and saliva production and the stapedius reflex (loud-sound sensitivity) can be affected.
  4. If axons are lost, regrowing fibres can reach the wrong muscles, causing synkinesis, muscle tightness (contracture) and "crocodile tears".

5Clinical features

Asked for 3 marks in W24 and with management for 7 marks in S24.

  • Sudden weakness of one whole side of the face over hours to 3 days; pain behind the ear may come first.
  • Cannot raise the eyebrow or wrinkle the forehead; eye does not close fully; flattened nasolabial fold; mouth droops and pulls to the normal side when smiling.
  • Food collects in the cheek; drinks dribble; speech of lip sounds (p, b, m) is unclear.
  • Watering or dry eye; loss of taste on the front two-thirds of the tongue; sensitivity to loud sounds.
  • Bell's phenomenon (3 marks in W18): when the patient tries to close the eyes, the eyeball on the weak side rolls upwards and outwards. This normal reflex becomes visible because the eyelid does not close.
  • Facial synkinesis (3 marks in W18): an involuntary movement of one part of the face during a voluntary movement of another part (for example the eye narrows when the person smiles, or the corner of the mouth lifts when blinking), due to misdirected regrowth of nerve fibres; it appears months after severe palsy. "Crocodile tears" (tears while eating) are a similar misdirection.

6Investigations

  • Bell's palsy is a clinical diagnosis; routine blood tests and imaging are not advised unless the picture is atypical (AAO-HNS, 2013).
  • Nerve conduction studies (electroneurography) and EMG may be offered in complete paralysis to predict recovery, not in incomplete paralysis.
  • Ear examination for Ramsay Hunt syndrome and middle ear disease; blood sugar in diabetics.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Oral steroids within 72 hours of onset for people aged 16 and over (strong recommendation). In a large trial, prednisolone raised complete recovery from 64% to 83% at 3 months and from 82% to 94% at 9 months (Sullivan et al., 2007).
  • Antivirals alone are not advised; they may be added to steroids.
  • Eye protection when the eye does not close: lubricating drops by day, ointment and taping or a patch at night, glasses outdoors.
  • Refer to a facial nerve specialist if new neurological signs, eye problems, or incomplete recovery at 3 months.
  • Botulinum toxin for synkinesis; surgery (nerve transfer, muscle transfer, gold weight in the eyelid) in long-standing paralysis.

8Complications and prevention

ComplicationPrevention and physio role

9PT assessment

  • History: onset, pain, ear symptoms, taste, hearing, eye symptoms, steroid start date, occupation (speaking, teaching).
  • Observation at rest: symmetry of eyebrow, eye, nasolabial fold and mouth.
  • Voluntary movement of each muscle group (section 5) and grading: House-Brackmann or Sunnybrook; photographs for comparison.
  • Eye closure and Bell's phenomenon; synkinesis in later stages.
  • Function: eating, drinking, speaking, blowing; quality of life (Facial Disability Index).

12PT management

Asked for 7 marks in W16 (with education and advice), W17 and S24.

By stage

  • Acute (paralysed): reassurance (most recover); eye care; gentle massage of the face (upward strokes towards the eye and ear); moist warmth if comfortable, protecting the eye; supporting the cheek with taping; chewing on the strong side.
  • Recovering (movement returning): active-assisted, then active exercises in front of a mirror for each muscle group: raise eyebrows, gently close the eyes, smile, purse the lips, puff the cheeks, flare the nostrils. Small, slow, symmetrical movements; hold the strong side back with the fingers so it does not overpower.
  • Late (synkinesis risk): facial neuromuscular retraining: slow, isolated movements with mirror or EMG biofeedback; relax the unwanted muscle while moving the wanted one; stretching and massage of tight muscles; botulinum toxin by the doctor.

Education and advice (W16)

  • Most people recover within weeks to months; start steroids early (doctor).
  • Eye: drops, ointment and taping at night, glasses outdoors; report pain or redness.
  • Eat on the strong side; clean food from the cheek after meals; sip through a straw.
  • Do exercises gently in front of a mirror several times a day; avoid forceful grimacing and gum chewing that encourage mass movements.

Electrical stimulation

Traditionally used for the paralysed face, but trials show no benefit over placebo for recovery, and many clinicians avoid it in case it encourages mass movement (Cochrane, 2011).

Classic answer vs current evidence: physiotherapy for Bell's palsy

Classic

Faradic or galvanic stimulation of the facial muscles, infrared, massage and vigorous facial exercises.

Current

The 2013 guideline makes no recommendation for or against physiotherapy because evidence is weak; electrical stimulation showed no benefit over placebo. Tailored facial exercises and mirror or EMG biofeedback may help, especially in moderate and chronic cases (low-quality evidence).

Write in the exam

"Eye care, reassurance, gentle massage, mirror-guided slow symmetrical facial exercises, neuromuscular retraining for synkinesis; electrical stimulation has no proven benefit."

13Trigeminal nerve and trigeminal neuralgia

Clinical assessment of the trigeminal nerve (3 marks in W18)

  • Sensory: light touch and pinprick over the forehead (V1, ophthalmic), cheek (V2, maxillary) and jaw (V3, mandibular), comparing sides.
  • Motor: clench the teeth and feel the masseter and temporalis; open the mouth against resistance: the jaw deviates to the weak side (pterygoids).
  • Reflexes: corneal reflex (V1 carries the sensation; the facial nerve closes the eye); jaw jerk (brisk in UMN lesions above the pons).

Trigeminal neuralgia (7 marks in S20 and W22)

  • Features: sudden, severe, electric shock-like pain on one side of the face, usually in the V2 or V3 area, lasting seconds to about 2 minutes, in repeated attacks; triggered by light touch, talking, chewing, brushing the teeth, shaving or a cold wind ("trigger zones"); normal sensation between attacks.
  • Causes: usually a blood vessel pressing on the nerve root; sometimes MS or a tumour (suspect in young people or with sensory loss).
  • Medical: carbamazepine (first choice) or oxcarbazepine; microvascular decompression or other procedures if drugs fail.
  • Physiotherapy (supportive): education on avoiding triggers (scarf in cold wind, lukewarm water, soft toothbrush, chewing on the other side); relaxation and stress management; posture and jaw relaxation; TENS may be tried by some patients away from trigger zones; encourage nutrition and oral hygiene, which patients often avoid. Do not stimulate trigger zones directly.

14Precautions

Take care
  • Protect the eye during heat or light treatment; never apply heat with the eye open.
  • Avoid forceful, mass facial exercises and vigorous gum chewing in the recovery phase.
  • Refer if weakness spreads, other cranial nerves are involved, the ear has vesicles, or there is no recovery by 3 months.
  • Trigeminal neuralgia: do not touch trigger zones during treatment.

15Prognosis

  • Most people recover fully; with early prednisolone, about 94% recover completely by 9 months (Sullivan et al., 2007).
  • Worse outlook: complete paralysis, older age, Ramsay Hunt syndrome, diabetes, no recovery by 3 weeks, and signs of axon loss on nerve tests.
  • Synkinesis and tightness may remain after severe palsy.

3 mWhat is Bell's phenomenon?

On attempted eye closure, the eyeball on the paralysed side rolls upward and outward; it is visible because the eyelid cannot close in LMN facial palsy.

3 mWrite the signs and symptoms of Bell's palsy.

Sudden one-sided weakness of the whole face; cannot wrinkle the forehead or close the eye; Bell's phenomenon; flattened nasolabial fold and drooping mouth; drooling and food pocketing; pain behind the ear; loss of taste on the front of the tongue; sensitivity to loud sound; watery or dry eye.

7 mPhysiotherapy management of Bell's palsy with education and advice.

Assess and grade (House-Brackmann). Education: good outlook, early steroids, eye care, eating on the strong side, gentle practice. Acute: massage, warmth with eye protection, cheek taping. Late: neuromuscular retraining with biofeedback for synkinesis, stretching tight muscles. Evidence note: electrical stimulation shows no benefit. Refer if incomplete at 3 months.
