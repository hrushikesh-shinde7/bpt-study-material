TP2Neurotherapeutic approaches: Rood, Bobath, Brunnstrom, MRP, PNF, imagery and CIMT

The named approaches came up in 8 papers, as 3-mark lists of principles (Rood, Bobath, MRP) and 7-mark questions on Rood, MRP and PNF. Learn each approach's principles and two or three techniques, because examiners ask for them by name. Then add one line every time: no single approach is better than the others, and the best results come from active, task-specific practice that borrows from all of them.

My revision

1What the approaches are, and what the evidence says

  • The neurophysiological approaches (Rood, Bobath, Brunnstrom, PNF) were developed from the 1940s to the 1970s, using reflexes, sensory stimulation and handling to change tone and bring out movement.
  • The motor learning approaches (Motor Relearning Programme, task-oriented training, CIMT) treat the patient as an active learner who practises real tasks.
  • Evidence: physical rehabilitation that combines components from different approaches is effective after stroke, but no one named approach is more or less effective than another, so treatment should not be limited to compartmentalised approaches (Pollock et al., Cochrane, 2014).

Classic answer vs current evidence: choosing an approach

Classic

Choose one approach (for example Bobath) and follow its techniques for the whole programme.

Current

Use evidence-based components from any approach, centred on active, repetitive, task-specific practice with enough dose; passive handling alone does not improve function.

Write in the exam

Give the approach's principles and techniques as asked, then add: "Used as part of task-oriented practice; no single approach is superior (Cochrane, 2014)."

2Rood's approach

Asked for 3 marks in S16 (three Rood techniques for spasticity) and S22 (three principles), and for 7 marks in S17 (principles and techniques).

Principles

  1. Normalise tone and evoke the wanted response with the right sensory stimulus: some stimuli facilitate, others inhibit.
  2. Sensorimotor control develops in sequence (the ontogenetic sequence), so treatment follows the developmental patterns.
  3. Movement is purposeful: use activities that call out the response automatically.
  4. Repetition of sensorimotor responses is needed for learning.

Ontogenetic motor patterns: supine withdrawal, roll over, pivot prone, neck co-contraction, prone on elbows, all fours, standing, walking. Levels of control: mobility, stability, controlled mobility (mobility on stability), skill.

Techniques

Facilitatory (to raise tone or start movement, as in hypotonia):

  • Fast brushing over the skin of the muscle
  • Quick icing (brief strokes of ice)
  • Quick stretch; tapping over the muscle belly or tendon
  • High-frequency vibration over the muscle
  • Heavy joint compression (approximation) for co-contraction; resistance
  • Fast vestibular stimulation (fast rocking, spinning)

Inhibitory (to lower tone, as in spasticity):

  • Slow stroking down the back over the spinal muscles for about 3–5 minutes
  • Neutral warmth: wrap the part or body in a blanket for about 10–20 minutes
  • Prolonged (maintained) stretch
  • Maintained pressure over the tendon insertion
  • Light joint compression; slow rhythmic rocking or rolling
  • Slow vestibular stimulation; prolonged cold

Three techniques for spasticity (S16): slow stroking, neutral warmth, prolonged stretch (or maintained tendon pressure, slow rocking, prolonged cold).

Rood's techniques are classic exam content; their effects are short-lived and research support is limited. Use them to prepare for active practice, not instead of it.

3Bobath concept (neurodevelopmental treatment, NDT)

Asked for 3 marks in W16 (three principles) and W19 (reflex-inhibiting patterns with an example).

Classic principles

  • Abnormal tone and abnormal postural reflexes block normal movement, so first inhibit them.
  • Facilitate normal automatic postural reactions (righting, equilibrium, protective reactions) and normal movement patterns.
  • Use handling through key points of control (head, shoulder girdle, pelvis proximally; hands and feet distally).
  • Treat the person as a whole, and include the affected side in all activity (bilateral, symmetrical).

Reflex-inhibiting patterns (RIPs)

Positions or movements opposite to the abnormal spastic pattern, used to reduce tone before facilitating movement.

  • Example, hemiplegic arm (flexor pattern): shoulder forward and externally rotated, elbow extended, forearm supinated, wrist and fingers extended, thumb abducted; for example, sitting with weight borne on the extended arm placed beside or behind the body.
  • Example, hemiplegic leg (extensor pattern): hip and knee flexed, hip abducted and externally rotated, ankle dorsiflexed.
  • Example, child with extensor thrust: a flexed position (head, trunk, hips and knees bent) in side lying or carried in flexion.

Contemporary Bobath and evidence

  • The modern Bobath concept describes itself as a problem-solving approach focused on postural control, selective movement and function, rather than inhibition followed by facilitation.
  • After stroke, it is not superior to other approaches (Pollock, 2014). In cerebral palsy, NDT in its original passive form is rated ineffective for motor goals (Novak et al., 2020).

4Brunnstrom's movement therapy

  • Built on the stages of motor recovery after stroke (flaccidity, synergies with spasticity, voluntary synergies, movements out of synergy, isolated movement); see the stages and synergy patterns on the stroke page.
  • Principle: in early stages, use associated reactions, tonic reflexes and synergies to bring out movement; then train the person to break out of synergy into isolated movement.
  • Phenomena used: associated reactions (effort in the strong limb raises tone in the weak one); Souques' phenomenon (lifting the arm above about 150° can release finger extension); Raimiste's phenomenon (resisted hip abduction or adduction of the strong leg causes the same movement in the weak leg); homolateral limb synkinesis.
  • Today: the stages remain useful for assessment, but deliberately training synergies is not recommended; aim for selective, task-specific movement.

5Motor Relearning Programme (Carr and Shepherd)

Asked for 3 marks in S18 (three principles) and 7 marks in W18 (MRP for the hemiplegic upper limb).

Principles

  • Recovery is relearning of real-life tasks, so practise the tasks themselves (task-specific) in the right context.
  • The patient is an active learner; the therapist teaches, using instructions, demonstration and feedback.
  • Eliminate unnecessary muscle activity and compensation; practise the missing components.
  • Practice and carry-over: many repetitions, with practice continued on the ward and at home.
  • Based on the biomechanics of normal movement and motor learning science.

Four steps for each task

  1. Analyse the task: compare with normal and find the missing components.
  2. Practise the missing components.
  3. Practise the whole task.
  4. Transfer the training into daily life.

Seven sections: upper limb function; oro-facial function; sitting up over the side of the bed; balanced sitting; standing up and sitting down; balanced standing; walking.

MRP for the hemiplegic upper limb (W18)

  • Analyse: reaching (shoulder flexion and abduction with the shoulder girdle moving correctly, elbow control), grasping and releasing (wrist extension with the fingers flexing, opposition), manipulating.
  • Missing components: in lying, reach up to the ceiling and control the arm at different points of range; in sitting, reach forward and sideways to objects; wrist extension while grasping a cup; release; thumb opposition to each finger; turning the hand over.
  • Whole task: drink from a cup, pick up and place objects, comb hair, write.
  • Avoid: shoulder hitching, trunk leaning; protect the shoulder; stop soft-tissue shortening (arm positioned and stretched).
  • Transfer: practice sheets for the ward and home, with family help.

6Proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF)

Asked for 7 marks in W25 (principles of PNF).

Philosophy

A positive approach that builds on what the person can do, works on function, uses the strong parts to help the weak, treats the whole person, and uses motor learning.

Basic principles (procedures)

  1. Resistance: the right amount to facilitate contraction and motor learning.
  2. Irradiation and reinforcement: strong muscles spread activity to weaker ones in the pattern.
  3. Manual contact: a lumbrical grip over the muscles that should work guides direction.
  4. Body position and body mechanics of the therapist in line with the movement.
  5. Verbal commands: short, clear, well-timed.
  6. Vision: the patient watches the movement.
  7. Traction and approximation: traction facilitates movement; approximation facilitates stability and weight-bearing.
  8. Stretch: the stretch stimulus and the stretch reflex facilitate contraction.
  9. Timing: normal timing from distal to proximal; timing for emphasis to strengthen a weak part.
  10. Patterns: diagonal, spiral mass movements.

Neurophysiological principles behind them (Sherrington): after-discharge, temporal and spatial summation, irradiation, successive induction, reciprocal innervation.

Techniques

7Mental imagery, CIMT and transfer of learning

  • Mental (motor) imagery: rehearsing a movement in the mind without moving. Added to physical practice, it improves upper limb function after stroke (low-quality evidence, Cochrane 2020). Use short, guided sessions, from the person's own view, of tasks they are practising.
  • Constraint-induced movement therapy: restraint of the strong hand plus intensive, shaped practice with the weak hand; for people with some active wrist and finger extension. Criteria and doses are on the stroke page; in children it is rated effective (Novak et al., 2020).
  • Transfer of learning: practise in the setting where the task will be used (context-specific); move from part practice to whole-task practice; bilateral transfer (practice with one limb improves the other); vary practice so skills generalise.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite three principles of the Motor Relearning Programme.

Task-specific practice of real-life tasks in context; the patient as an active learner with instruction and feedback; eliminate unnecessary muscle activity and practise missing components; repetition with carry-over into daily life.

3 mWhat is a reflex-inhibiting pattern? Give an example.

A position or movement opposite to the abnormal spastic pattern, used in the Bobath approach to reduce tone. Example: for a flexed hemiplegic arm, sitting with weight borne on the arm with the shoulder externally rotated, elbow straight, forearm supinated and wrist and fingers extended.

7 mExplain the principles of PNF.

Philosophy: positive, functional, uses strong parts, whole person, motor learning. Basic principles: optimal resistance; irradiation and reinforcement; manual contact (lumbrical grip); body position and mechanics; clear verbal commands; vision; traction and approximation; stretch stimulus and reflex; normal timing and timing for emphasis; diagonal spiral patterns (D1, D2). Neurophysiological basis: after-discharge, summation, irradiation, successive induction, reciprocal innervation. Name a few techniques: rhythmic initiation, combination of isotonics, rhythmic stabilisation, contract-relax.

LAQPlan upper limb retraining for a man 3 months after stroke with some shoulder and elbow movement but poor hand control, using components from different approaches. (Practice case)

Assessment of components (reach, grasp, release, manipulation); MRP: analyse, practise missing components (reaching to targets, wrist extension with grasp, release, opposition), then whole tasks (cup, comb, writing) with feedback and many repetitions; PNF patterns with rhythmic initiation for starting movement and combination of isotonics for control; mental imagery of the same tasks; modified CIMT if he has enough wrist and finger extension; shoulder protection and positioning; home practice programme.

MCQWhich is an inhibitory Rood technique?A. Fast brushingB. Quick icingC. Slow stroking down the backD. Tapping

C. The others facilitate.

MCQIn the D1 flexion pattern of the upper limb, the shoulder moves into:A. Flexion, abduction, internal rotationB. Flexion, adduction, external rotationC. Extension, abduction, internal rotationD. Extension, adduction, external rotation

B. The hand moves towards the opposite ear.

MCQThe 2014 Cochrane review of stroke rehabilitation approaches concluded that:A. Bobath is superiorB. Brunnstrom is superiorC. No single approach is more effective; combining components worksD. Rehabilitation is not effective

C. Use evidence-based components, not one named approach.
