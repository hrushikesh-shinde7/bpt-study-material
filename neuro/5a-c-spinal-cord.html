5A(c)Spinal cord injury: level and grade, complications, ICF and rehabilitation

Spinal cord injury was a long case in 9 of the 20 papers (2016–2026), and autonomic dysreflexia alone was asked 8 times. Most cases are adults with a lesion near T10 (a fracture, tuberculosis or myelitis), asked for ICF, goals and the expected level of walking. Every answer starts from the level and grade of the injury, because they decide what the person can learn to do, what equipment they need and which complications to prevent.

My revision

1Definition

  • Spinal cord injury (SCI) is damage to the spinal cord or the cauda equina that changes its motor, sensory or autonomic function, for a time or permanently. It may be traumatic (fracture or dislocation) or non-traumatic (tuberculosis, tumour, inflammation, disc, blood-supply loss).
  • Tetraplegia: loss of function in the cervical segments, so the arms, trunk, legs and pelvic organs are affected.
  • Paraplegia: loss of function in the thoracic, lumbar or sacral segments (or the cauda equina). The arms are spared; the trunk, legs and pelvic organs are affected, depending on the level.
  • Neurological level of injury (NLI): the lowest segment where sensation and antigravity power (grade 3 or more) are both normal, with normal function above it.
  • Complete injury: no sensory or motor function in the lowest sacral segments (S4–S5). Incomplete injury: some function is preserved there ("sacral sparing").

2Classification and grading

The ISNCSCI examination (ASIA)

  • Motor: 10 key muscles on each side graded 0–5. C5 elbow flexors, C6 wrist extensors, C7 elbow extensors, C8 finger flexors (middle finger), T1 little-finger abductors; L2 hip flexors, L3 knee extensors, L4 ankle dorsiflexors, L5 long toe extensors, S1 ankle plantar flexors.
  • Sensory: 28 key points on each side (C2 to S4–S5), each tested for light touch and pinprick and scored 0 (absent), 1 (altered) or 2 (normal).
  • Anorectal examination: voluntary anal contraction (VAC) and deep anal pressure (DAP).
  • Motor level: the lowest key muscle with grade 3 or more, provided the muscles above are grade 5. Sensory level: the lowest dermatome with normal light touch and pinprick.

Sacral sparing (3 marks in S16) and complete vs incomplete (3 marks in W25)

Sacral sparing means any sensation at S4–S5 (light touch, pinprick or deep anal pressure) or voluntary anal contraction. It shows that some fibres cross the lesion, so the injury is incomplete. Sacral fibres lie on the outer edge of the long tracts, so they often survive a central lesion. No sacral sparing means a complete injury (AIS A).

India: falls cause more injuries than road accidents. In 2,716 patients in Jaipur, falls from height caused 53% and road accidents 28%; most patients were young men (71% aged 20–49) and 79% came from rural areas (Mathur et al., 2015).

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Primary injury: the force of the fracture, dislocation or compression tears and bruises the cord.
  2. Secondary injury over hours to days: swelling, bleeding, poor blood flow and chemical damage spread the lesion. Early decompression, good blood pressure and oxygen limit it.
  3. Spinal shock: reflexes below the lesion are lost or depressed at first, then return over days to weeks and become exaggerated.
  4. Below the lesion: because the cord above the sacral segments is cut off from the brain, a cord lesion gives UMN signs (spasticity, brisk reflexes, reflex bladder) once spinal shock ends. Because a cauda equina lesion damages nerve roots, it gives LMN signs (flaccid, areflexic, atonic bladder).
  5. Autonomic: because the sympathetic outflow (T1–L2) loses brain control, lesions at or above T6 cause low blood pressure, orthostatic hypotension and autonomic dysreflexia, and poor temperature control.
  6. Secondary problems: immobility and lost sensation lead to pressure injuries, DVT, contractures, osteoporosis, chest infections and pain.

The neurological level comes from the examination, not the X-ray: the roots that leave just below a fracture are often damaged too.

Why does a T10 fracture give a reflex (spastic) bladder but an L1 fracture often gives a flaccid one?

The bladder reflex centre is in the sacral cord (S2–S4), which lies at the L1 vertebra. A T10 fracture damages the cord above that centre, so the reflex arc is intact but cut off from the brain, and the bladder empties by reflex. An L1 fracture can destroy the sacral centre itself, so the reflex arc is broken and the bladder becomes atonic.

5Clinical features

Spinal shock (3 marks in S17 and W20)

  • Starts at injury: flaccid paralysis and loss of all reflexes below the lesion, including the bulbocavernosus reflex; loss of sensation.
  • Atonic bladder with retention; paralytic ileus; loss of sweating and temperature control below the lesion.
  • Four phases (Ditunno et al., 2004): reflexes absent or depressed (day 0–1); first reflexes return, starting with the bulbocavernosus reflex (days 1–3); early hyperreflexia (1–4 weeks); late hyperreflexia and spasticity (1–12 months).
  • Do not confuse it with neurogenic shock: low blood pressure and slow heart rate in injuries at or above T6, from loss of sympathetic tone.

By level

  • C1–C4: all four limbs and the trunk paralysed; the diaphragm (C3–C5) is weak or paralysed, so breathing may need a ventilator.
  • C5–C8: partial arm function by level; weak cough; orthostatic hypotension and autonomic dysreflexia; hand function decides independence.
  • T1–T12: arms normal; trunk control falls the higher the lesion; lesions at or above T6 risk autonomic dysreflexia.
  • Conus and cauda equina: LMN legs, saddle anaesthesia, areflexic bladder and bowel.

Non-traumatic conditions in the papers

  • Transverse myelitis (S17, S21): inflammation across a few segments; weakness of both legs, a sensory level and bladder loss developing over hours to days. Treated with steroids or plasma exchange. Recovery begins within weeks and varies: roughly a third each recover well, partly or poorly.
  • Pott's spine (W22): tuberculosis of the vertebrae, most often thoracic; back pain, kyphosis (a gibbus), weight loss, evening fever; paraplegia from pus, granulation tissue or bone pressing on the cord. Treated with anti-tubercular drugs for many months and a brace, with surgery when the deficit worsens or does not improve.

6Investigations

  • X-ray and CT of the spine: fracture, dislocation, stability.
  • MRI: cord compression, bleeding, swelling; disc, abscess (Pott's spine) or tumour; inflammation in transverse myelitis.
  • ISNCSCI examination within 72 hours and repeated: level and AIS grade.
  • Urodynamics and kidney ultrasound: bladder pressure, detrusor overactivity, reflux.
  • Others: Doppler for DVT; blood tests (ESR in tuberculosis); bone density scan later.

7Medical and surgical management

  • At the scene and in casualty: immobilise the spine; airway, breathing and blood pressure; transfer to a spinal unit.
  • Surgery: decompression and stabilisation (screws and rods), ideally early, so the person can sit up and start rehabilitation within days. Stable fractures may be treated with a brace.
  • Prevention: blood thinners and compression stockings for DVT; stomach protection; bowel and bladder care (catheter, then intermittent catheterisation).
  • Later: drugs for spasticity (baclofen) and nerve pain; antimuscarinic drugs for the bladder; surgery for tendon transfers in tetraplegia.
  • Non-traumatic: anti-tubercular treatment (Pott's spine); steroids or plasma exchange (transverse myelitis); radiotherapy or surgery (tumours).

Classic answer vs current practice: early care

Classic

Bed rest with postural reduction for 6–12 weeks before sitting up.

Current

Early surgical stabilisation where possible, then sitting and rehabilitation within days, following the surgeon's precautions.

Write in the exam

"After stabilisation, mobilise early within the precautions advised: chest care, turning, range of motion, then tilt table and sitting."

8Complications and prevention

Autonomic dysreflexia (7 marks in W18 and W24; 3 marks in S17, W19, S21, W21, S22, S24)

Definition: a sudden rise in systolic blood pressure of more than 20 mmHg above the person's usual level (more than 15 mmHg in children), in people with SCI at or above T6. It can cause a stroke, seizures or death, so it is an emergency.

  1. A harmful stimulus below the lesion, most often a full bladder, sends impulses up the cord.
  2. Because the large splanchnic blood vessels are controlled from T6–L2, below the lesion, a massive reflex sympathetic discharge narrows them, so the blood pressure rises sharply.
  3. The brain senses the high pressure and slows the heart through the vagus nerve and widens vessels above the lesion (flushing, headache, blocked nose).
  4. Because the calming signals cannot pass down through the lesion, the pressure stays high until the stimulus is removed.
ItemWhat to know
CausesBladder distension (the commonest: blocked or kinked catheter, missed catheterisation), bowel impaction, pressure injury, ingrown toenail, tight clothes or straps, fractures, menstruation and labour, sexual activity, procedures such as cystoscopy
Symptoms and signsSudden pounding headache; high blood pressure; slow (or fast) pulse; flushing and sweating above the lesion; goose bumps; blurred vision or spots; blocked nose; anxiety. Some people have few symptoms
Prevent it in the physiotherapy department
  • Ask about the bladder before every session; check that the catheter drains and the leg bag is not full.
  • Avoid tight straps on the tilt table and in standing frames; check skin and toenails.
  • Know the person's usual blood pressure: in tetraplegia it is often only 90–110 systolic, so 130 may already be high.
  • Stop exercise or electrical stimulation at once if a headache starts, and check blood pressure.

9PT assessment

  • History: mechanism and date, surgery and spinal precautions, bladder and bowel method, autonomic dysreflexia episodes, skin, pain, home (floor, stairs, toilet type), job, family, goals.
  • ISNCSCI examination: key muscles, key sensory points, anorectal examination; level and AIS grade.
  • Respiratory: breathing pattern, chest expansion, vital capacity, cough strength.
  • Tone and range: Modified Ashworth Scale, spasm frequency, range (hamstrings, hip flexors, ankles, shoulders).
  • Skin: pressure areas; risk score.
  • Function: bed mobility, sitting balance (long sitting and short sitting), transfers, wheelchair skills; Spinal Cord Independence Measure (SCIM III); Walking Index for SCI (WISCI II) and 10-metre walk test in walkers.
  • Others: blood pressure lying and sitting; mood; hand function in tetraplegia.

Worked example: level and grade (S16, 2 + 1 marks)

"A 21 year old male suffered from traumatic cervical spinal cord injury... bilateral muscle power: biceps 5/5, wrist extensors 5/5, triceps 4/5 and no active contraction below C7. Intact pinprick and light touch C2–T4, absent below T4. Intact anal sensation... Write his neurological level of injury. What is the patient's ASIA impairment classification?"

  1. Motor level: C5 and C6 are 5/5 and C7 (triceps) is 4/5, which is grade 3 or more with normal muscles above, so the motor level is C7.
  2. Sensory level: light touch and pinprick are normal to T4, so the sensory level is T4.
  3. Neurological level: the higher of the two, so the NLI is C7.
  4. Grade: anal sensation (deep anal pressure) is intact, so the injury is incomplete; there is no motor function below C7, so it is AIS B (sensory incomplete).

12PT management

Principles: start early within spinal precautions; prevent complications; strengthen what works; teach new ways to move; set goals from the level and grade; plan for home, work and lifelong fitness.

From the Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine outcomes guideline (2nd edition, 2026). Incomplete injuries usually do better. Walking with KAFOs costs far more energy than wheeling, so most people with thoracic paraplegia use the wheelchair for daily mobility and walk for exercise or short distances at home.

By phase

  • Acute (bed and ICU, first days to weeks): chest care (deep breathing, assisted cough, positioning); turning and pressure care; daily range of motion within the surgeon's precautions; resting splints to keep ankles at 90°; strengthening of all working muscles; DVT prevention; sitting up in stages once the spine is stable, watching blood pressure.
  • Rehabilitation (weeks to months): tilt table and wheelchair sitting; mat programme (rolling, prone on elbows, long sitting, sitting balance, push-ups); transfers (sliding board, then lifting transfers, then floor transfers); wheelchair skills; standing programme; gait training where the level allows; hand training in tetraplegia; bladder and bowel routines with the nurse.
  • Community and lifelong: fitness and sport; home and workplace changes; driving with hand controls; yearly review of skin, shoulders, bladder and equipment.

Mat and transfer skills, step by step

  1. Rolling using momentum: swing the arms across the body, with the legs crossed in the direction of the roll.
  2. Prone on elbows, then shifting weight between the elbows.
  3. Long sitting: needs a hamstring length of about 100–110° straight-leg raise; do not overstretch, because a little tightness helps sitting balance.
  4. Sitting balance: hold, then reach out, then catch and throw a ball, first with a mirror, then without.
  5. Push-ups on blocks: lift the buttocks clear using shoulder depressors, latissimus dorsi and triceps.
  6. Transfers: sliding board first, then a lifting (depression) transfer, to level and then to uneven surfaces; finally floor to wheelchair.

Ambulation with orthoses (the S22 and S26 long cases)

  • Before walking: strong shoulder depressors, latissimus dorsi and triceps; full hip extension and ankle dorsiflexion to neutral; standing tolerance in a frame without dizziness; good skin.
  • Orthoses: bilateral KAFOs with drop-lock knees and the ankles set in slight dorsiflexion; for higher thoracic levels, a hip-knee-ankle-foot orthosis or a reciprocating gait orthosis (RGO).
  • In the parallel bars: putting on the orthoses; sit-to-stand with the knees locked; standing balance with the hips pushed forward (the body hangs on the Y-ligaments) and the shoulders behind the hips; push-ups; swing-to gait, then swing-through; turning.
  • Progress: walker, then elbow crutches; four-point gait with an RGO; sitting down, falling safely and getting up from the floor; kerbs; stairs backwards with a rail and a crutch.
  • Mode: household or exercise walking with KAFOs at T9–T12; the wheelchair stays the main way to get about.

Ambulation after an L3 vertebral fracture (7 marks in S18)

An L3 fracture lies below the end of the cord, so it damages the cauda equina: an LMN, often incomplete and uneven, weakness of the legs. Strengthen the hip extensors, abductors and quadriceps; train standing balance; fit AFOs for weak ankles (KAFOs only if the quadriceps are weak); walk with elbow crutches using a four-point, then two-point gait, then canes; practise stairs, kerbs and slopes. Expect community walking. Protect the insensate feet, and follow the brace and lifting precautions.

Tetraplegia: what is different

  • Breathing: breathing exercises; assisted cough (hands push in and up under the ribs as the person coughs); abdominal binder when sitting; inspiratory muscle training.
  • Tenodesis grasp (C6–C7): wrist extension makes the fingers close. Stretch the finger flexors only with the wrist flexed, and never stretch the fingers and wrist into extension together.
  • Upper limb: strengthen every working muscle; splints to hold the wrist and thumb; practise feeding and writing with the occupational therapist.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Follow the surgeon's spinal precautions: brace, log rolling, limits on bending, twisting and lifting.
  • Stop and check blood pressure if a headache, flushing or sweating starts (autonomic dysreflexia) or if dizziness starts (orthostatic hypotension).
  • No heat packs or ice on insensate skin; check skin under orthoses and splints.
  • Handle osteoporotic limbs gently; no forceful stretching, especially with heterotopic ossification.
  • Protect the tenodesis grasp in tetraplegia and the shoulders in all wheelchair users.
  • No leg exercise or massage if a DVT is suspected until it is treated.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Pressure relief, skin checks with a mirror, cushion care and nutrition.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia: signs, first steps and a wallet card (lesions at or above T6).
  • Bladder and bowel routine; enough fluids; signs of urine infection.
  • Home: ramps, wide doors, a western toilet or commode chair, a bathroom with a shower chair, ground-floor living or a lift.
  • Daily stretching, standing and the exercise doses above; safe transfers; wheelchair maintenance.
  • Work, driving with hand controls, sport and sexuality; disability certificate and benefits; peer support.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Most motor recovery happens in the first 6–9 months, fastest in the first 3 months.
  • Most injuries graded AIS A stay AIS A; about 20–30% improve, half of them to AIS B and half to AIS C or D. Conversion is more likely in tetraplegia than paraplegia.
  • Walking is more likely with a lower AIS grade (D best), younger age, stronger quadriceps (L3) and calf muscles (S1), and preserved pinprick sensation in the legs and sacral area.
  • Discharge from inpatient rehabilitation when the person reaches the goals expected for the level, can direct their own care, and has the equipment, home changes and follow-up in place.

Answer: type of bladder in the S26 case

A T9–T10 fracture damages the cord above the sacral micturition centre (S2–S4, at the L1 vertebra), so the reflex arc is intact but cut off from the brain: a reflex (UMN, spastic) neurogenic bladder. It empties by reflex when stimulated, often against a sphincter that does not relax (detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia). His sense of fullness suggests that some sensory fibres are spared. Manage it with clean intermittent catheterisation 4–6 times a day and drugs to calm the bladder; avoid tapping and pressing to empty it unless urodynamics show safe pressures. Full detail is on the bladder and bowel page.

3 mWrite the clinical features of Brown-Séquard syndrome.

Hemisection of the cord. Same side: UMN weakness and loss of position sense and vibration below the lesion; a band of sensory loss at the level. Opposite side: loss of pain and temperature from 1–2 segments below the lesion. Best outlook of the cord syndromes.

3 mWhat is spinal shock? Write its features.

Temporary loss or depression of all reflexes below the lesion after SCI. Features: flaccid paralysis, areflexia including the bulbocavernosus reflex, sensory loss, urinary retention, paralytic ileus. Reflexes return over days to weeks (the bulbocavernosus first) and then become exaggerated.

7 mExplain autonomic dysreflexia: causes, symptoms and intervention.

Definition: systolic BP more than 20 mmHg above the usual level in SCI at or above T6. Mechanism: a stimulus below the lesion triggers a reflex sympathetic discharge that narrows splanchnic vessels; the brain cannot send calming signals down. Causes: full bladder (commonest), bowel impaction, pressure injury, ingrown toenail, tight clothing. Symptoms: pounding headache, flushing and sweating above the lesion, blocked nose, blurred vision, slow pulse. Intervention: sit up and lower the legs; loosen clothing; BP every 1–2 minutes; check the bladder; drug if systolic stays 150 or more; check the bowel; other triggers; monitor 2 hours; educate with a wallet card.

LAQA 25-year-old labourer fell from the second floor and had a T10 vertebral fracture a month ago; he cannot move both legs. Discuss the ICF with clinical reasoning, and short- and long-term goals with physiotherapy. (5 + 10, S25)

ICF (5): health condition: traumatic paraplegia after T10 fracture (examine for level and grade). Impairments: paralysis and sensory loss of both legs, spasticity developing, neurogenic bladder and bowel, risks of pressure injury and DVT. Activities: cannot stand or walk; bed mobility, transfers and self-care to be learnt. Participation: cannot do manual labour; family income lost. Environment: likely a poor family, stairs, Indian toilet, rough roads (barriers); free government schemes and family (facilitators). Personal: young man, manual worker.

Goals and physiotherapy (10): short-term: chest care, pressure relief and skin checks, full range, strong arms and shoulders, sitting balance, independent transfers and wheelchair skills. Long-term: independent in a wheelchair at home and outside; standing and household walking with KAFOs and crutches; bladder and bowel routine; a new seated job (training, disability schemes). Management by phase as in section 12, with low-cost equipment and a home programme.
