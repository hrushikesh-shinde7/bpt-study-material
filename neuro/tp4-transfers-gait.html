TP4Transfers, functional re-education, posture and gait training

Transfers and gait training are part of nearly every long-case management answer, and named gaits (hemiplegic, parkinsonian, ataxic, high-stepping) appear as 7-mark questions on the condition pages. This page gives the shared method: break the task into parts, practise the whole task many times at a challenging level, and measure progress with walking speed and distance.

My revision

1Bed mobility and transfers

  • Rolling: head and arm lead, knees bent; in hemiplegia practise rolling to both sides (towards the weak side is easier; towards the strong side trains the weak side).
  • Supine to sitting: roll to side lying, legs over the edge, push up through the arm; reverse to lie down.
  • Sit to stand: slide forward, feet back under the knees, lean the trunk forward ("nose over toes"), push up through both legs, stand tall; sit down by leaning forward and bending the hips and knees with control. Raise the seat height to make it easier, lower it to progress.
  • Transfers: stand-pivot (in hemiplegia usually towards the stronger side, guarding the weak knee); sliding board or depression (lift) transfers in paraplegia; car, toilet and bath transfers; getting up from the floor after a fall.
  • Teach families safe assistance and the equipment that replaces lifting (see lifting and wheelchairs).

2Functional re-education and posture

  • Task practice: sitting balance and reaching, standing up and sitting down, standing balance, walking, and reaching and manipulation, practised as whole tasks in real settings (see motor relearning).
  • Part practice only for components the patient cannot yet do (for example weight shift onto the weak leg), then back to the whole task.
  • Repetition and intensity: many repetitions, progressively harder (less support, faster, varied surfaces, dual tasks).
  • Posture: sitting with the pelvis upright and weight even on both buttocks; standing with weight even on both feet; correct the habitual posture of the condition (flexed in Parkinson's disease, asymmetrical after stroke) with feedback (mirror, verbal cues) and strengthening of the extensors and trunk.

3The normal gait cycle

  • Tasks of gait: weight acceptance (initial contact, loading response), single-limb support (midstance, terminal stance), limb advancement (pre-swing and the swing phases).
  • Measures: walking speed (10-metre walk test), endurance (6-minute walk test), step and stride length, cadence, symmetry.
  • Walking speed after stroke predicts walking in the community: below about 0.4 m/s household walking, 0.4–0.8 m/s limited community walking, above about 0.8 m/s community walking (Perry et al., 1995).

5Principles of gait training

  • Walking practice improves walking: in chronic stroke, incomplete spinal cord injury and brain injury, walking training at moderate to high intensity (or with added feedback such as virtual reality) improves walking speed and distance (Hornby et al., 2020).
  • Methods: overground walking with variety (turns, slopes, obstacles, crowds), treadmill training with or without body-weight support, stepping practice, dual-task walking, cueing in Parkinson's disease.
  • Prerequisites to train alongside: standing balance, weight shift onto each leg, strength (hip extensors, quadriceps, plantarflexors, dorsiflexors), range (hip extension, ankle dorsiflexion), endurance.
  • Orthoses and aids: an AFO for foot drop (see orthoses); the least aid that keeps walking safe.
  • Progression: parallel bars, then walker, then crutches or stick, then no aid; level floor, then slopes, stairs and outdoors; shorter to longer distances; supervised to independent.
  • Measure walking speed and distance at the start and at reviews.

Walking aids and crutch gaits

  • Aid height: handgrip at the wrist crease (or greater trochanter) with about 20–30° of elbow bend; stick in the hand opposite the weak leg.
  • Four-point: one crutch, the opposite foot, the other crutch, the other foot; slow and most stable.
  • Two-point: a crutch and the opposite foot together, then the other pair; faster, needs better balance.
  • Three-point: both crutches with the affected leg, then the good leg (non- or partial weight bearing).
  • Swing-to and swing-through: both crutches forward, then both legs swung up to or past them (paraplegia with KAFOs).
  • Stairs: going up, the stronger leg leads; going down, the weaker leg and the stick or crutch lead ("up with the good, down with the bad").

Answer skeleton: gait training in a long case

  1. Gait analysis: deviations in each phase and their causes.
  2. Prerequisites: balance, weight shift, strength, range.
  3. Walking practice with intensity and variety; treadmill if available; orthosis and aid.
  4. Progression (aids, surfaces, distance, stairs) and outcome measures (speed, distance).

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite the phases of the gait cycle.

Stance (about 60%): initial contact and loading response, midstance, terminal stance, pre-swing. Swing (about 40%): initial swing, mid-swing, terminal swing. Two double-support periods of about 10% each.

3 mDescribe crutch gaits.

Four-point (crutch, opposite foot, other crutch, other foot: most stable); two-point (crutch with opposite foot together: faster); three-point (both crutches with the affected leg, then the good leg: for non- or partial weight bearing); swing-to and swing-through (both crutches, then both legs: paraplegia with KAFOs).

7 mDescribe the principles of gait training in a neurological patient.

Gait analysis to find the deviations and their causes; train prerequisites (balance, weight shift, strength, range); task-specific walking practice with many repetitions at moderate to high intensity, overground with variety and on a treadmill with or without body-weight support; feedback and cues; an orthosis for foot drop and the least aid needed; progression of aids, surfaces, distance, stairs and outdoor walking; measure walking speed and distance; home walking programme and falls safety.

LAQPractice: a 58-year-old woman 2 months after a right MCA stroke stands with support and walks 10 m in parallel bars with her left knee snapping back. Plan transfers and gait training. (5 + 10)

Transfers (5): rolling both ways, supine to sit, sit to stand with feet back and trunk forward (raised seat, then lower), stand-pivot towards the right, toilet and car transfers; teach her daughter safe assistance. Gait (10): weight shift onto the left leg, step-ups and knee control in small ranges (0–15°), quadriceps and hip extensor strengthening, calf stretching; AFO if the ankle drops into plantarflexion and drives the knee back; walking practice in bars, then with a quad stick, increasing distance and speed; treadmill practice; turns and obstacles; stairs (up with the right, down with the left); measure 10-metre walk speed weekly.
