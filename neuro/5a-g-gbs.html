5A(g)Guillain-Barré syndrome: ICU to recovery, ICF and physiotherapy

Guillain-Barré syndrome was a long case in 9 of the 20 papers (2016–2026), and most of those patients were in the ICU or had just left it. The examiner wants three things: watch the breathing, prevent the complications of lying still, and rebuild strength without overworking muscles whose nerves are still recovering.

My revision

1Definition

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute, immune-mediated disease of the peripheral nerves and nerve roots (a polyradiculoneuropathy). It causes rapidly progressive, usually symmetrical weakness of the limbs with absent or reduced tendon reflexes, reaching its worst within 4 weeks.

  • How common: about 1–2 per 100,000 people a year, at any age.
  • A trigger: about two-thirds have an infection in the 6 weeks before, often diarrhoea or a chest infection.
  • It is a lower motor neuron (LMN) disease: the brain and cord are normal.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Infections linked to GBS: Campylobacter jejuni (diarrhoea), cytomegalovirus, hepatitis E, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Epstein–Barr virus and Zika virus.
  • Other triggers reported: influenza and other viral illnesses, surgery, and rarely vaccines.
  • Mechanism: the immune response to the infection cross-reacts with nerve molecules (molecular mimicry), so antibodies and immune cells attack the myelin or the axon.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. An immune attack on myelin (AIDP) slows or blocks conduction; an attack on the axon (AMAN) stops it.
  2. Because the motor nerves fail, muscles become weak and flaccid with lost reflexes, usually starting in the legs and rising (ascending paralysis).
  3. Because the phrenic and intercostal nerves can be involved, breathing muscles weaken: up to 22% need a ventilator in the first week.
  4. Because cranial nerves can be involved, facial weakness and swallowing problems appear.
  5. Because autonomic nerves are affected, blood pressure and heart rate swing and arrhythmias can occur.
  6. Because sensory nerves are affected, there is tingling, loss of position sense and nerve pain.
  7. Recovery comes from remyelination (weeks) and from axonal regrowth (months), so demyelinating forms recover faster than axonal ones.

5Clinical features

  • Weakness: rapidly progressive, symmetrical, flaccid; usually starts in the legs and ascends to the arms, trunk, breathing and face. Proximal muscles are often as weak as distal ones.
  • Reflexes: absent or reduced in the weak limbs.
  • Sensory: tingling in the hands and feet; loss of position sense; often pain in the back and legs.
  • Cranial nerves: bilateral facial weakness; swallowing and speech problems.
  • Breathing: breathlessness, weak cough, rapid shallow breathing, use of accessory muscles.
  • Autonomic: swings in blood pressure and heart rate, arrhythmias, sweating, urinary retention, constipation.
  • Fatigue is very common and can persist after strength returns.

Diagnosis

  • Required: progressive weakness of both arms and legs (it may start in the legs only), and absent or reduced tendon reflexes in the weak limbs.
  • Strongly support it: progression over days to 4 weeks (usually under 2); relative symmetry; mild sensory signs; cranial nerve involvement, especially bilateral facial weakness; autonomic dysfunction; pain.
  • Make you doubt it: a sensory level, early bladder or bowel loss, clear asymmetry, or more than 50 cells in the CSF; these point to a cord lesion or infection.

6Investigations

  • CSF (lumbar puncture): raised protein with a normal cell count (albuminocytological dissociation). Protein is normal in 30–50% in the first week, so a normal result does not rule GBS out.
  • Nerve conduction studies and EMG (3 marks in W17): see the table.
  • Breathing tests: vital capacity, maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressure, single breath count, arterial blood gases.
  • Others: ECG; stool tests for Campylobacter; antibodies (anti-GQ1b in Miller Fisher syndrome); MRI of the spine if a cord lesion is possible.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Immunotherapy (EAN/PNS 2023) for people who cannot walk unaided: IVIg 0.4 g/kg a day for 5 days, within 2 weeks of onset (up to 4 weeks as good practice); or plasma exchange 12–15 L over 4–5 exchanges within 4 weeks. Both work equally well; plasma exchange followed straight away by IVIg is not advised.
  • Not advised: corticosteroids (oral steroids are recommended against); a second IVIg course.
  • ICU care: admit if breathing is failing, swallowing is unsafe or the autonomic system is unstable. Intubate early when the 20/30/40 rule is met: vital capacity under 20 mL/kg, maximum inspiratory pressure under 30 cmH2O, or maximum expiratory pressure under 40 cmH2O. A single breath count of 19 or less predicts the need for ventilation. Tracheostomy if ventilation is prolonged.
  • Supportive: DVT prophylaxis; monitoring of ECG, heart rate, blood pressure, bladder and bowel; nutrition (tube feeding if swallowing is unsafe); eye care in facial weakness; medicines for pain (gabapentinoids, tricyclic antidepressants or carbamazepine).

9PT assessment

  • History: preceding infection, date of onset, speed of progression, immunotherapy given, ventilation, swallowing, pain, job, home and family.
  • Vital signs and monitors: heart rate and rhythm, blood pressure (lying and sitting), oxygen saturation, ventilator settings.
  • Respiratory: vital capacity, single breath count, cough strength, chest expansion, auscultation, secretions.
  • Motor: manual muscle testing; MRC sum score (6 muscle groups on each side, total 0–60); reflexes; facial and bulbar muscles.
  • Sensory: light touch, position sense; pain score.
  • Range of motion and skin.
  • Function: GBS disability scale; bed mobility, sitting balance, transfers, standing, walking (10-metre walk, 6-minute walk later); hand function.
  • Fatigue: Fatigue Severity Scale.

12PT management

Principles: watch the breathing and the autonomic system; prevent the complications of immobility; exercise within the person's strength; never overwork recovering muscles; plan the return to home and work.

By phase

  • Progression and plateau (ICU, 7 marks in W21):
    • Chest: positioning (side lying, upright when allowed), deep breathing and incentive spirometry if the person can, airway clearance and suctioning, assisted cough; measure vital capacity or single breath count and report a fall.
    • Ventilated patients: positioning, secretion clearance as per the unit protocol, support during weaning.
    • Limbs: neutral positioning; resting splints for the wrist and hand and an AFO or foot support; gentle passive range of motion once or twice a day; pad the elbows and fibular heads; turn regularly.
    • Pain: gentle handling and positioning; explain everything before touching.
    • Start sitting up and the tilt table as soon as breathing and blood pressure are stable, watching for postural hypotension.
  • Early recovery (ward): active-assisted, then active exercise, first with gravity eliminated; sitting balance; rolling, bridging, sit-to-stand; standing in the parallel bars; short sessions with rests.
  • Recovery and rehabilitation: progressive strengthening; gait training (parallel bars, walker, then a stick), with an AFO for foot drop; balance training with vision when position sense is poor; hand function with the occupational therapist; endurance training; fatigue management (pacing, planning the day); return to school, work and sport.
Avoid overwork

Supervised exercise improves fitness and independence, but its intensity must be watched closely, because overwork causes fatigue (Leonhard et al., 2019). If weakness or tiredness is worse the next day, the session was too hard: shorten it or lower the load.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Stop and report if breathing worsens: vital capacity or single breath count falling, rising respiratory rate, a weaker cough.
  • Watch heart rate, rhythm and blood pressure during turning, suctioning and sitting up; tilt back if blood pressure drops.
  • Do not overstretch flaccid joints; support the shoulders.
  • Avoid fatigue: short sessions, rests, and less work if the person is weaker the next day.
  • Upright positioning for meals if swallowing is weak; protect the eyes if the face is weak.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Explain that GBS is not contagious, that most people recover, and that recovery is slow.
  • Teach the family positioning, range of motion and safe transfers.
  • A home exercise programme that builds slowly; pacing and energy conservation for fatigue.
  • Return to school or work in stages; hand controls or an AFO if needed.
  • Seek help at once if weakness returns: treatment-related fluctuations happen in 6–10% and recurrence in 2–5%.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • About 80% walk independently 6 months after onset. Recovery is fastest in the first year but may continue for more than 5 years.
  • Death occurs in 3–10%, even with the best care, mostly in older people with severe disease.
  • Older age, diarrhoea before the illness and severe weakness are linked to a poorer outcome; the modified Erasmus GBS outcome score (mEGOS) estimates the chance of walking.
  • Severe pain still affects at least a third at 1 year, and fatigue affects 60–80%.
  • Worsening 8 weeks or more after onset, or three or more relapses after treatment, suggests acute-onset CIDP rather than GBS.
  • Discharge when the person is safe at home, independent or with planned help, with a home programme and follow-up.

Answer: diagnosis with rationale in the S18 case

Guillain-Barré syndrome, probably a pure motor (axonal) form. Rationale: a gut infection 3 weeks before; acute weakness of all four limbs over 2 days; symmetrical, with proximal muscles as weak as distal ones; sensation normal; delayed F-waves, which show early nerve root involvement. Rule out myasthenia gravis (fatigable weakness, eye signs, normal F-waves), an acute cord lesion (sensory level, bladder loss, later spasticity), hypokalaemic paralysis (low potassium) and polymyositis (raised muscle enzymes, normal nerve conduction).

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mDescribe the EMG and NCV picture in GBS.

Demyelinating form: slowed conduction velocity, prolonged distal latency, prolonged or absent F-waves (often the earliest sign), conduction block and temporal dispersion, abnormal arm sensory responses with a normal sural response. Axonal form: low motor amplitudes with near-normal velocity; denervation on EMG after 2–3 weeks.

3 mList three features that support a diagnosis of GBS.

Progression over days to 4 weeks (usually under 2); relative symmetry; bilateral facial weakness; autonomic dysfunction; raised CSF protein with a normal cell count; nerve conduction showing demyelination or axonal loss.

7 mPhysiotherapy in acute-stage GBS.

Assess breathing (vital capacity, single breath count, cough) and report a fall; chest care: positioning, deep breathing, incentive spirometry, airway clearance, suctioning, assisted cough; monitor heart rate and blood pressure during turning and suctioning. Neutral positioning, regular turning, padding of elbows and knees; resting splints; gentle passive range once or twice a day. DVT prevention with leg movements. Pain relief by positioning and gentle handling. Sitting up and tilt table once stable. Explain and reassure; teach the family.

LAQA 10-year-old boy is out of the ICU for 2 days after GBS; legs grade 2, arms grade 3, hands grade 2, weak breathing muscles. He needs to ride a bicycle to school. Write short- and long-term goals and the management. (5 + 10, W25)

Goals (5): short-term (2–4 weeks): clear chest and stronger cough; sits unsupported; stands with support; grips a spoon and pencil; no contractures. Long-term (3–6 months): walks independently; climbs stairs; writes in class; rides his bicycle to school; full school day without exhaustion.

Management (10): breathing exercises with incentive spirometry and games (blowing bubbles); positioning and range of motion; graded strengthening through play, few repetitions with rests; sitting balance, sit-to-stand, standing frame, gait training with AFOs if foot drop; hand activities (putty, building blocks, writing); balance and coordination; static cycling before road cycling; fatigue pacing at school; teach parents a home programme; watch for overwork.

MCQWhich finding means a patient with GBS needs ventilation?A. Vital capacity 35 mL/kgB. Vital capacity under 20 mL/kgC. Single breath count of 30D. Absent ankle reflexes

B. The 20/30/40 rule: vital capacity under 20 mL/kg, inspiratory pressure under 30 or expiratory pressure under 40 cmH2O; a single breath count of 19 or less also predicts ventilation.

MCQThe classic CSF finding in GBS is:A. High cells, normal proteinB. High protein, normal cellsC. Low glucoseD. Blood-stained fluid

B. Albuminocytological dissociation; it may be absent in the first week.

MCQWhich treatment is not recommended for GBS?A. IVIgB. Plasma exchangeC. Oral corticosteroidsD. DVT prophylaxis

C. The 2023 EAN/PNS guideline recommends against oral corticosteroids.
