3Neuroplasticity, recovery, motor learning and skill acquisition

This chapter came up in 9 papers, almost all as 3-mark questions: define neuroplasticity, brain changes and synaptogenesis, the stages of motor learning (three times), factors that help motor learning, and intrinsic vs extrinsic feedback, plus a 7-mark question on feedback. One idea links them: the brain changes with what the person practises, so therapy must give enough meaningful, repeated, challenging practice with the right feedback.

My revision

1Neuroplasticity

Asked for 3 marks in S16 (define), S18 (brain changes) and S26 (neuroplasticity and synaptogenesis).

  • Neuroplasticity is the ability of the nervous system to change its structure and function in response to experience, learning, training or injury.
  • It happens in the intact brain (learning a skill) and after a lesion (recovery and reorganisation). It can be adaptive (helps function) or maladaptive (for example spasticity, pain, or a habit of not using the weak arm).

Brain changes in neuroplasticity (S18)

LevelChange
SynapseStronger or weaker transmission at existing synapses (long-term potentiation and depression); unmasking of silent synapses
NeuronSynaptogenesis (new synapses); new dendritic branches and spines; axonal sprouting from intact neurons into areas that lost their input
White matterChanges in myelination with practice
Cortical mapsThe area representing a trained part enlarges; areas next to a lesion take over lost functions; the other hemisphere may be recruited

Synaptogenesis (S26): the formation of new synapses between neurons. It is fastest in early development, continues with learning throughout life, and after injury intact neurons sprout and form new synapses (reactive synaptogenesis), which therapy can shape.

Principles of experience-dependent plasticity (Kleim and Jones, 2008)

PrincipleMeaning for therapy
Use it or lose itFunctions not used decline
Use it and improve itTraining a function strengthens it
SpecificityThe skill trained is the skill that changes
Repetition mattersLasting change needs many repetitions
Intensity mattersTraining must be challenging enough
Time mattersDifferent changes happen at different times after injury
Salience mattersPractice must be meaningful to the person
Age mattersPlasticity is greater in the young brain
TransferenceTraining one skill can help related skills
InterferenceLearning one pattern (a compensation) can block learning another

2Recovery after a brain lesion

  • Spontaneous (early) recovery: swelling settles, blood flow returns to the penumbra around the lesion, and distant areas that were "switched off" by the lesion (diaschisis) start working again. Most of this happens in the first weeks to months.
  • Experience-dependent recovery: reorganisation driven by practice, continuing for months to years.
  • Recovery vs compensation (Levin et al., 2009): recovery means doing the movement in the same way as before the injury; compensation means doing the task in a new way (for example leaning the trunk to reach). Compensation gets tasks done but can block true recovery.
  • Learned non-use: after early failed attempts, the person stops using the weak limb; the brain area shrinks. CIMT reverses this.
  • Environment and behaviour: an active, stimulating environment with chances to practise (an "enriched" environment) supports recovery; long periods of inactivity on a ward slow it.

Muscle adaptation after neurological injury

  • Loss of motor units and weakness; muscle fibres become smaller and fibre types change.
  • Muscles kept in a shortened position lose sarcomeres and become stiffer, so contractures form.
  • Changes in tendons and connective tissue add non-neural stiffness to the reflex part of tone.
  • So treat muscle length and strength as well as the nervous system.

3Strength training and physical conditioning

  • Strength training after stroke increases strength and improves activity, and does not increase spasticity (Ada et al., 2006).
  • Use progressive resistance, functional strengthening (sit-to-stand, step-ups) and power training, with enough load to fatigue the muscle in a set.
  • Aerobic conditioning improves fitness, walking and health in most neurological conditions; doses are on each condition page: stroke, SCI, MS, Parkinson's disease, CP.
  • Watch blood pressure, fatigue, heat (MS), autonomic dysreflexia (SCI) and overwork (GBS, DMD).

Classic answer vs current evidence: strengthening spastic muscles

Classic

Avoid resisted exercise in spasticity because effort increases tone and abnormal patterns.

Current

Strength training improves strength and activity without increasing spasticity; weakness, not spasticity, limits most function.

Write in the exam

"Progressive strength and functional training; it does not increase spasticity."

4Motor learning

  • Motor learning is a set of processes, linked to practice or experience, that leads to relatively permanent changes in the capability for skilled movement.
  • Performance vs learning: performance is what you see during practice; learning is shown by retention (doing it later) and transfer (doing it in a new setting).

Stages of motor learning (3 marks in S21, W24 and W25)

Stage (Fitts and Posner)What the learner is likeWhat the therapist does
CognitiveWorks out what to do; many errors; slow, variable, needs full attentionClear instructions, demonstration, frequent feedback, a quiet setting, some guidance
AssociativeRefines how to do it; fewer errors; more consistentLess feedback, more practice, start to vary the task
AutonomousAutomatic; little attention needed; can do two tasks at onceVaried, real-world practice; dual tasks; little feedback

Other models: Gentile (getting the idea of the movement, then fixation or diversification); Bernstein (freezing the degrees of freedom, releasing them, then exploiting them).

Factors that help motor learning (3 marks in W17)

  • Practice: enough repetitions; distributed rather than massed; variable and random order for retention and transfer; whole-task practice where possible.
  • Feedback: right type and amount, reduced as learning progresses.
  • Attention and motivation: meaningful goals; an external focus (on the effect of the movement, for example "reach the cup") rather than an internal focus on the body part.
  • Task-specific and context-specific practice in a setting like real life.
  • Demonstration and mental practice; good sleep and rest.

5Skill acquisition: instruction, demonstration, feedback and practice

Feedback (3 marks in S22; 7 marks in S25)

TypeMeaningExamples
IntrinsicFrom the person's own senses during and after movementFeeling the foot hit the ground; seeing the cup move; balance from the inner ear
Extrinsic (augmented)Added from outside to supplement intrinsic feedbackTherapist's words, a mirror, video, EMG biofeedback, force platforms, virtual reality
Knowledge of results (KR)Extrinsic feedback about the outcome"You walked 10 metres in 12 seconds"
Knowledge of performance (KP)Extrinsic feedback about the movement pattern"Your knee locked back in stance"
  • Timing: concurrent (during) or terminal (after); immediate or delayed.
  • Schedule: frequent feedback helps the early stage, but too much creates dependence; fade it, give summary feedback after several trials, or give it only when errors exceed a set band.
  • Role in neuro-rehabilitation (S25): gives information to correct errors, motivates, reinforces correct movement, and replaces lost intrinsic feedback (for example visual feedback for poor proprioception, EMG biofeedback for weak muscles).

Instruction, demonstration and practice

  • Instructions: short, one or two key points, with an external focus.
  • Demonstration: show the whole task first; observational learning activates motor areas.
  • Practice: blocked practice helps early performance; random and variable practice gives better retention and transfer (contextual interference). Part practice suits complex tasks with separable parts; whole practice suits continuous tasks like walking.
  • Mental practice adds to physical practice.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mDefine neuroplasticity.

The ability of the nervous system to change its structure and function in response to experience, learning, training or injury: through changes in synaptic strength, new synapses, sprouting and cortical reorganisation.

3 mWrite the three stages of motor learning.

Cognitive (understanding the task; many errors, needs attention and frequent feedback); associative (refining the movement; fewer errors); autonomous (automatic, consistent, can dual-task) (Fitts and Posner).

7 mExplain the role and types of feedback in neuro-rehabilitation.

Feedback is information about movement. Intrinsic (own senses) and extrinsic (therapist, mirror, video, biofeedback). Extrinsic is KR (outcome) or KP (pattern); concurrent or terminal; immediate or delayed; summary, faded or bandwidth schedules. Role: corrects errors, motivates, reinforces, substitutes for lost sensation. Give more early and fade it to avoid dependence. Examples: EMG biofeedback for a weak dorsiflexor; mirror for posture; KR on walking speed.

LAQUsing motor learning principles, plan training of sit-to-stand for a patient 6 weeks after stroke. (Practice question)

Analyse the task and missing components; give a short instruction with an external focus ("bring your nose over your toes and push to stand") and a demonstration; practise the whole task with many repetitions per session (blocked at first, then varied: different chair heights, speeds, with and without arms); give KP early (trunk lean, weight on the weak leg), then fade to summary KR (number of stands in 30 seconds); add a home practice schedule; progress to dual tasks and real settings.

MCQ"Your heel touched first this time" is an example of:A. Intrinsic feedbackB. Knowledge of resultsC. Knowledge of performanceD. Concurrent visual feedback

C. It describes the movement pattern.

MCQWhich practice schedule gives the best retention and transfer?A. Blocked, constant practiceB. Random, variable practiceC. Watching onlyD. Practice with feedback after every trial

B. Contextual interference improves learning, though early performance looks worse.

MCQThe principle "the skill trained is the skill that changes" is:A. TransferenceB. SpecificityC. SalienceD. Interference

B. Kleim and Jones (2008).
