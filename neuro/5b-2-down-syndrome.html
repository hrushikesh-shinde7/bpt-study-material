5B(2)Down's syndrome: physical traits, neurological impairments and physiotherapy

Down's syndrome was a 7-mark question three times: physiotherapy twice (S20, W25) and the physical traits and neurological impairments once (S23). Low tone, lax joints and slow learning delay every motor milestone, so physiotherapy practises the skills themselves (standing, stepping, walking, balance), builds strength and fitness, and keeps the neck and heart safe.

My revision

1Definition

  • Down's syndrome is a genetic condition caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21). It causes typical physical features, intellectual disability, low muscle tone, delayed development and a higher risk of several medical problems.
  • It is the commonest chromosomal cause of intellectual disability.

2Classification and grading

Genetic types (AAP, 2022)
TypeSharePoint to know
Non-disjunction (free trisomy 21)About 96%Chromosomes fail to separate, usually in the egg; risk rises with maternal age
Translocation3–4%Extra 21 attached to another chromosome; a parent may carry it, so the recurrence risk is higher
Mosaicism1–2%Only some cells are affected; often milder

Intellectual disability is usually moderate (IQ 35–50), sometimes mild (50–70) and occasionally severe (20–35).

3Causes and risk factors

  • Older maternal age (risk rises steeply after 35); a previous child with Down's syndrome; a parent carrying a balanced translocation.
  • India: about 1 in 1000 live births (estimates range from about 1 in 800 to 1 in 1150).
  • Screening: first- and second-trimester blood tests with nuchal translucency on ultrasound, or cell-free DNA testing; diagnosis by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. The extra chromosome 21 changes brain development: the brain, and especially the cerebellum and brainstem, is smaller, with fewer neurons and connections. This gives low tone, poor postural control, slow reactions and slow motor learning.
  2. Collagen differences make ligaments lax: hypermobile joints, flat feet, unstable patellae and hips, and a risk of atlantoaxial instability.
  3. Low tone, lax joints and weak muscles together delay every motor milestone, and children often widen their base and lock their joints for stability.
  4. Heart defects, sleep apnoea and frequent chest infections reduce exercise tolerance; hearing and vision problems make learning and balance harder.
  5. Low activity and a low metabolic rate lead to obesity, which further limits movement.

5Clinical features

Physical traits (S23)

  • Head and face: small, flat-backed (brachycephalic) head; flat facial profile and nasal bridge; upward-slanting eyes with epicanthic folds; white spots on the iris (Brushfield spots); small mouth with a protruding tongue; small, low-set ears; excess skin at the back of the neck.
  • Hands: short, broad hands; a single palmar crease; a short, curved little finger (clinodactyly).
  • Feet: a wide gap between the first and second toes with a deep groove on the sole; flat feet.
  • Body: short stature, low tone, hypermobile joints.

Neurological impairments (S23)

  • Hypotonia (low tone) and reduced muscle strength.
  • Intellectual disability, usually moderate; speech and language delay.
  • Delayed motor milestones: on average sitting at about 10 months, crawling at about 14 months and walking at about 26 months; the slowest children walk at about 40 months (Winders et al., 2019).
  • Poor balance and postural control, slow reaction and movement times, difficulty with motor planning and sequencing.
  • Gait: wide base, out-turned feet, knees locked in extension, little heel strike.
  • Hearing loss (75%) and vision problems (60–80%); seizures (1–13%); autism in some (7–19%); early Alzheimer's disease in adults.

6Investigations

  • Karyotype to confirm the diagnosis and type (needed for genetic counselling).
  • Echocardiogram; hearing and vision tests; thyroid tests; blood count.
  • Cervical spine X-rays in the neutral position only if there are symptoms of atlantoaxial instability; routine screening of children without symptoms is not recommended (AAP, 2022).
  • Developmental assessment: Alberta Infant Motor Scale (infants), Gross Motor Function Measure, Peabody Developmental Motor Scales.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Heart surgery in infancy when needed; treatment of thyroid, ear, eye and sleep problems.
  • Early intervention from infancy: physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech therapy, with the parents as the main therapists at home.
  • Inclusive education and support for families.
  • Surgical fixation of the upper neck for symptomatic atlantoaxial instability.

8Complications and prevention

ComplicationPhysio role
Atlantoaxial instability with cord compressionKnow the signs; avoid trampolines; advise about contact sports and gymnastics; support the neck in handling
Flat feet, knee and patellar problemsFoot orthoses (supramalleolar or insoles) when needed; strengthen around the knees
Obesity and low fitnessDaily active play, sport, aerobic and strength training
Chest infectionsActive play, airway clearance when unwell
Early ageing and dementia (adults)Keep active; falls prevention

9PT assessment

  • History: heart condition and surgery, hearing and vision, breathing and sleep, neck symptoms, milestones, family and play.
  • Gross motor milestones and quality of movement; standardised tests (Alberta Infant Motor Scale, Gross Motor Function Measure, Peabody).
  • Tone (hypotonia), joint laxity (Beighton score in older children), range of motion, strength.
  • Postural control in sitting and standing; balance; gait (base, foot position, knee locking).
  • Foot posture, hip stability, spine.
  • Endurance (breathlessness, colour, heart rate), attention, understanding and communication.

10PT diagnosis by ICF

No Down's syndrome long case was set in 2016–2026, so this is a typical case for practice.

Practice case (typical presentation)

A 2-year-old boy with Down's syndrome, whose heart defect was repaired at 6 months, sits alone and crawls on all fours but cannot pull to stand or cruise. He is floppy, with lax joints and flat feet, tires quickly, says a few words and has frequent colds. His parents want him to walk before he starts playschool.

Spot the ICF clues before you write

heart defect repaired: check his heart status and watch breathlessness. sits and crawls but cannot pull to stand: next skills are pulling to stand and cruising, then walking. floppy, lax joints, flat feet: strengthen and support, do not overstretch. tires quickly, frequent colds: short, frequent sessions. parents want him to walk before playschool: a home programme run by the parents.

12PT management

By age

  • Infancy (0–1 year): early intervention with the parents; handling in midline with trunk support; tummy time and play in prone for head and trunk control; weight bearing through the arms; rolling; supported then independent sitting; avoid long periods in the frog position (hips wide and turned out).
  • Toddler (1–3 years): pull to stand, cruising, standing balance, stepping practice; treadmill stepping with a parent supporting the child, from when the child can sit alone; squat to stand; stairs with help; foot orthoses if the feet collapse.
  • Preschool and school age: balance, running, jumping, ball skills and coordination through play; strength and endurance; swimming and other sport; motor planning with demonstration and repetition.
  • Adolescents and adults: progressive resistance training, aerobic fitness and weight control; community sport; work-related tasks.

Answer for S20 and W25: physiotherapy for a child with Down's syndrome

  1. Assess milestones, tone, laxity, balance, endurance, heart status and neck symptoms.
  2. Developmental training in the order of milestones: head and trunk control, sitting, crawling, pull to stand, cruising, walking.
  3. Treadmill training: supported stepping on a small treadmill at home, 8 minutes a day, 5 days a week, from sitting alone; infants trained this way walked about 101 days earlier (Ulrich et al., 2001).
  4. Strength and postural control: weight-bearing play, squat to stand, step-ups, ball activities; balance practice on varied surfaces.
  5. Endurance and fitness: active play, swimming, cycling, dance.
  6. Joint protection: no overstretching of lax joints; foot orthoses for flat feet when needed.
  7. Teaching style and family: short, frequent, play-based sessions with demonstration, simple words and praise; a daily home programme run by the parents; safety advice for the neck and heart.

Classic answer vs current guidance: improving tone or practising skills

Classic

Facilitate tone with tapping, joint approximation and vestibular stimulation, and wait until the child is "ready" to walk.

Current

Practise the skills themselves: supported treadmill stepping helps infants walk earlier (Ulrich et al., 2001), and strength training builds leg strength in adolescents (Shields and Taylor, 2010). Facilitation is at most an add-on.

Write in the exam

"Task-specific practice of standing, stepping and walking (including treadmill training), with strength, balance and fitness training."

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Atlantoaxial instability: stop and refer for neck pain, head tilt, new weakness or stiffness, a change in walking, brisk reflexes, or a change in bladder or bowel control. Avoid trampolines; contact sports and gymnastics carry a risk of spinal cord injury (AAP, 2022). Support the head and neck during handling.
  • Heart: know the cardiac history; stop for breathlessness, blue lips or unusual tiredness.
  • Lax joints: no forceful or end-range stretching.
  • Hearing and vision: face the child, use gestures and demonstration.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Parents are the main therapists: a short daily programme of play-based practice.
  • Tummy time in infancy; standing and stepping practice; treadmill at home if available.
  • Footwear and orthosis use; healthy diet and daily activity.
  • Choosing safe sports; warning signs of neck problems.
  • Regular health checks (heart, thyroid, hearing, vision) and inclusion at school.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Almost all children learn to walk, later than other children (on average at about 26 months).
  • Function depends on the heart and other medical problems, the degree of intellectual disability and early intervention; many adults work and live with some support.
  • Discharge from an episode when the walking or other goal is reached and the family runs the home programme; review at key stages (school, adolescence).

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mList the genetic types of Down's syndrome.

Non-disjunction (free trisomy 21, about 96%), translocation (3–4%, may be inherited from a carrier parent), and mosaicism (1–2%, often milder).

3 mWhat is atlantoaxial instability in Down's syndrome?

Excess movement between the first and second cervical vertebrae due to lax ligaments; symptomatic in 1–2%. Signs: neck pain, head tilt, weakness, change in gait, brisk reflexes, bladder or bowel change. Routine X-rays are not advised without symptoms; avoid trampolines and take care with contact sports and gymnastics.

7 mWrite down the physical traits and neurological impairments seen in Down's syndrome. (S23)

Physical traits: brachycephaly, flat face and nasal bridge, upslanting eyes, epicanthic folds, Brushfield spots, small mouth with protruding tongue, small ears, excess nuchal skin, short broad hands, single palmar crease, clinodactyly, sandal gap, flat feet, short stature, lax joints. Neurological impairments: hypotonia, intellectual disability (usually moderate), delayed milestones (sitting about 10 months, walking about 26 months on average), poor balance and postural control, slow reactions and motor planning, speech delay, hearing and vision problems, seizures in some, early dementia in adults.

LAQPractice: a 2-year-old with Down's syndrome and a repaired heart defect sits and crawls but cannot pull to stand. Write ICF with clinical reasoning, goals and physiotherapy. (5 + 3 + 7)

ICF (5): the table in section 10 with the one-line diagnosis. Goals (3): short term: pulls to stand, stands holding, cruises; long term: walks independently, squats and recovers, keeps up at playschool. Physiotherapy (7): developmental training (pull to stand, cruising, stepping), treadmill stepping 8 minutes a day 5 days a week, strength and balance through play, endurance, foot orthoses if needed, heart and neck precautions, and a daily parent-run home programme.
