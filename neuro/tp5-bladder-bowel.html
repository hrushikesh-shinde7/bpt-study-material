TP5Neurogenic bladder and bowel: types, assessment and training

A 7-mark question on the types of bladder and their physiotherapy was in 5 papers, and a 3-mark list of types in 4 more. The answer always rests on one idea: where the lesion sits (brain, cord above the sacral segments, or the sacral cord and roots) decides how the bladder behaves, and that decides how it is emptied safely.

My revision

1What it is

  • Neurogenic bladder (neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction) is abnormal storage or emptying of urine caused by a lesion of the brain, spinal cord or peripheral nerves.
  • Neurogenic bowel is loss of normal control of stool storage and emptying for the same reasons.
  • Common causes: spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, stroke, Parkinson's disease, spina bifida, cauda equina lesions, diabetic neuropathy.
  • Why it matters: a badly managed bladder causes infections, stones, autonomic dysreflexia, and in the long run kidney damage from high bladder pressure. Leaking urine also damages skin and stops people working and going out.

2How the bladder is controlled

Nerve supplyFromAction
Sympathetic (hypogastric nerve)T10–L2Relaxes the detrusor and closes the bladder neck, so the bladder stores
Parasympathetic (pelvic nerve)S2–S4Contracts the detrusor, so the bladder empties
Somatic (pudendal nerve)S2–S4Keeps the external sphincter closed under voluntary control
Pontine micturition centrePons, under cortical controlSwitches from storing to emptying and makes the sphincter relax as the detrusor contracts

Reflex bladder in 3 marks (W22)

A reflex bladder follows a complete cord lesion above the sacral segments. Because the sacral reflex arc is intact but cut off from the brain, the bladder contracts by reflex when it fills, without sensation or voluntary control. The sphincter often tightens at the same time (dyssynergia), so pressure is high and emptying incomplete. Manage it with intermittent catheterisation and drugs that calm the detrusor.

4Assessment

  • History: how the person empties now (catheter, reflex leaks, straining), leaks, urgency, infections, autonomic dysreflexia, bowel pattern, fluids, medicines.
  • Bladder diary for at least 3 days: fluids in, times and volumes of voiding or catheterisation, leaks.
  • Residual urine after voiding: bladder scanner or catheter.
  • Urodynamics (done by the urologist): bladder pressure, capacity, detrusor overactivity, dyssynergia.
  • Physio-specific: hand function and sitting balance for self-catheterisation; transfers to the toilet or commode; dressing; cognition; pelvic floor contraction in incomplete lesions, MS and stroke; skin.
  • Home: toilet type (an Indian squat toilet is not possible for most paraplegics), bathroom access, privacy, water for washing.

5Bladder management and the physiotherapist's role

By type

TypeManagementPhysio role
Uninhibited (stroke, PD, MS)Bladder training; drugs for overactive bladderTimed voiding by the clock; urge suppression; pelvic floor training; faster, safer walking and transfers to the toilet; a bedside commode; easy clothing
Reflex (cord above S2)Intermittent catheterisation with antimuscarinic drugs; botulinum toxin into the detrusor if needed; a condom sheath only if urodynamics show safe pressureTeach self-catheterisation skills; balance and hand function; autonomic dysreflexia signs and first steps
Areflexic (sacral cord, cauda equina)Intermittent catheterisation; an indwelling or suprapubic catheter only if that is not possibleTeach self-catheterisation; pelvic floor training if there is any contraction; protect skin from leaks
Sensory paralyticTimed voiding; catheterise if the residual stays highVoid by the clock even without an urge
Motor paralyticIntermittent catheterisationAs for areflexic

Clean intermittent catheterisation (CIC)

  • The preferred method after SCI where possible (Canadian SCI guidance, adapted from the Canadian Urological Association, 2019).
  • How often: on average 4–6 times a day, keeping each volume under 400–500 mL (EAU 2026). Plan fluids with the team so the volumes stay in range.
  • Catheter: usually 12–16 Fr; hydrophilic catheters lower the infection rate.
  • Teach: hand washing; position (long sitting on the bed, or in the wheelchair); balance with both hands free; clothing; cleaning; recording volumes. Women may need a mirror and a leg spreader; people with C6–C7 tetraplegia need adapted aids.

Bladder training for the uninhibited bladder

  1. Timed voiding: go to the toilet at set times by the clock, before the bladder triggers; lengthen the interval as control improves.
  2. Urge suppression: when an urge comes, stop and stand or sit still; squeeze the pelvic floor quickly a few times; relax and breathe; walk calmly to the toilet when the urge fades.
  3. Pelvic floor muscle training: helps urinary symptoms and leaks in MS, stroke and incomplete SCI, though the evidence is limited (EAU 2026). Supervise it for at least 3 months, as for stress incontinence (NICE).
  4. Electrical stimulation: tibial nerve stimulation and TENS may help an overactive bladder in MS and stroke; more trials are needed.
  5. Lifestyle: steady fluids through the day, less tea and coffee late in the day, no constipation.

Classic answer vs current guidance: emptying by tapping or pressing

Classic

Spastic bladder: trigger reflex voiding by suprapubic tapping or stroking the thigh. Flaccid bladder: empty by pressing on the lower abdomen (Credé) or straining (Valsalva).

Current

These raise bladder pressure, can damage the kidneys and can set off autonomic dysreflexia. Discourage them unless urodynamics show the pressure stays safe (EAU 2026). Clean intermittent catheterisation is preferred.

Write in the exam

"Clean intermittent catheterisation 4–6 times a day, with volumes under 400–500 mL. Tapping, Credé and Valsalva only if urodynamics show safe pressures."

Recall check. List the differences between a spastic and a flaccid bladder, and how each is emptied today.

6Bowel training

TypeLesion and featuresBowel programme
Reflexic (UMN) bowelCord above the conus. The anal sphincter stays tight and the reflex works, so stool is held and constipation is commonDaily, or at least 3 times a week; a suppository or mini enema; digital rectal stimulation; sitting upright on a commode where possible
Areflexic (LMN) bowelConus or cauda equina. The sphincter is slack and there is no reflex emptying, so stool leaksOnce or more each day; manual removal of stool; a firmer stool to prevent leaks
  • Same time each day, often after a meal or a hot drink, to use the gastrocolic reflex.
  • Aim for soft, formed stool (Bristol type 3–4), with the session finished in under 30 minutes, and never longer than 1 hour.
  • Increase fibre slowly; a high-fibre diet is not right for everyone. Drink enough, balanced against the bladder plan.
  • Physio role: sitting balance and transfers to the commode or shower chair; adapted aids (suppository inserter, digital stimulator) for weak hands; daily activity and exercise.

Classic answer vs current guidance: abdominal massage and straining

Classic

Clockwise abdominal massage and straining to empty the bowel.

Current

The 2020 bowel guideline advises against abdominal massage and the Valsalva manoeuvre for emptying.

Write in the exam

"A regular bowel programme: rectal stimulation for a reflexic bowel, manual removal for an areflexic bowel, upright on a commode, stool consistency controlled."

7Precautions

Take care
  • In SCI at or above T6, a full bladder or bowel can trigger autonomic dysreflexia: check drainage before exercise and know the first steps (see the spinal cord page).
  • Watch for urine infection: fever, cloudy or smelly urine, more spasms or leaks, new autonomic dysreflexia.
  • Keep skin dry and clean after leaks; check under condom sheaths.
  • Never clamp a catheter for long or block drainage tubing on the tilt table or during transfers.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mEnumerate three types of neurological bladder.

Uninhibited (brain lesion: stroke, MS); reflex or automatic (cord lesion above S2: SCI); autonomous or atonic (sacral cord or cauda equina). Also sensory paralytic and motor paralytic.

3 mWhat is a reflex bladder?

The bladder after a complete cord lesion above the sacral segments. The sacral reflex arc is intact but cut off from the brain, so the bladder empties by reflex when full, without sensation or control, often against a tight sphincter (dyssynergia). Managed with intermittent catheterisation and antimuscarinic drugs.

7 mExplain the difference between spastic and flaccid bladder. Write physiotherapy management for both. (3 + 4)

Difference (3): spastic: UMN lesion above S2, reflex arc intact, overactive small bladder, dyssynergia, reflex leaks, high pressure and autonomic dysreflexia risk. Flaccid: LMN lesion of the sacral cord or cauda equina, reflex arc broken, large underactive bladder, overflow dribbling, infection and stones.

Physiotherapy (4): assessment with a bladder diary and residual urine; teach clean intermittent catheterisation 4–6 times a day with volumes under 400–500 mL (balance, positioning, hand skills); fluid planning; pelvic floor training where any contraction exists; transfers to the toilet and an accessible toilet; autonomic dysreflexia and infection signs. Avoid tapping, Credé and Valsalva unless urodynamics show safe pressures.

LAQA 55-year-old man had a T9–T10 fracture fixed 2 weeks ago. He senses a full bladder but cannot void without stimulation. Explain the type of bladder. (Part of S26 Q4(a))

A reflex (UMN, spastic) bladder: the lesion is above the sacral micturition centre, so the reflex arc is intact but cut off from the brain, and it empties by reflex when stimulated, often with dyssynergia. His sense of fullness suggests some spared sensory fibres. Manage with clean intermittent catheterisation and antimuscarinic drugs; avoid triggered voiding unless urodynamics show safe pressures; teach infection signs.

MCQA patient with a cauda equina lesion is most likely to have:A. Uninhibited bladderB. Reflex bladderC. Autonomous (areflexic) bladderD. Normal bladder

C. The sacral reflex arc is broken, so the bladder is atonic with a large residual.

MCQDuring intermittent catheterisation, the bladder volume should ideally stay below:A. 150–200 mLB. 400–500 mLC. 800 mLD. 1000 mL

B. EAU 2026: as a rule, not more than 400–500 mL, with catheterisation 4–6 times a day.

MCQWhich is the first-choice method for a reflex bladder after SCI?A. Suprapubic tappingB. Credé manoeuvreC. Clean intermittent catheterisationD. Permanent indwelling catheter

C. Tapping and Credé raise bladder pressure; indwelling catheters carry more complications.
