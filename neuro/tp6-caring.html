TP6Developing a philosophy of caring: person- and family-centred neurological rehabilitation

The syllabus asks you to develop a philosophy of caring; it was not asked on its own in 2016–2026, but it shows in every long case (goals agreed with the patient, family education) and is marked in the practical as the affective domain. The core idea: the person and family are partners, their goals lead, and how you treat them matters as much as what you do.

My revision

1Person-centred care

  • Start from the person's life, values and goals (work, family roles, faith, hobbies), not only from the diagnosis.
  • Set goals together and write them in the person's words (see ICF and goals).
  • Respect autonomy: explain options, risks and benefits; accept informed choices, including saying no.
  • Build self-management and confidence (self-efficacy) so progress continues after discharge.

2Family-centred care in children

  • Parents know their child best and are the main therapists: they decide priorities with you.
  • Give clear, honest information in plain language; teach handling, positioning and a short home programme that fits the family's day.
  • Build on the family's strengths; respect culture, beliefs and resources; support siblings and grandparents in joint families.
  • Coordinate with school and other services; plan transitions (preschool, school, adulthood).

3Communication and the therapeutic relationship

  • Introduce yourself, explain what you will do and why, and ask permission before touching (consent every session).
  • Listen actively; check understanding (ask the person to explain back); use the person's language and avoid jargon.
  • Adapt to aphasia, hearing loss, neglect or cognitive problems: short sentences, gestures, pictures, demonstration, a quiet room.
  • Show empathy and give realistic hope; praise effort and progress.
  • Protect dignity and privacy: drape, close curtains, chaperone where appropriate, respect modesty and cultural norms.

4Ethics in everyday practice

PrincipleIn practice
AutonomyInformed consent; the person's choices; involve them in decisions even when capacity is reduced
BeneficenceEvidence-based treatment aimed at their goals
Non-maleficenceSafe handling, precautions, no unnecessary or ineffective treatment
JusticeFair care regardless of income, caste, religion, gender or disability; help to access schemes
ConfidentialityShare information only with consent and the care team

5Carers, teamwork and difficult situations

  • Carer burden: ask how the carer is coping; teach safe handling; suggest respite, support groups and help with schemes.
  • Teamwork: work with doctors, nurses, occupational and speech therapists, psychologists, social workers, orthotists and teachers; one shared plan.
  • Progressive and terminal disease (MND, advanced Parkinson's, DMD): honest, compassionate conversations; plan ahead; focus on comfort, function and quality of life; respect advance wishes (see MND).
  • Reflective practice: after difficult sessions, think about what went well and what to change; seek supervision.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mPrinciples of family-centred care.

Parents are partners and decide priorities; honest, clear information; teaching handling and a home programme that fits the family's routine; building on family strengths and respecting culture and resources; coordinating with school and services.

7 mDescribe a philosophy of caring in neurological rehabilitation.

Person- and family-centred goals; respect for autonomy with informed consent; communication adapted to the person (aphasia, hearing, cognition); empathy and realistic hope; dignity, privacy and cultural sensitivity; ethics (beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, confidentiality); support for carers; teamwork; honest planning in progressive disease; reflective practice.
