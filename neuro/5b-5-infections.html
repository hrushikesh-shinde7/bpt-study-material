5B(5)Infections of the nervous system: meningitis, encephalitis and their after-effects

Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs were a 3-mark question twice (W19, S21). Brain infections matter to physiotherapists for their after-effects: weakness, spasticity, hydrocephalus, hearing loss, seizures and learning problems that need rehabilitation, much as after brain injury or in cerebral palsy.

My revision

1Meningitis

Definition and types

  • Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, usually from infection.
  • Bacterial (the most serious): group B streptococcus in newborns; meningococcus, pneumococcus and Haemophilus influenzae in children and adults.
  • Tuberculous meningitis (TBM): slower onset over weeks; common in India; often leaves hydrocephalus, strokes and cranial nerve palsies.
  • Viral (usually milder), fungal and parasitic forms.

Causes and key features

  • Fever, headache, neck stiffness, light sensitivity, vomiting, confusion; seizures, coma or limb weakness in severe cases; a non-blanching rash in meningococcal sepsis.
  • Infants: irritability, poor feeding, drowsiness, a bulging fontanelle.
  • TBM stages (modified British Medical Research Council): I, alert with no focal signs; II, drowsy or with focal signs; III, coma.

Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs (3 marks in W19 and S21)

SignHow to elicitPositive when
Kernig's signPatient supine; flex the hip and knee to 90°, then try to straighten the kneePain and resistance in the back of the thigh stop knee extension (stretch on inflamed nerve roots)
Brudzinski's neck signPatient supine; flex the neck passively, chin towards chestThe hips and knees flex involuntarily
Brudzinski's contralateral leg signFlex one hip and knee passivelyThe other leg flexes too

Meaning: signs of meningeal irritation (meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage). Limit: in adults with suspected meningitis each sign was positive in only about 5% of those with meningitis (Thomas et al., 2002), so a negative sign never rules meningitis out; neck stiffness is also often absent.

Medical point that matters for physiotherapy

  • Diagnosis by lumbar puncture (CSF cells, protein, sugar, organisms), blood cultures, and CT or MRI before the puncture when needed.
  • Urgent antibiotics (and steroids in some types); anti-tuberculous treatment for months in TBM; shunt for hydrocephalus.
  • About 1 in 6 people with bacterial meningitis die, and about 1 in 5 survivors have lasting effects: hearing loss, seizures, limb weakness, vision, speech and memory problems, and amputations after sepsis (WHO).
  • Every child should have a hearing test after bacterial meningitis.

Precautions

  • Infection control in the acute stage (masks, hand hygiene) as the team directs.
  • Avoid neck flexion and head-down positions while brain pressure may be high; watch for seizures.
  • Watch for shunt problems and new weakness after TBM (stroke, hydrocephalus).

Exam points

  • For 3 marks: both signs with method, response and meaning, plus the low-sensitivity point.

2Encephalitis

  • Encephalitis is inflammation of the brain tissue, usually viral: fever, headache, confusion, behaviour change, seizures, coma, focal weakness.
  • Japanese encephalitis is spread by Culex mosquitoes and is endemic in parts of India; about 1 in 250 infections causes severe illness, up to 30% of those with symptoms die, and 20–30% of survivors keep cognitive, behavioural or neurological problems (WHO, 2024). JE vaccine is in India's immunisation programme in endemic districts.
  • After-effects: movement disorders (parkinsonism, dystonia), spasticity, cognitive and behavioural problems, epilepsy.
  • Physiotherapy: as for brain injury: positioning and chest care in the acute stage, then graded task practice, tone management, balance and gait training, and behavioural strategies (see brain injury).

3Other infections that need rehabilitation

  • Neurocysticercosis (pork tapeworm cysts in the brain): a common cause of seizures in India; occasionally hydrocephalus.
  • Brain abscess: focal weakness or seizures depending on the site.
  • Tuberculosis of the spine (Pott's disease) and transverse myelitis: paraplegia (see spinal cord).
  • Post-infectious and infection-related conditions: GBS (GBS page), poliomyelitis (PPRP), leprosy (neuropathies), HIV-related neuropathy and myelopathy.

3 mWhat is Kernig's and Brudzinski's sign? (W19, S21)

Kernig's: supine, hip and knee flexed to 90°; attempting to straighten the knee causes pain and resistance in the hamstrings. Brudzinski's neck sign: passive neck flexion causes involuntary flexion of the hips and knees. Both indicate meningeal irritation (meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage). They are often negative even when meningitis is present (sensitivity about 5% in adults), so they do not rule it out.

3 mList the complications of tuberculous meningitis.

Hydrocephalus, strokes from inflamed arteries (hemiparesis), cranial nerve palsies, vision loss, seizures, spinal arachnoiditis with paraparesis, cognitive and behavioural problems.

7 mPhysiotherapy for a child recovering from bacterial meningitis.

Acute: positioning with head up, turning, chest physiotherapy, gentle range of motion, splints; avoid raising brain pressure. Assessment: milestones, tone, power, balance, hearing and vision, cognition. Rehabilitation: developmental training in sequence; task practice for sitting, standing and walking; tone management and orthoses; balance and coordination; play-based sessions with gestures for hearing loss; parent education and home programme; refer for hearing aids, seizure care and school support.

LAQPractice: an 8-year-old girl treated for tuberculous meningitis 2 months ago has a VP shunt, right hemiparesis and poor balance, and is back home. Write ICF with clinical reasoning, goals and physiotherapy. (5 + 3 + 7)

ICF (5): body functions: right hemiparesis, spasticity, poor balance, fatigue, possible hearing or vision loss; activities: walking, stairs, writing, self-care; participation: school, play; environment: parents, school access, therapy distance; personal: young, keen to return to school. Goals (3): short term: walks indoors safely, uses the right hand as a helper; long term: walks to school, climbs stairs, writes with adaptations. Physiotherapy (7): task-specific gait and balance training, strengthening, stretching and an AFO if needed, constraint-based or bimanual hand practice, endurance, shunt warning signs, home programme and school liaison.
