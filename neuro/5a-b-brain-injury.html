5A(b)Traumatic brain injury and space-occupying lesions: GCS, Rancho levels, raised ICP and physiotherapy

Brain injury was a long case in 5 papers, and the Glasgow Coma Scale was a 3-mark question four times. The cases run from a man unresponsive at GCS 5 to an agitated patient at Rancho level IV and a teacher with a frontal subdural haematoma at GCS 12. Every answer starts by placing the patient on these scales, because the level of consciousness and cognition decides what physiotherapy can do and how to keep the brain safe while doing it.

1Definition

  • Acquired brain injury: damage to the brain after birth that is not hereditary, congenital or degenerative: trauma, stroke, tumours, infection, lack of oxygen.
  • Traumatic brain injury (TBI): an alteration in brain function, or other evidence of brain pathology, caused by an external force (a blow, acceleration–deceleration, a penetrating object or a blast).
  • Space-occupying lesion (SOL): a mass inside the skull (tumour, abscess, haematoma, cyst) that compresses the brain and raises intracranial pressure.
  • In India, road traffic accidents (often two-wheeler riders without helmets) and falls are the main causes of TBI, mostly in young men.

Glasgow Coma Scale (3 marks in W16, S19, S20 and S26)

ResponseScores
Eye opening (E, 1–4)4 spontaneous; 3 to sound (speech); 2 to pressure (pain); 1 none
Verbal response (V, 1–5)5 oriented; 4 confused; 3 words (inappropriate); 2 sounds (incomprehensible); 1 none
Motor response (M, 1–6)6 obeys commands; 5 localises pain; 4 normal flexion (withdraws); 3 abnormal flexion (decorticate); 2 extension (decerebrate); 1 none
  • Total 3–15. Severe 3–8 (8 or less is coma, and the airway usually needs protection); moderate 9–12; mild 13–15.
  • Interpreting GCS (S26): always report the parts, for example "E2 V2 M4 = 8"; the motor score predicts outcome best; record "T" for verbal when the person has a tube; use the best response, and the better side for motor.
  • Used in (W16): head injury, stroke, meningitis and encephalitis, drug overdose, any patient with reduced consciousness.

3Causes and risk factors

  • TBI: road traffic accidents, falls (children and older people), assault, sports, industrial accidents. Risk rises with alcohol, no helmet and no seat belt.
  • Space-occupying lesions: tumours (gliomas, meningiomas, metastases, pituitary tumours), abscess, tuberculoma (common in India), chronic subdural haematoma, cysts.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Primary injury: contusions under the impact and opposite it (coup and contrecoup), haematomas, and shearing of axons in rotational injuries (diffuse axonal injury: coma from the moment of injury, often with a nearly normal CT; MRI shows small lesions in the corpus callosum, internal capsule and brainstem).
  2. Secondary injury: swelling and bleeding raise intracranial pressure; because the skull cannot expand, cerebral perfusion pressure falls (mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure), so more brain becomes ischaemic. Low oxygen and low blood pressure make it worse.
  3. If pressure keeps rising, the brain herniates: the uncus presses on the third cranial nerve (a fixed, dilated pupil), then the brainstem fails.
  4. Impairments depend on where the damage is: reduced consciousness; motor problems (hemiplegia, spasticity, ataxia, rigidity); frontal lobe problems (poor attention, planning, judgement, disinhibition, apathy); memory loss; speech, swallowing and visual problems; seizures.
  5. Secondary complications: contractures, heterotopic ossification, pressure sores, chest infection, deconditioning.

Raised intracranial pressure (3 marks in W22 and S25)

  • Symptoms: headache (worse on waking, coughing and bending), vomiting (may be projectile), blurred vision.
  • Signs: falling level of consciousness; papilloedema; a dilating pupil on the side of the mass; sixth nerve palsy; seizures; in infants a bulging fontanelle.
  • Cushing's triad (late, dangerous): rising blood pressure with a wide pulse pressure, slowing heart rate and irregular breathing.
  • Numbers: normal ICP is about 5–15 mmHg in adults; guidelines treat ICP above 22 mmHg and aim for a cerebral perfusion pressure of 60–70 mmHg (Brain Trauma Foundation, 2016).

5Clinical features

  • Consciousness: coma, then vegetative state (eyes open, no awareness) or minimally conscious state; post-traumatic amnesia while confused.
  • Motor: hemiparesis, often on both sides; spasticity; abnormal postures (decorticate, decerebrate); ataxia; balance loss.
  • Cognitive and behavioural: attention, memory, executive function, slowed processing, agitation, impulsivity, poor insight.
  • Frontal lobe injury (S23, S24 cases): poor planning and judgement, disinhibition or apathy, poor safety awareness; if the motor cortex is involved, weakness on the opposite side.
  • Others: visual field or acuity loss (S24 case), double vision, dizziness and benign positional vertigo, speech and swallowing problems, seizures, fatigue, headache.
  • Space-occupying lesions: slowly progressive headache, seizures, focal weakness, personality change, signs of raised ICP.

6Investigations

  • CT brain: the first scan: fractures, haematomas, contusions, swelling, midline shift.
  • MRI: diffuse axonal injury, tumours and smaller lesions.
  • ICP monitoring in severe TBI; blood gases; electrolytes; EEG for seizures.
  • Bedside: GCS and pupils at regular intervals; post-traumatic amnesia testing.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Prevent secondary injury: airway, oxygen and blood pressure; treat raised ICP (head-up position, sedation, osmotic therapy, drainage of CSF); prevent seizures and fever.
  • Surgery: evacuation of extradural or acute subdural haematoma; elevation of a depressed fracture; decompressive craniectomy for uncontrolled ICP (a skull flap is left out, so the soft area must be protected); burr holes for chronic subdural haematoma; tumour removal.
  • Later: tracheostomy and feeding tube if needed; treatment of spasticity (botulinum toxin, baclofen) and agitation; tumour therapy (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, steroids for swelling).

9PT assessment

  • Before treatment: GCS, pupils, ICP and CPP if monitored, blood pressure, oxygen, sedation, ventilator, skull precautions (craniectomy), fractures elsewhere.
  • Level of cognition: Rancho level; post-traumatic amnesia; Coma Recovery Scale-Revised in disorders of consciousness; Agitated Behaviour Scale.
  • Respiratory: chest, secretions, cough, swallow.
  • Motor: tone (Modified Ashworth), posture, range, voluntary movement, reflexes; balance and gait when able (Berg, High-level Mobility Assessment Tool later).
  • Others: vision, hearing, sensation, communication, behaviour; family, job and home.

12PT management

Principles: protect the injured brain (ICP and CPP limits); prevent secondary complications; match the session to the level of consciousness and cognition; use functional, familiar tasks; involve the family.

Acute stage in the ICU (7 marks in S18 and S20)

  • Positioning: head of the bed raised about 30° and the head in midline as the unit advises; avoid neck flexion and rotation, which compress the jugular veins; side lying and supported positions that reduce abnormal postures; turn regularly.
  • Chest care: positioning, gentle manual techniques and short, well-oxygenated suctioning as allowed; no head-down postural drainage while ICP is raised or unstable.
  • Range and splints: daily passive range of all joints; splints or serial casts for ankles and wrists when tone rises.
  • Early mobilisation: sitting over the bed edge and tilt table once ICP is controlled and the team agrees.
  • Sensory input: structured, meaningful input (family voices, familiar music, touch) in short periods; evidence for "coma stimulation" is weak, so avoid overstimulation.
  • Watch ICP, CPP, blood pressure and oxygen before, during and after each treatment.

By level of cognition

  • Unresponsive (GCS 3–8, Rancho I–II; S26 case): positioning and turning; chest care; range, splints and casting; tilt table and sitting for arousal; structured sensory input; teach the family.
  • Confused and agitated (Rancho IV; W24 case): reduce stimulation (quiet room, one therapist, short sessions); familiar, automatic tasks such as walking, sitting to standing and washing the face; let the person move safely (low bed, mats) instead of restraining; do not argue or test memory; stop and try later if agitation rises.
  • Confused, non-agitated (Rancho V–VI): structured routine at the same time each day; one-step instructions; errorless learning (guide the right movement rather than letting mistakes happen); memory aids; repeated functional practice.
  • Automatic to purposeful (Rancho VII–VIII): balance, dual tasks, community walking, fitness, vocational and driving assessment.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Stop if ICP rises and stays above the set limit, CPP falls, or the GCS drops; report a new dilated pupil at once.
  • No head-down positions or tight neck collars that block venous drainage while ICP is raised; keep suctioning short with pre-oxygenation.
  • After craniectomy, protect the skull defect (helmet when mobilising); no pressure on the soft area.
  • Clear other fractures and spinal injuries before moving the patient.
  • Seizure plan known; agitation: keep yourself and the patient safe, keep exits clear.
  • Brain tumours: watch fatigue, seizures and steroid effects (weak muscles, high sugar); set goals with the patient's prognosis in mind.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Explain the stages of recovery to the family, especially that agitation is a stage and usually passes.
  • Teach positioning, range, safe transfers and a calm routine at home.
  • Memory aids, a daily timetable, rest breaks; avoid alcohol.
  • Helmet and seat-belt use; driving only after medical clearance (vision, cognition, seizures).
  • Graded return to work or study; support groups and counselling for the family.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Worse outcome with older age, a low GCS motor score, non-reacting pupils, low oxygen or blood pressure after injury, and large lesions or swelling on CT.
  • A longer period of post-traumatic amnesia means a more severe injury and a longer recovery.
  • Recovery is fastest in the first months but continues for years; cognitive and behavioural problems often limit return to work more than physical ones.
  • Discharge from inpatient care when safe at home with supervision as needed, with outpatient or community rehabilitation in place.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite the components of the Glasgow Coma Scale.

Eye opening 1–4 (spontaneous, to sound, to pressure, none); verbal 1–5 (oriented, confused, words, sounds, none); motor 1–6 (obeys, localises, normal flexion, abnormal flexion, extension, none). Total 3–15; severe 3–8, moderate 9–12, mild 13–15.

3 mEnumerate the signs of raised intracranial pressure.

Headache worse on waking and coughing; vomiting; papilloedema; falling consciousness; dilating pupil; sixth nerve palsy; Cushing's triad (rising blood pressure, slow pulse, irregular breathing); seizures; bulging fontanelle in infants.

7 mPhysiotherapy in the acute stage of traumatic brain injury.

Assess GCS, pupils, ICP and CPP, vital signs, chest and tone. Position with the head of the bed up about 30° and the head in midline; avoid neck flexion and head-down positions. Chest care within ICP limits: positioning, gentle manual techniques, short suctioning with pre-oxygenation. Turning and pressure care. Daily passive range, splints and serial casting for rising tone. Early sitting and tilt table once ICP is controlled. Structured sensory input. Family education. Stop if ICP rises above the limit or GCS falls.

LAQA 40-year-old man a week after a road accident has diffuse axonal injury, GCS 5, no voluntary movement and no response to stimuli. Plan goals and treatment techniques. (S26)

Goals: short-term (2–4 weeks): clear chest; no pressure sores or contractures; tolerates sitting and tilt table; shows more responses. Long-term (months): sits with support; follows commands; family able to manage positioning and range; progress to active rehabilitation as consciousness returns.

Techniques: positioning with head up 30° and midline; turning; chest care within ICP limits; passive range of all joints daily; resting splints and serial casts; tilt table and sitting for arousal; structured sensory input (familiar voices, touch, music) in short periods; monitoring of ICP, CPP and vital signs; family teaching.
