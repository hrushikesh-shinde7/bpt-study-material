5A(i)Parkinson's disease: features, Hoehn and Yahr, gait, falls and physiotherapy

Parkinson's disease was a long case in 7 papers, usually a retired person at Hoehn and Yahr stage III, and parkinsonian gait was a 7-mark question four times. Every answer rests on one idea: the basal ganglia no longer scale and automate movement, so the person must move big and on purpose, use cues, and keep exercising to stay strong, fit and safe from falls.

My revision

1Definition

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a slowly progressive neurodegenerative disease in which dopamine-producing neurons of the substantia nigra die, so the basal ganglia cannot control movement normally. It causes parkinsonism: bradykinesia together with rest tremor, rigidity or both (Movement Disorder Society criteria, 2015), plus many non-motor problems.

  • It affects about 1% of people over 60 and becomes more common with age; men are affected a little more often.
  • Parkinsonism is the clinical picture; PD is its commonest cause.

2Classification and grading

Causes of parkinsonism

  • Idiopathic PD (the commonest), including young-onset and genetic forms.
  • Drug-induced: antipsychotics such as haloperidol, metoclopramide; often reversible when the drug is stopped.
  • Vascular parkinsonism: small-vessel disease; legs more than arms ("lower-body parkinsonism"), little tremor.
  • Parkinson-plus syndromes: progressive supranuclear palsy (early falls, poor vertical gaze), multiple system atrophy (early autonomic failure, ataxia), corticobasal degeneration. They respond poorly to levodopa.
  • Others: after encephalitis or repeated head injury; toxins (manganese); Wilson's disease in young people.

Hoehn and Yahr stages (3 marks in W20)

StageOriginal scaleModified scale adds
1One side only; little or no disability1.5: one side plus the trunk (axial) involved
2Both sides, balance not impaired2.5: both sides, recovers on the pull test
3Both sides, mild to moderate; some postural instability; physically independentNo change
4Severe disability; can still walk or stand without helpNo change
5Wheelchair-bound or bedridden unless helpedNo change

Stage III is the turning point that examiners like: balance is now impaired (falls begin), but the person is still independent.

Clinical subtypes: tremor-dominant and postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD). The PIGD type has more falls and tends to progress faster.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Age: the strongest risk factor.
  • Genes: about 5–10% of cases (for example LRRK2, SNCA, PINK1, parkin, GBA), more in young-onset disease.
  • Environment: pesticides, rural living and well water have been linked with PD; head injury.
  • Most cases come from a mix of ageing, genes and environment; no single cause is found.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

What the basal ganglia do (3 marks in W21)

  • Select and start the wanted movement and suppress unwanted ones.
  • Scale the size, speed and force of movement.
  • Run well-learned movements automatically and in sequence (walking, writing, turning in bed).
  • Help motor learning and habits; loops also serve thinking, motivation and mood.

What goes wrong in PD

  1. Dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra (pars compacta) die, and clumps of the protein alpha-synuclein (Lewy bodies) form.
  2. Because striatal dopamine falls, the output of the basal ganglia over-inhibits the thalamus and motor cortex, so movements become slow and small (bradykinesia, hypokinesia).
  3. Because automatic control is lost, routine movements need conscious attention, so walking deteriorates during a second task, and steps freeze in doorways and turns.
  4. Rigidity and rest tremor arise from abnormal basal ganglia rhythms.
  5. Because other brain systems are affected too, non-motor problems (sleep, mood, memory, bowel, blood pressure) appear, some years before the motor signs.
  6. Symptoms appear only after a large part of the nigral neurons is lost, so the disease begins years before diagnosis.

5Clinical features

Cardinal features (3 marks in S25)

FeatureDescription
TremorAt rest, 4–6 per second, "pill-rolling" of thumb and fingers; often starts in one hand; less with movement and absent in sleep; worse with stress
RigidityStiffness through the whole range in flexors and extensors alike (lead-pipe), or jerky (cogwheel) when tremor is added
Akinesia and bradykinesiaSlowness, difficulty starting, and movements that get smaller as they repeat; mask-like face, soft voice, small handwriting
Postural instabilityPoor balance reactions, a later feature; falls backwards on the pull test
Memory hook

TRAP

  • Tremor at rest
  • Rigidity
  • Akinesia and bradykinesia
  • Postural instability

Checked against the MDS 2015 criteria: diagnosis needs bradykinesia plus rest tremor or rigidity; postural instability is a later feature, not a diagnostic one.

Rigidity and its types (3 marks in W19 and S24)

  • Lead-pipe rigidity: smooth, even resistance through the whole range, in both directions, at any speed (PD).
  • Cogwheel rigidity: jerky, ratchet-like resistance, because tremor is added to lead-pipe rigidity (PD).
  • Decorticate rigidity: arms flexed, legs extended; lesion above the red nucleus (severe brain injury).
  • Decerebrate rigidity: arms and legs extended, arms internally rotated; brainstem lesion (worse sign).

Spasticity vs rigidity (3 marks in W17)

FeatureSpasticityRigidity
LesionUpper motor neuron (pyramidal)Extrapyramidal (basal ganglia)
SpeedDepends on speed: worse with fast stretchSame at any speed
PatternClasp-knife: resistance then sudden giveLead-pipe or cogwheel through the whole range
MusclesMainly antigravity muscles (arm flexors, leg extensors)Flexors and extensors equally
ReflexesBrisk; clonus; Babinski signNormal; no Babinski sign

Gait abnormalities (7 marks in S16 and W17; gait training in W16 and S17)

  • Flexed posture: head forward, rounded shoulders, trunk, hips and knees bent.
  • Short, shuffling steps with a flat foot (no heel strike) and little foot clearance.
  • Festination: steps become shorter and faster as if chasing the centre of gravity; propulsion forwards, retropulsion backwards.
  • Reduced arm swing (often one side first) and reduced trunk rotation.
  • Turning en bloc in many small steps.
  • Start hesitation and freezing: feet "glued" to the floor at the start, in doorways, narrow spaces, turns, or when talking while walking.
  • Walking worsens with a second task and in the "off" period of medicines.

Other features

  • Motor: mask face, soft voice, small handwriting, drooling, swallowing difficulty; stooped or sideways-leaning trunk (camptocormia, Pisa syndrome).
  • Non-motor: loss of smell, constipation, acting out dreams in sleep, depression and anxiety, dizziness on standing, urinary urgency, pain, fatigue, and memory problems or dementia in later years.

6Investigations

  • The diagnosis is clinical; a clear, lasting response to levodopa supports it.
  • Dopamine transporter scan (DaTscan): shows loss of nigral nerve endings when the diagnosis is uncertain (for example, PD vs essential tremor).
  • MRI: excludes vascular parkinsonism, tumours and normal-pressure hydrocephalus.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Levodopa with carbidopa: the most effective drug. After some years, its effect wears off before the next dose ("off" periods) and involuntary movements (dyskinesia) appear at peak dose.
  • Others: dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole); MAO-B inhibitors (selegiline, rasagiline); COMT inhibitors (entacapone); amantadine for dyskinesia; anticholinergics (trihexyphenidyl) for tremor in young people, avoided in the elderly because of confusion.
  • Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus for motor fluctuations, dyskinesia and tremor in selected people.
  • Speech and swallowing therapy, occupational therapy, and treatment of depression, constipation and low blood pressure.
For the physiotherapist

Plan exercise and gait training in the "on" period, when the medicine works; teach safety strategies for the "off" period. Record the time of the last dose with every assessment.

9PT assessment

  • History: onset and side, medicines and timing (on and off periods), falls and near-falls, freezing, daily activities, home, job, goals.
  • Staging: Hoehn and Yahr; MDS-UPDRS (part III is the motor examination).
  • Posture, range and strength: kyphosis, hip and knee flexion contractures, chest expansion, sit-to-stand (Five Times Sit to Stand).
  • Balance: Mini-BESTest or Berg Balance Scale; the pull (retropulsion) test.
  • Gait: 10-metre walk (speed, step length, cadence); Timed Up and Go, also with a second task; 6-minute walk; freezing questionnaire; observation of turns and doorways.
  • Others: cognition, mood, fatigue, lying and standing blood pressure; quality of life (PDQ-39).

Worked example from a real paper (W21, 5 + 10 marks)

12PT management

Principles: exercise early and for life; train big, fast and on purpose; use cues and movement strategies to bypass the damaged automatic control; practise the real tasks; prevent falls; time sessions to the "on" period.

What the evidence supports (APTA guideline, 2022)

Strong recommendations ("should"): aerobic exercise, resistance training, balance training, gait training, task-specific training, external cueing, community-based exercise, behaviour-change approaches and integrated (team) care. Weak ("may"): flexibility exercises and telerehabilitation. Most evidence comes from Hoehn and Yahr stages 1–3.

By stage (Hoehn and Yahr)

  • Stages 1–2 (early): stop inactivity and fear of moving; aerobic, resistance and balance exercise; join a community class (dance, tai chi, walking groups).
  • Stages 2–4 (middle, 7 marks for stage III in S26): keep the above, and add gait training with cues, cognitive movement strategies for transfers and turning, balance and fall prevention, posture, and home safety.
  • Stage 5 (late): keep the person safe and comfortable: positioning, pressure care, breathing, assisted transfers, and carer training.

Gait training (7 marks in S16, W16, S17 and W17)

  • External cues: visual stripes on the floor at the target step length; a metronome or music beat; a verbal cue ("big step"). Cues bypass the faulty basal ganglia timing.
  • Attention: think about long steps and heel strike; avoid talking or carrying things while walking in the early sessions.
  • Practise: walking with long steps and arm swing; treadmill walking; walking with Nordic poles; turning in a wide arc rather than pivoting; doorways and narrow spaces; stops and starts; walking backwards and sideways.
  • Festination: stop, stand tall, and restart with a long step; widen the base.
  • Dose: gait training 20–60 minutes, 3–5 days a week, for 4–12 weeks; cueing training 2–5 times a week (APTA 2022). Gains fade when training stops, so keep it going.

Freezing of gait

  1. Stop trying to push forward; stand tall and shift weight to one leg.
  2. Step over a real or imagined line, or onto a mark on the floor.
  3. Use a rhythm: count aloud, march on the spot, or a metronome.
  4. Turn in a wide arc; walk through doorways with a target beyond them.

Cognitive movement strategies (for transfers and daily tasks)

Break a complex movement into separate steps, rehearse it in the mind, then perform each step consciously in order. Example, rising from a chair: move to the front of the seat, feet back, lean well forward ("nose over toes"), push up and stand tall. The same approach works for turning in bed and getting up from the floor.

Bradykinesia and rigidity (7 marks in S22)

  • Bradykinesia: large-amplitude training such as LSVT BIG (16 one-hour sessions, 4 days a week for 4 weeks) using whole-body movements at maximal size; speed and power training; cues and cognitive strategies; aerobic exercise.
  • Rigidity: slow rhythmic trunk and limb rotation; rhythmic initiation (passive, then active-assisted, then active); large reciprocal movements; flexibility exercises for pectorals, hip flexors and hamstrings; relaxation; warm-up before exercise.

Classic answer vs current evidence: high-intensity exercise

Classic

Gentle range-of-motion and relaxation exercises; avoid fatigue.

Current

Moderate- to high-intensity aerobic and resistance exercise is recommended (APTA 2022). In early PD, treadmill exercise at 80–85% of maximum heart rate was safe, and motor scores stayed stable over 6 months while usual care worsened (SPARX, 2018).

Write in the exam

"Aerobic exercise at moderate to high intensity 3 days a week for 30–40 minutes, with resistance and balance training, cueing and task practice."

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Train in the "on" period; expect slower, riskier movement in "off" periods.
  • Guard closely during balance and turning practice; use a gait belt.
  • Check lying and standing blood pressure; stop if dizzy.
  • Use perceived exertion as well as heart rate, because the heart rate response may be blunted.
  • Keep dual tasks for when single-task walking is safe; watch for confusion in people with cognitive decline.
  • Swallowing difficulty: no exercise straight after meals in a reclined position.

14Education, lifestyle and home management

S19 asked for home management that helps the person perform better (5 marks).

  • Home safety: remove loose rugs and clutter; good lighting with switches at the bed; grab bars in the bathroom; a raised western toilet or commode; firm chairs with armrests; clear wide paths for turning; floor stripes at freezing spots such as doorways.
  • Daily tasks: do demanding tasks in the "on" period; sit to dress; loose clothes with Velcro or large buttons, elastic laces; large-handled or weighted cutlery and a non-slip mat for tremor; a bed rope or rail for turning.
  • Walking out: a stick or walker if advised; avoid crowds at first; good footwear.
  • Exercise: a daily home programme plus a community class; keep it up for life.
  • Other: take medicines on time; fluids and fibre for constipation; speech and swallowing exercises from the speech therapist; family education and support groups.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • PD progresses slowly over many years; the speed varies. The tremor-dominant type usually progresses more slowly than the postural instability and gait difficulty type.
  • Falls, swallowing problems and dementia mark the later stages and raise the risk of death, often from pneumonia.
  • Physiotherapy is given in blocks with regular review, and the person keeps exercising in between; discharge from a block when its goals are met and a home programme is in place.

Answer: fall prevention strategies (S22, 4 marks)

  • Find her risks: previous falls, falls on turning, freezing, dual-task walking, medicine timing, low blood pressure, home hazards.
  • Balance training 2–3 times a week (reactive steps, turning, weight shifts), with leg strengthening.
  • Turning in a wide arc; freezing strategies and floor cues; no talking while walking in risky places until trained.
  • Home safety (rugs, lighting, grab bars, raised toilet); safe footwear; a walking aid if needed.
  • Activities in the "on" period; stand up slowly; teach getting up from the floor.

7 mDescribe the common gait abnormalities in Parkinson's disease and their physiotherapy. (2 + 5)

Abnormalities (2): flexed posture; short shuffling steps without heel strike; festination; reduced arm swing and trunk rotation; en bloc turning; start hesitation and freezing; worse with dual tasks and in "off" periods.
