5A(j)Anterior horn cell diseases: motor neuron disease, poliomyelitis and SMA

Motor neuron disease came up in 8 papers: two long cases of young men with ALS, the types and diagnostic criteria of MND, pseudobulbar palsy, and physiotherapy for a terminally ill patient. MND progresses steadily, so physiotherapy aims to keep the person independent, comfortable and breathing well for as long as possible, staying a step ahead with equipment and never exercising to exhaustion.

My revision

1Definition

  • Motor neuron disease (MND) is a progressive degeneration of motor neurons in the cortex (upper motor neurons), brainstem and spinal cord anterior horns (lower motor neurons), causing increasing weakness, wasting and spasticity, and eventually breathing failure. Sensation is usually spared.
  • It is rare (about 1–3 new cases per 100,000 people a year), usually starting after 50; about 1 in 10 cases is familial.

2Classification and grading

Asked for 3 marks in S21 and W24 (types of MND) and for 7 marks in S23 (classify MND with features).

Diagnostic criteria (3 marks in S18)

  • Gold Coast criteria (2020): (1) progressive motor impairment, after previously normal function, shown by history or repeated examination; (2) UMN and LMN dysfunction in at least one body region, or LMN dysfunction in at least two regions; (3) investigations that exclude other diseases.
  • The older revised El Escorial criteria graded ALS as definite, probable or possible according to the number of regions (bulbar, cervical, thoracic, lumbosacral) with UMN and LMN signs.
  • EMG (fibrillations, fasciculations, chronic neurogenic changes) shows LMN involvement even in muscles that look normal.

Function scale: ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R, 0–48: bulbar, fine motor, gross motor and breathing).

3Causes and risk factors

  • Cause unknown in most; about 10% familial (genes such as SOD1 and C9orf72); age and male sex increase risk.
  • Mechanisms include protein misfolding, glutamate excitotoxicity, oxidative stress and inflammation.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Because LMNs die, muscles lose their supply: wasting, fasciculations (twitches), cramps and weakness.
  2. Because UMNs die, there is spasticity, brisk reflexes and Babinski signs, even in wasted limbs.
  3. Because brainstem neurons fail, speech, swallowing and chewing weaken (risk of aspiration and weight loss).
  4. Because the diaphragm and intercostals weaken, breathing fails, first at night (morning headache, poor sleep).
  5. Eye movements, sensation and bladder and bowel control are usually spared; some people develop frontotemporal dementia.

5Clinical features

  • Limb onset: weakness and wasting of the small hand muscles (difficulty buttoning or opening the fist), foot drop, cramps, fasciculations; the thenar muscles waste more than the hypothenar ("split hand").
  • Bulbar onset: slurred speech, choking, drooling, wasted tongue with fasciculations.
  • Later: neck extensor weakness (head drop), breathing weakness, weight loss, fatigue, emotional lability.

Poliomyelitis and spinal muscular atrophy (brief)

  • Acute poliomyelitis: a virus that destroys anterior horn cells, causing sudden, asymmetrical, flaccid paralysis after a feverish illness, with normal sensation. India has been polio-free since 2014; the residual effects are on the post-polio residual paralysis page.
  • Spinal muscular atrophy: an inherited loss of anterior horn cells, usually in childhood: symmetrical, proximal weakness with hypotonia; see the SMA and HSMN page.

6Investigations

  • EMG and nerve conduction: widespread denervation with normal sensory studies.
  • MRI of brain and spine to exclude mimics (cervical myelopathy, tumours).
  • Blood tests (thyroid, B12, calcium, CK); genetic tests in familial cases.
  • Breathing: forced vital capacity (FVC), sniff nasal pressure, oxygen saturation overnight, blood gases.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Riluzole prolongs median survival by about 2–3 months; other drugs (edaravone; tofersen for SOD1-related ALS) are available in some countries.
  • Non-invasive ventilation for breathing weakness improves sleep, symptoms and survival, most clearly when bulbar weakness is not severe.
  • Nutrition: gastrostomy feeding when swallowing is unsafe or weight falls.
  • Symptom control: cramps, spasticity (baclofen), saliva, emotional lability, pain, low mood.
  • A multidisciplinary team, with early palliative care and advance care planning.

9PT assessment

  • History: onset region, speed of progression, falls, fatigue, cramps, breathing at night, swallowing, work and home.
  • Muscle strength (MRC grades or hand-held dynamometry), wasting, fasciculations, tone, reflexes.
  • Function: ALSFRS-R; grip and pinch; timed walk; Berg; transfers.
  • Breathing: FVC (sitting and lying), cough peak flow, breathlessness lying flat.
  • Range of motion, posture, neck control, pain, fatigue, mood and cognition.

Worked example from a real paper (W16, 7 + 4 + 4 marks)

"A 29 years old male teacher, who belongs to the middle class and has one child and a non working spouse is diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis 4 months back. He complains of difficulty in writing on the board and fatigue along with difficulty in standing for prolonged period of time. Describe functional impairments with clinical reasoning short and long term PT management."

Spot the ICF clues before you write

ALS 4 months: early stage, mixed UMN and LMN. difficulty writing on the board: shoulder and hand weakness. fatigue, difficulty standing for long: leg weakness and reduced endurance. teacher, one child, non working spouse: he is the only earner, so keep him working as long as possible with energy conservation and adaptations, and plan ahead for the family.

One-line functional diagnosis

A 29-year-old teacher with early ALS, with shoulder, hand and leg weakness and marked fatigue, who struggles to write on the board and stand through lessons, putting his job and family income at risk.

Practice case: S16 36-year-old dentist with ALS, weakness and wasting of the small hand muscles, fasciculations in the forearm and upper arm, brisk reflexes in the arms, reduced reflexes in the legs and bilateral Babinski signs (structural and functional impairments, goals, physiotherapy).

12PT management

By stage

  • Early (independent): moderate, non-fatiguing strengthening of muscles at grade 3 or more and gentle aerobic activity (walking, cycling, swimming); daily range of motion and stretching; energy conservation and pacing; ergonomic changes at work; education.
  • Middle (some help): orthoses (AFOs for foot drop, wrist and thumb splints, a supportive neck collar), walking aids, then a wheelchair for distances; transfer training; breathing exercises and airway clearance; manage cramps and spasticity with stretching.
  • Late (dependent): positioning and pressure care, passive range of motion to prevent painful stiffness, chest care with assisted cough and suction, support of NIV, equipment (hoist, profiling bed, tilt-in-space chair), carer training.

Two small trials (43 people) found no harm from moderate exercise and slightly better function at 3 months, but they are too small to be sure (Cochrane 2013). Never exercise to exhaustion.

Physiotherapy for the terminally ill patient (7 marks in W25)

  • Comfort and positioning: supported positions, pillows, a profiling bed, pressure-relieving mattress; regular turning.
  • Range of motion: gentle passive movements to prevent painful stiff joints, especially shoulders.
  • Breathlessness: upright or forward-lean positions, a hand-held fan, breathing control and relaxation; support NIV use and mask comfort.
  • Secretions: positioning, gentle airway clearance, manually assisted cough or a cough-assist machine, suction as needed.
  • Equipment: hoist, slide sheets, tilt-in-space wheelchair, head support.
  • Carers and patient: teach safe handling and positioning; respect the person's wishes and advance care plan; support emotionally; work with the palliative care team.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Avoid exercise to fatigue, heavy eccentric work and strengthening of muscles below grade 3.
  • Stop if cramps, fasciculations or weakness increase the next day.
  • Watch for breathlessness lying flat, morning headache and poor sleep (night hypoventilation): refer.
  • Aspiration risk: upright for meals and for 30 minutes after.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Honest information and time; plan ahead for equipment, work and finances.
  • Daily range of motion and gentle exercise; energy conservation.
  • Home changes: ground-floor living, rails, a commode, bathroom changes.
  • Communication aids early, before speech fails.
  • Support for the spouse and children; patient associations.

15Prognosis

  • Median survival is about 3 years from the first symptoms (2–4 years in most studies), mostly limited by breathing failure; up to 1 in 10 people live more than 10 years.
  • Bulbar onset and older age progress faster; primary lateral sclerosis progresses slowest.
  • NIV, nutrition and riluzole lengthen survival; physiotherapy maintains function and comfort.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite the types of MND.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (UMN and LMN); progressive bulbar palsy; progressive muscular atrophy (LMN only); primary lateral sclerosis (UMN only).

3 mWhat is pseudobulbar palsy?

Weakness of bulbar muscles from bilateral UMN (corticobulbar) lesions, as in bilateral strokes, MND or MS: small stiff tongue without wasting, spastic speech, dysphagia, brisk jaw jerk and emotional lability.

7 mPhysiotherapy for a terminally ill patient with MND.

Comfort positioning and pressure care; gentle passive range of motion; breathlessness management (positions, fan, breathing control, NIV support); secretion clearance (positioning, assisted cough, cough machine, suction); equipment (hoist, tilt-in-space chair, head support); carer training; respect for the patient's wishes and advance care plan; teamwork with palliative care.

LAQA 36-year-old dentist with ALS has wasting of the small hand muscles, fasciculations, brisk arm reflexes and bilateral Babinski signs. Write structural and functional impairments, goals and physiotherapy. (3 + 5 + 7, S16)

Impairments (3): structural: degeneration of anterior horn cells and corticospinal tracts; functional: hand weakness and wasting (cannot open the fist, poor fine grip for dental work), fasciculations, spasticity and brisk reflexes, possible leg weakness, fatigue. Goals (5): keep hand function for work as long as possible with adapted instruments; maintain strength and range; manage fatigue; prevent falls; plan for work changes and equipment. Physiotherapy (7): non-fatiguing strengthening of muscles at grade 3 or more; range and stretching; splints for the thumb and wrist; adapted, thicker instrument handles; energy conservation; aerobic walking or cycling at moderate intensity; breathing monitoring; education and stage-based planning.
