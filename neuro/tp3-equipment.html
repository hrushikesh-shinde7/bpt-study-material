TP3Tools and adaptive equipment for neuro-rehabilitation: balls, tilt boards, bolsters, wedges, benches and mats

The syllabus names the equipment of a neuro gym: vestibular balls, tilt boards, bolsters, wedges, graded benches and therapeutic mats. It was not asked in theory in 2016–2026, but you will use and name these in the practical; for each piece know what it trains, one or two activities, how to progress, and how to keep the patient safe.

1The named equipment

EquipmentTrainsExample activities and progression
Therapy (vestibular, Swiss) ballPostural and equilibrium reactions, protective reactions, trunk control, vestibular input; relaxation by slow rockingChild prone over the ball for head and arm weight bearing; sitting on the ball with weight shifts; tipping slowly to elicit protective arm extension. Progress: larger movements, less support, faster tilts; a peanut-shaped ball for more stability
Tilt and wobble boardsEquilibrium (tilting) reactions, ankle and hip strategies, standing and sitting balanceSit, kneel or stand on the board while it tilts in one direction (rocker) or all directions (wobble). Progress: from sitting to standing, eyes open to closed, holding on to free hands, adding a reaching or catching task
Bolsters and rollsWeight bearing through arms, hip abduction (straddle sitting), positioning, trunk controlProne over a roll for elbow and hand weight bearing; straddle sitting to reduce adductor tone and train sitting balance; side-to-side rolling for reactions

Graded benches and stepsSit to stand, step-ups, stair training, seated balance at different heightsSit to stand from a higher bench, lowered stepwise; step-ups on graded steps; benches as stable seats for reaching
Therapeutic matsBed and floor mobility, rolling, crawling, kneeling, getting up from the floorMat programme: rolling, prone on elbows, four-point kneeling, kneeling, half-kneeling, floor to stand; push-ups in long sitting for spinal cord injury

2Other common equipment

  • Parallel bars: early standing and walking with safe hand support; weight shifting; move to a walker or stick as soon as possible.
  • Tilt table: gradual uprighting for orthostatic hypotension; early weight bearing; stretch of the calves.
  • Standing frame and prone stander: supported standing for children and adults who cannot stand alone.
  • Treadmill (with or without body-weight support): repetitive walking practice and endurance.
  • Mirror: visual feedback for posture and symmetry; mirror therapy for the arm after stroke.
  • Foam pads, balance beams, cones and obstacles: balance on unstable surfaces, narrow-base and obstacle walking.
  • Weights, resistance bands, pulleys and static cycles: strengthening and fitness.
  • Hand-function boards, pegboards and putty: grasp, release and dexterity.

3Safety and good practice

Take care
  • Guard the patient on unstable equipment; use a gait belt; clear the space; mats under balls and boards.
  • Check the ball is properly inflated and the right size (hips and knees at about 90° when sitting).
  • Avoid fast spinning or rocking in people prone to seizures or autonomic problems; watch for dizziness and blood pressure changes.
  • Children with atlantoaxial instability (Down's syndrome): no forced neck flexion or trampoline-type bouncing.
  • Clean equipment between patients; use latex-free balls and bands where latex allergy is possible (spina bifida).
  • Equipment supports the task; the goal is always a real-life skill without it.

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mUses of a therapy (vestibular) ball in neuro-rehabilitation.

Trains postural, equilibrium and protective reactions and trunk control, gives vestibular input, and can relax by slow rocking. Activities: prone over the ball for head and arm weight bearing; sitting with weight shifts; tipping to elicit protective extension. Progress by larger, faster movements and less support. Guard the patient and use a mat.

3 mUses of a wedge.

Prone on a wedge with forearm support to train head and upper-trunk control (toys in front); positioning for comfort, feeding or chest care in half-lying. Progress to a lower wedge and then flat prone.

7 mDescribe the equipment used to train balance in neurological patients.

Therapy and peanut balls (sitting reactions); rocker and wobble boards (standing and sitting tilting reactions); foam pads and beams (unstable and narrow surfaces); parallel bars (early standing with support); steps and benches (step-ups, stairs); cones and obstacles (dynamic balance); mirror (feedback). Give progressions (support, base, eyes, surface, speed, dual task) and safety (guarding, gait belt, mats).

LAQPractice: plan a one-hour gym session using equipment for a 4-year-old with spastic diplegia who sits alone but cannot stand without support. (15)

Warm-up: prone over a roll with forearm weight bearing and reaching (5 min). Straddle sitting on a bolster with weight shifts and reaching across the midline (10 min). Ball: sitting with tilts for equilibrium and protective reactions (10 min). Kneeling and half-kneeling on the mat with a bench in front (10 min). Sit to stand from a graded bench, lowering the height (10 min). Standing in a standing frame with play at a table, then supported standing on a rocker board with hands held (10 min). Close with calf and hamstring stretches and parent teaching (5 min). Guard throughout; record repetitions and the bench height reached.

MCQA board that tilts in all directions is a:A. Rocker boardB. Wobble boardC. WedgeD. Bolster

B.

MCQStraddle sitting on a bolster mainly helps a child with:A. Adductor spasticityB. Hypermobile kneesC. Foot dropD. Torticollis

A. It holds the hips abducted while training sitting balance.

MCQWhen sitting on a correctly sized therapy ball, the hips and knees are at about:A. 45°B. 90°C. 120°D. Full extension

B.
