5B(3)Neural tube defects: spina bifida (myelomeningocele) and hydrocephalus

Spina bifida and hydrocephalus came up in 9 papers: physiotherapy for meningomyelocele or spina bifida five times, physiotherapy for hydrocephalus once, and the types of spina bifida, its deformities and the features of hydrocephalus for 3 marks. Every answer turns on the neurological level, because the muscles that still work decide the deformities, the orthoses and how far the child will walk.

My revision

1Definition

  • Neural tube defects happen when the neural tube fails to close in the first 4 weeks of pregnancy: anencephaly and encephalocele at the head end, spina bifida along the spine.
  • Spina bifida is incomplete closure of the vertebral arches, with or without a sac containing the meninges and the spinal cord.
  • Hydrocephalus is too much cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the ventricles of the brain, usually under raised pressure, so the ventricles enlarge.

2Classification and grading

Types of spina bifida (3 marks in S25)

  • Spina bifida occulta: a gap in the bony arch only; the skin is closed, sometimes with a tuft of hair, dimple or fatty lump; usually no deficit.
  • Meningocele: a skin-covered sac of meninges and CSF through the gap; the cord stays in the canal, so deficits are few.
  • Myelomeningocele (meningomyelocele): the cord and nerve roots lie in the sac, which is often open; paralysis, sensory loss and bladder problems below the level. The commonest serious type.
  • Others: lipomyelomeningocele (a fatty mass tethering the cord under closed skin) and myeloschisis (an open, flat cord plate).

Functional level in myelomeningocele

LevelMuscles workingUsual mobility
Thoracic and high lumbarTrunk; hip flexors and adductors at most; no quadricepsStands and walks in childhood only with hip-spanning orthoses (reciprocating gait orthosis or HKAFO); most adults use a wheelchair
Low lumbarQuadriceps and medial hamstrings; no gluteus medius or maximusWalks with AFOs and crutches; about 80% stay community walkers as adults
SacralQuadriceps and gluteus medius; calf muscles weak in high sacral levelsHigh sacral: AFOs, gluteal lurch; low sacral: near-normal walking without orthoses

The level of the nerve damage, not the state of the hip, decides walking ability most (Swaroop and Dias, 2009).

Types of hydrocephalus (W19)

  • Non-communicating (obstructive): CSF is blocked inside the ventricles, for example aqueduct stenosis, Chiari II malformation, tumours.
  • Communicating: CSF flows out of the ventricles but is not absorbed, for example after bleeding in a premature baby or after meningitis.
  • Congenital (with spina bifida, aqueduct stenosis, Dandy–Walker malformation) or acquired; normal-pressure hydrocephalus in older adults.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Spina bifida is multifactorial: low folate, a previous affected pregnancy, maternal diabetes and obesity, some antiepileptic drugs (valproate), high fever in early pregnancy, and genes.
  • India: neural tube defects occur in about 4.5 per 1000 births in pooled studies, higher than in many countries (Allagh et al., 2015).
  • Prevention: folic acid 400 micrograms daily from at least a month before conception until 12 weeks of pregnancy; a higher dose (4–5 mg) on medical advice after an affected pregnancy; food fortification.
  • Hydrocephalus: Chiari II malformation with myelomeningocele, aqueduct stenosis, bleeding in premature babies, meningitis, tumours.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. The exposed cord is damaged before and at birth, so muscles below the level are paralysed and flaccid and the skin is numb; some children also have spasticity.
  2. Working muscles above the level pull without opposition, and posture adds to it, causing deformities: at L3–L4, hip flexors and adductors pull the hip into flexion and adduction and can dislocate it; at low lumbar levels, a working tibialis anterior with a paralysed calf pulls the foot into calcaneus.
  3. The sacral roots (S2–S4) are affected in almost all, so the bladder and bowel are neurogenic.
  4. The Chiari II malformation blocks CSF flow, causing hydrocephalus; about 8 in 10 babies repaired after birth need a shunt in the first year (MOMS trial).
  5. Numb skin under pressure breaks down; unused bones become thin and break easily, especially after a cast.
  6. As the child grows, the repaired cord can stay stuck (tethered) and be stretched, causing new loss.

5Clinical features

  • A sac on the back, usually lumbar or lumbosacral; paralysis and sensory loss below the level; neurogenic bladder and bowel; hydrocephalus.
  • Learning, attention and hand-coordination difficulties are common when there is hydrocephalus.

Clinical features of hydrocephalus (W19, S20, W20)

  • Infants: a head growing too fast (crossing the head-circumference centiles), a full or bulging anterior fontanelle, widened sutures, prominent scalp veins, "setting-sun" eyes (looking down), irritability, vomiting, poor feeding, drowsiness, a high-pitched cry, stiff legs, delayed milestones, seizures.
  • Older children and adults: headache (worse in the morning), vomiting, drowsiness, swelling of the optic disc, double vision, poor balance.
  • Normal-pressure hydrocephalus in older adults: walking difficulty, memory loss and urinary incontinence.

Tethered cord: signs of new deterioration

Back pain that worsens with activity, new weakness or loss of walking, changing foot shape, increasing scoliosis, and new bladder or bowel problems. Report these at once.

6Investigations

  • Before birth: maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein, the anomaly ultrasound scan, and sometimes amniotic fluid tests.
  • After birth: MRI of the brain and spine (Chiari malformation, hydrocephalus, tethering), cranial ultrasound in infants, serial head circumference, X-rays of the spine and hips.
  • Bladder: kidney ultrasound and urodynamic studies.
  • Physiotherapy: repeated muscle charting and sensory testing to fix the level and pick up any change.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Closure of the back in the first days of life to prevent infection. In some centres the defect is repaired before birth: in the MOMS trial this cut the need for a shunt from 82% to 40% and improved walking at 30 months, at the cost of more premature births.
  • Hydrocephalus: a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt, or endoscopic third ventriculostomy. Shunts can block, become infected or drain too much.
  • Bladder and bowel: clean intermittent catheterisation, medicines, a bowel routine (see bladder and bowel).
  • Orthopaedics: clubfoot casting or surgery; contracture releases; for the hip, the aim is a level pelvis and free movement rather than putting a dislocated hip back; spinal fusion for severe curves; untethering surgery for a tethered cord.

9PT assessment

  • History: pregnancy and birth, closure, shunt and other surgery, milestones, bladder and bowel routine, school.
  • Muscle chart of the legs and trunk, and sensory testing (touch and pin), to find the motor and sensory level; tone (any spasticity).
  • Range of motion and deformity: hips (flexion contracture, adduction, stability), knees, feet, spine; limb lengths.
  • Head circumference and shunt signs in infants; skin.
  • Motor development and mobility: head control, sitting, rolling, crawling, standing, walking with orthoses, wheelchair skills.
  • Upper limb strength and coordination; cognition and attention; family and home.

10PT diagnosis by ICF

No spina bifida long case was set in 2016–2026, so this is a typical lumbar case (as in W22) for practice.

Practice case (typical presentation)

A 4-year-old boy has a myelomeningocele at L4, closed at birth, and a VP shunt. His hip flexors, adductors and quadriceps work; his glutei and calf muscles do not; sensation is absent below the knees. He walks short distances with AFOs and a walker, has a red mark on his left heel, is catheterised by his mother, and attends nursery school.

Spot the ICF clues before you write

L4, quadriceps working: low lumbar level, so AFOs and crutches are realistic and he should stay a walker. no glutei: hip flexion–adduction pull, so watch the hips. sensation absent below the knees and red mark on his heel: pressure-sore risk. VP shunt: know the warning signs. nursery school: plan mobility and toileting there.

12PT management

By age

  • Newborn, before closure: prone or side lying to protect the sac; gentle handling; record which muscles move.
  • After closure (infancy): positioning, gentle range of motion, teaching parents to handle and carry; development: head control, prone play, rolling, sitting, arm strength.
  • Toddler: a standing frame from about 12 months (or once head control develops); a parapodium or reciprocating gait orthosis from about 2 years in high lesions; AFOs or KAFOs with a walker in lumbar lesions.
  • School age: gait training with orthoses and crutches; wheelchair skills for distances; strength and fitness; independence in self-care and, later, self-catheterisation.
  • Adolescence and adulthood: keep walking where possible, add a wheelchair when needed; weight control, skin care and sport.

Answer for W22: physiotherapy of a lumbar myelomeningocele

  1. Assess the motor and sensory level by muscle charting; hips, knees, feet and spine; skin.
  2. Strengthen working muscles (hip flexors and adductors, quadriceps, trunk) and the arms and shoulders for crutches and transfers.
  3. Prevent deformity: prone lying, stretching of hip flexors and adductors, hamstrings and calf; positioning; night splints.
  4. Orthoses by the muscles working: no quadriceps (thoracic, high lumbar): reciprocating gait orthosis or HKAFO with a walker, plus a wheelchair; weak quadriceps: KAFO; strong quadriceps (low lumbar, as at L4): AFOs with crutches; sacral: AFOs or none.
  5. Gait training: standing balance, walker then crutches (swing-through or four-point), stairs, falls.
  6. Wheelchair for long distances and wheelchair skills.
  7. Skin, bladder and latex care; parent and school education; watch for shunt and tethered cord signs.

Physiotherapy for hydrocephalus (7 marks in W21)

  • Handling: always support the heavy head and neck when lifting and carrying; teach parents.
  • Positioning: change the head position every few hours to prevent scalp pressure sores and flattening; avoid long pressure over the shunt valve; supported side lying and prone on a wedge.
  • Development: head control (prone on elbows, supported sitting), rolling, sitting balance, reaching, standing in a frame; follow the developmental sequence.
  • Tone and range: stretching and positioning for stiff legs; strengthening of weak muscles.
  • Sensory, visual and cognitive stimulation through play; adapted seating with head support.
  • Education: shunt warning signs (vomiting, drowsiness, irritability, bulging fontanelle, fast head growth, fever) and when to seek help.

Classic answer vs current guidance: the dislocated hip

Classic

Reduce every dislocated hip surgically so the child can walk.

Current

Aim for a level pelvis and free hip movement rather than an X-ray-perfect hip; walking depends mainly on the neurological level (Swaroop and Dias, 2009).

Write in the exam

"Prevent hip flexion–adduction contracture and keep the pelvis level; plan orthoses and walking by the level."

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Use only latex-free gloves and equipment.
  • Stop and refer for shunt warning signs or new weakness, pain or bladder change (tethered cord).
  • Thin bones: gentle range of motion, no forceful stretching; avoid crawling for 3–4 weeks after a period of immobilisation.
  • No hot packs or heat on numb skin; check the skin after every orthosis session.
  • Before closure, never put pressure on the sac.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Daily positioning, prone lying, stretches and a standing programme.
  • Skin checks, orthosis wear and care, footwear.
  • Bladder and bowel routine; latex avoidance; healthy weight.
  • Shunt and tethered cord warning signs.
  • Folic acid before and in any future pregnancy.
  • School inclusion: accessible classroom, toilet access, a wheelchair for distances.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Walking depends mainly on the level: about 80% of children with low lumbar lesions stay community walkers as adults, while most with thoracic or high lumbar lesions use a wheelchair as adults (Swaroop and Dias, 2009).
  • Hydrocephalus, shunt problems, a tethered cord and obesity can reduce function; most children now survive into adulthood.
  • Discharge from an episode when goals are met, orthoses fit, the family runs the home programme and knows the warning signs; follow-up continues through growth.

3 mEnumerate the types of spina bifida. (S25)

Spina bifida occulta (bony gap, skin closed); meningocele (sac of meninges and CSF, cord in place); myelomeningocele (cord and roots in an open sac, the commonest serious type); also lipomyelomeningocele and myeloschisis.

3 mWrite the clinical features of hydrocephalus. (S20, W20)

In infants: rapidly growing head, bulging fontanelle, widened sutures, prominent scalp veins, setting-sun eyes, irritability, vomiting, poor feeding, drowsiness, high-pitched cry, stiff legs, delayed milestones, seizures. In older children: morning headache, vomiting, drowsiness, papilloedema.

7 mDescribe the musculoskeletal deformities in infantile spina bifida and its physiotherapy management. (W17, 2 + 5)

Deformities (2): spine: scoliosis, kyphosis; hip: flexion contracture, flexion–adduction with dislocation; knee: flexion contracture or valgus; feet: clubfoot, vertical talus, calcaneus, cavovarus. Physiotherapy (5): muscle charting for the level; positioning and handling; prone lying and stretching of hip flexors, adductors, hamstrings and calves; assisting clubfoot casting and splints; developmental training; standing frame from about 12 months; orthoses and gait training by level; strengthening of working muscles and arms; skin care, latex precautions and parent education on shunt and tethered cord signs.

LAQPractice: a 4-year-old boy with an L4 myelomeningocele and a VP shunt walks short distances with AFOs and a walker and has a heel pressure mark. Write ICF with clinical reasoning, goals and physiotherapy. (5 + 3 + 7)

ICF (5): the table in section 10 with the one-line diagnosis. Goals (3): short term: walks at home with AFOs and walker, starts crutches, heel healed, full hip extension; long term: walks at nursery with AFOs and crutches, wheelchair for distance, no sores, level pelvis. Physiotherapy (7): the W22 answer in section 12 adapted to him, plus play-based training and education of parents and nursery staff.
