5B(4)Obstetric brachial plexus injury: types, differential diagnosis and physiotherapy

Obstetric brachial plexus palsy came as a long case once (S19): a baby who has not moved one arm since a forceps delivery. The examiner wants three things: rule out the conditions that mimic it, refer early enough for surgery if the biceps does not recover, and teach the parents a home programme that keeps the shoulder from stiffening into internal rotation.

My revision

1Definition

Obstetric (neonatal) brachial plexus palsy is weakness of an infant's arm caused by stretching or tearing of the C5–T1 nerve roots during birth. Reported rates vary widely, from about 0.4 to 5 per 1000 births.

The anatomy, nerve degrees and adult injuries are on the nerve injuries page.

2Classification and grading

Narakas groupRootsPicture
IC5–C6 (Erb's)No shoulder abduction or elbow flexion; hand works; the commonest and best outlook
IIC5–C7As above, plus weak wrist and finger extension ("waiter's tip" with a dropped wrist)
IIIC5–T1Flail arm, no Horner's syndrome
IVC5–T1 with Horner'sFlail arm with drooping eyelid and small pupil: T1 root avulsion; the worst outlook
  • Active Movement Scale (0–7): grades each movement in infants, with and without gravity.
  • Mallet score (in children who can follow commands, about 3 years and over): abduction, external rotation, hand to neck, hand to back and hand to mouth, each graded 1–5.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Shoulder dystocia: the shoulder gets stuck, and traction on the head stretches the upper roots.
  • Large baby; maternal diabetes; prolonged or difficult labour; forceps or vacuum delivery; breech delivery.
  • Some cases occur after a normal delivery or a caesarean section.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Traction stretches (neurapraxia), ruptures or avulses the roots; the upper roots are hit most often.
  2. Because the deltoid, spinati and biceps are paralysed while the pectoralis major and subscapularis keep working, the shoulder is pulled into internal rotation.
  3. Because the infant's shoulder joint is still forming, a persistent internal rotation pull deforms the glenoid and can subluxate the humeral head backwards (glenohumeral dysplasia).
  4. Because the infant does not use the arm, the brain may "forget" it (developmental disregard), even when the nerves recover.
  5. The arm may grow shorter and smaller; recovering nerves can cause muscles to fire together (co-contraction).
Recall check. Why is external rotation range the most important thing to protect in an infant with Erb's palsy?

5Clinical features

  • Erb's posture: arm by the side, internally rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated ("waiter's tip"); the hand moves in group I.
  • Moro reflex absent on the affected side; grasp reflex present in Erb's palsy but absent in a total palsy.
  • Total palsy: flail, numb arm; Horner's syndrome if T1 is avulsed.
  • Breathing difficulty if the phrenic nerve (C3–C5) is involved.
  • Later: internal rotation contracture, posterior shoulder subluxation, elbow flexion contracture, winging of the scapula, a shorter arm, the "trumpet sign" (the elbow lifts when the child brings the hand to the mouth).

6Investigations

  • X-ray of the clavicle, humerus and shoulder to rule out a fracture.
  • Ultrasound of the shoulder to check for dysplasia and subluxation.
  • MRI (or CT myelography) for root avulsion when surgery is considered.
  • Nerve studies help, but in infants they can look better than the clinical picture.
  • Chest X-ray or ultrasound of the diaphragm if breathing is affected.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Refer early: every infant to a brachial plexus clinic by 1 month of age, with management started before 3 months.
  • Nerve surgery (grafts, nerve transfers) is considered when the biceps has not recovered by about 3 months (or the Toronto test score is under 3.5), or when the child fails the "cookie test" (bringing a biscuit to the mouth with the arm held at the side) by about 9 months. A total palsy with Horner's syndrome is explored early. Infants who regain antigravity biceps by 4–5 months do about as well as those who have surgery.
  • Later surgery: release of tight internal rotators, tendon transfers for external rotation, or a rotation osteotomy of the humerus.
  • Botulinum toxin into tight internal rotators, with casting, in selected children.

8Complications and prevention

9PT assessment

  • History: birth weight, delivery (forceps, shoulder dystocia), breathing problems, milestones, parents' concerns.
  • Observation: resting posture, spontaneous movement, use in play, Horner's signs.
  • Movement: Active Movement Scale for each joint; Moro and grasp reflexes.
  • Range: passive external rotation with the arm at the side and in abduction (compare sides), abduction, elbow extension, supination.
  • Sensation: reaction to touch and pinprick.
  • Development: head control, rolling, sitting, weight-bearing through the arms, crawling.
  • Mallet score once the child can follow commands.

12PT management

Principles: refer early; protect the shoulder; keep full range, especially external rotation; get the infant to use the arm through play; train the parents.

By phase

  • First 1–2 weeks: gentle handling; support the arm; do not pull on it; dress the affected arm first and undress it last; position the arm supported, not trapped under the body. Rigid splinting in the newborn period is discouraged.
  • Early home programme: gentle passive range several times a day (for example at each nappy change): shoulder external rotation with the arm at the side and the scapula held, abduction and flexion, elbow extension, forearm supination, wrist and finger extension. Sensory input with touch, massage and textures.
  • Active use through play: toys placed to make the infant reach with the affected arm; hand-to-mouth play; prone on elbows and four-point weight-bearing; sitting with arm support; crawling.
  • Older infants and children: constraint of the good arm for short play periods if the child ignores the weak arm; splints (resting wrist splint, elbow extension splint) for specific problems; serial casting or botulinum toxin for contractures under specialist care; rehabilitation after surgery as the surgeon advises.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Never force a movement, especially external rotation and abduction; hold the scapula still while moving the shoulder.
  • Do not lift the baby by the arms or pull on the affected arm.
  • Stop and refer if the shoulder clunks, range is lost, or the arm swells or seems painful (fracture, dislocation).
  • Protect a numb hand from biting and scratching.

14Parent education and home programme

  • Explain the injury without blame; many infants recover well, and early referral matters.
  • Teach handling, dressing, positioning and the daily range-of-motion routine, with pictures.
  • Play ideas that use the arm; praise all attempts.
  • Keep all clinic appointments; record when new movements appear (for example the first time the hand reaches the mouth).

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Many infants recover well, especially with upper plexus (C5–C6) injuries; reported rates vary between studies.
  • Good signs: Erb's palsy only; antigravity biceps by about 3 months.
  • Poor signs: total palsy; Horner's syndrome (T1 avulsion); no biceps recovery by 3–6 months.
  • Even after good recovery, external rotation and abduction often stay limited, so the shoulder is reviewed until the child stops growing.

Answer skeleton

  1. [Differential diagnosis, 5] Obstetric plexus palsy (group by clinical picture) vs fracture, septic shoulder, hemiplegic CP, cord injury; tests that separate them.
  2. [Goals, 3–4] Short-term: full external rotation, active use in play, parents trained. Long-term: hand to mouth and reaching, two-handed play, no shoulder deformity.
  3. [Management, 6–7] Refer to a plexus clinic (surgery if no biceps by 3 months); handling and positioning; home range-of-motion programme; play-based active use and weight-bearing; constraint and splints later; precautions; parent education.

3 mWrite the Narakas classification of obstetric brachial plexus palsy.

I: C5–C6 (Erb's). II: C5–C7 (with wrist and finger drop). III: C5–T1, flail arm without Horner's. IV: C5–T1 with Horner's syndrome (T1 avulsion), the worst outlook.

7 mPhysiotherapy management of a baby with Erb's palsy.

Refer to a plexus clinic by 1 month. Gentle handling and positioning; no pulling on the arm. Home programme several times a day: external rotation with the scapula held, abduction, flexion, elbow extension, supination, wrist and finger extension. Play-based active use, hand-to-mouth play, weight-bearing in prone and sitting. Sensory input. Constraint of the good arm and splints later if needed. Surgery if the biceps has not recovered by about 3 months. Educate the parents and review the shoulder regularly.

MCQHorner's syndrome in a baby with a total plexus palsy suggests:A. A clavicle fractureB. Avulsion of the T1 rootC. Upper trunk stretch onlyD. Cerebral palsy

MCQThe most important range to maintain in an infant with Erb's palsy is:A. Shoulder internal rotationB. Shoulder external rotationC. Elbow flexionD. Wrist flexion
