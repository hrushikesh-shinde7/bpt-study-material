5BDevelopmental neurology: motor milestones, primitive reflexes and postural reactions

Development came up in 6 papers: gross and fine motor milestones for 7 marks three times, and the ATNR, a primitive reflex, cortical reflexes and the parachute reaction for 3 marks. You need the normal sequence and ages to spot delay in cerebral palsy, Down's syndrome and spina bifida, and to plan treatment in the order a child normally learns to move.

My revision

1Principles of development

  • Development is continuous, from conception to maturity.
  • The sequence is the same in all children, but the rate varies.
  • Cephalocaudal: control comes from head to foot (head control, then sitting, then standing).
  • Proximodistal: from the trunk and shoulders out to the hands and fingers.
  • General to specific: mass movements are replaced by specific, individual actions.
  • Primitive reflexes fade as higher control develops, so that new milestones can appear.
  • For babies born before 37 weeks, use the corrected age (age from birth minus the weeks born early) until about 2 years.

4Primitive reflexes

Automatic responses present at birth, controlled by the brainstem and spinal cord. They fade (are integrated) as the cortex matures, mostly by 4–6 months. A reflex that is absent, asymmetrical, too strong (obligatory) or persists beyond its usual age suggests brain damage, as in cerebral palsy.

Asymmetrical tonic neck reflex (ATNR; 3 marks in S20 and S21)

  • Elicit: child supine, head in midline; turn the head to one side (or watch when the child turns it).
  • Response: the arm (and leg) on the face side extend and the arm (and leg) on the skull side flex: the "fencing" posture.
  • Normal: present from birth, seen intermittently, and integrated by about 6 months, allowing the hands to come to the midline and the child to roll.
  • Abnormal: an obligatory ATNR at any age (the child cannot get out of it), or persistence after about 6 months, suggests brain damage (cerebral palsy).
  • Effects of persistence: cannot bring hands together or to the mouth, cannot roll or sit symmetrically, poor hand–eye coordination, asymmetrical posture with a risk of scoliosis and hip problems, and later difficulty crossing the midline in writing.
  • Physiotherapy: midline positioning (side lying, symmetrical seating, toys in front), approach and play from the front.

Moro reflex in detail (example of "any one primitive reflex", W20)

Elicited by a sudden drop of the supported head; the arms abduct and extend with the hands opening, then adduct and flex. Present from birth, gone by 3–4 months (normal up to 6). Absent: severe brain depression or hypotonia. Asymmetrical: brachial plexus palsy, fractured clavicle or humerus, hemiplegia. Persisting after 6 months: cerebral palsy.

Recall check. Give the response and normal timing of the ATNR, and three effects if it persists.

5Postural reactions

  • Righting reactions: keep the head upright in space and the head, trunk and limbs in line (neck righting, body righting, labyrinthine and optical righting). The Landau reaction (held prone in the air, the head lifts and the trunk and legs extend) appears at about 3 months and is gone by 2 years.
  • Protective (parachute) reactions: the arms shoot out to break a fall.
  • Equilibrium (tilting) reactions: when the support surface tilts, the trunk curves towards the higher side and the limbs move out to keep balance; they persist for life.

Parachute reaction (3 marks in W25)

  • Elicit: hold the child prone around the trunk and move the child suddenly head-first towards a surface.
  • Response: both arms extend and abduct, with the hands open, to protect the head.
  • Timing: the forward reaction appears at about 6–9 months and persists for life; sideways and then backward protective reactions follow over the next months.
  • Abnormal: absent, delayed or asymmetrical (hemiplegic cerebral palsy, brachial plexus palsy, motor delay); without it, sitting and standing are unsafe.
  • Physiotherapy: train protective reactions on a ball, roll or tilt board.

The reflex hierarchy and "cortical reflexes" (W20)

Classic levels (Fiorentino)
LevelReflexes and reactionsWhen
SpinalFlexor withdrawal, extensor thrust, crossed extensionFirst 2 months

Cortical reflexes (3 marks): the equilibrium reactions in each position (prone, supine, four-point kneeling, sitting, kneel-standing, standing), plus the hopping and dorsiflexion reactions. Each appears after the child can get into that position (sitting reactions from about 7–8 months, standing reactions in the second year) and stays for life.

Classic answer vs current guidance: the reflex hierarchy

Classic

Development is the cortex progressively inhibiting lower reflexes; treatment must inhibit primitive reflexes before skills can appear.

Current

Movement emerges from many interacting systems (strength, posture, perception, motivation, environment); reflexes and reactions are useful clinical signs, but treatment practises the functional skills themselves (see motor control).

Write in the exam

Give the classic levels when asked, then add one line that the hierarchy is a teaching model and skills are trained directly.

6Red flags and screening

When to worry (most children do these by the age shown; CDC, 2022)

  • 6 months: rolls from tummy to back; pushes up on straight arms in prone; leans on hands to sit.
  • 9 months: sits without support and gets into sitting alone; moves objects from hand to hand.
  • 12 months: pulls to stand and walks holding furniture; picks up small things with thumb and index finger.
  • 18 months: walks without holding on; scribbles; climbs on and off a chair.
  • 2 years: runs, kicks a ball, walks up a few stairs.
  • 5 years: hops on one foot.

Missing any of these needs a closer developmental check. Other red flags: loss of skills already gained; asymmetry of movement; a clear hand preference before about 12–18 months (weakness of the other hand); very low or high tone; primitive reflexes persisting beyond their age.

Screening and assessment tools

  • Trivandrum Developmental Screening Chart (Indian, simple screening in the first years); Denver II.
  • Alberta Infant Motor Scale (0–18 months), Peabody Developmental Motor Scales, Bayley scales.
  • Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination and General Movements assessment for early detection of cerebral palsy (see cerebral palsy).

7How physiotherapy uses development

  • Assess where the child is in the sequence, not just the age.
  • Train the next skill in the sequence: head control, rolling, sitting, crawling, kneeling, pull to stand, cruising, walking.
  • Use the key positions (prone, supine, side lying, sitting, four-point, kneeling, half-kneeling, standing) for strength, weight bearing and balance.
  • Practise protective and balance reactions once the child can hold a position.
  • Use play, toys and the family's daily routine; repetition matters more than any single technique.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mAsymmetrical tonic neck reflex. (S20, S21)

Turning the head of a supine infant to one side makes the face-side arm and leg extend and the skull-side limbs flex ("fencing" posture). Present from birth, integrated by about 6 months. Obligatory at any age, or persisting after 6 months, suggests cerebral palsy; persistence blocks hand-to-mouth and midline play and rolling, and causes asymmetry.

3 mWrite a note on parachute reactions. (W25)

A protective reaction: the child, held prone and moved suddenly head-first towards a surface, extends and abducts both arms with open hands. Forward appears at about 6–9 months and persists for life, followed by sideways and backward reactions. Absent or asymmetrical in hemiplegia, brachial plexus palsy or delay; trained on a ball or roll.

7 mDiscuss gross motor and fine motor milestones up to age 3 years. (S21)

Principles (one line). Gross motor: 3 months neck holding; 5 months rolls; 6 months sits with support; 8 months sits alone; 9 months stands holding, crawls; 12 months stands alone, walks with support; 15 months walks alone, creeps upstairs; 18 months runs; 2 years walks upstairs two feet per step, jumps, kicks a ball; 3 years alternate feet upstairs, tricycle. Fine motor: 4 months bidextrous reach; 6 months unidextrous reach, transfers by 6–7 months; 9 months immature pincer; 12 months mature pincer; 15 months tower of 2, imitates scribble; 18 months tower of 3, scribbles, spoon; 2 years tower of 6, strokes; 3 years tower of 9, copies a circle.

LAQPractice: a baby born at 32 weeks is now 9 months old and cannot sit alone. How would you assess development, what would worry you, and what would you advise? (5 + 5 + 5)

Assess (5): corrected age is 7 months, so sitting alone is not yet overdue (WHO range up to about 9 months); check head control, rolling, prone on extended arms, supported sitting, reaching and transfer, tone, primitive reflexes (Moro, ATNR should be fading) and early reactions; use the Alberta Infant Motor Scale. Worry (5): no rolling, poor head control, persistent or obligatory ATNR, asymmetry, very high or low tone, early hand preference, loss of skills; refer for full assessment (Hammersmith examination). Advise (5): daily tummy time, supported sitting play with toys in front, reaching in side lying, rolling games, and review in 6–8 weeks.
