5A(g)Multiple sclerosis: types, features, EDSS, ICF and physiotherapy

Multiple sclerosis was a long case in 6 papers, and the same 29-year-old executive after her second relapse was asked three times. Short questions ask for features, Uhthoff's phenomenon, Charcot's triad and the stages of the disease. Every answer turns on two facts: symptoms come and go with relapses and heat, and exercise is safe and helpful at every level of disability if it is paced around fatigue.

My revision

1Definition

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system in which patches of inflammation strip the myelin from nerve fibres in the brain, spinal cord and optic nerves, leaving scars (plaques) and later axonal loss. The lesions are scattered in space (different parts of the CNS) and in time (new lesions at different times).

  • About 2.8 million people live with MS worldwide; the average age at diagnosis is 32, and women are affected about twice as often as men (Atlas of MS, 2020).
  • It is less common in India than in Europe and North America, where it is the commonest non-traumatic cause of disability in young adults.

2Classification and grading

Disease courses ("stages of MS": 7 marks in S20)

CourseWhat happens
Clinically isolated syndrome (CIS)A first attack (for example optic neuritis) that may become MS
Relapsing-remitting (RRMS)Relapses lasting days to weeks, followed by full or partial recovery; stable between relapses
Secondary progressive (SPMS)After an initial relapsing course, disability increases steadily, with or without relapses
Primary progressive (PPMS)Steady worsening from the start, often a slowly progressive walking difficulty

Each course is also described as active or not active (new relapses or MRI lesions) and progressing or not (Lublin et al., 2014).

Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS, Kurtzke)

ScoreMeaning
0Normal neurological examination
1.0–3.5Mild to moderate signs; walks fully
4.0–5.5Walks without aid, but the distance falls: about 500 m at 4.0, 100 m at 5.5
6.0Needs a stick or one-sided support to walk 100 m
6.5Needs support on both sides to walk 20 m

10Death due to MS

3Causes and risk factors

  • Epstein–Barr virus infection (the strongest known environmental risk factor).
  • Low vitamin D and little sunlight (more MS far from the equator); smoking; obesity in adolescence.
  • Genes (for example HLA-DRB1*15:01); female sex.
  • Relapses may be triggered by infections and the period after childbirth.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Immune cells cross the blood–brain barrier and attack myelin, causing inflammation and plaques, mainly near the ventricles, in the brainstem and cerebellum, the spinal cord and the optic nerves.
  2. Because demyelinated fibres conduct slowly or not at all, symptoms appear according to the site: weakness, numbness, visual loss, ataxia, bladder problems.
  3. Because inflammation settles and some remyelination occurs, symptoms improve after a relapse (remission).
  4. Because axons are lost over time, disability gradually builds up and does not recover (progression).
  5. Because heat slows conduction further in demyelinated fibres, symptoms worsen temporarily with a rise in body temperature (Uhthoff's phenomenon).
Recall check. Why do symptoms improve after a relapse but disability still builds up over years?

5Clinical features

Asked for 3 marks in W16 and S22 and with physiotherapy for 7 marks in W20.

  • Sensory: numbness, tingling, band-like feelings; Lhermitte's sign (an electric shock down the spine on bending the neck).
  • Visual: optic neuritis (painful eye movement, blurred central vision, faded colours); double vision; nystagmus.
  • Motor: weakness and spasticity, usually in the legs; UMN signs.
  • Cerebellar: ataxia, intention tremor, dysarthria.
  • Fatigue: very common and often the most disabling symptom.
  • Bladder and bowel: urgency, frequency, incomplete emptying, constipation; sexual dysfunction.
  • Cognitive and emotional: slowed thinking, poor memory and attention; depression, anxiety, emotional lability.
  • Pain (including trigeminal neuralgia) and heat sensitivity.
Uhthoff's phenomenon (3 marks in W22) and Charcot's triad (3 marks in W25)

Uhthoff's phenomenon: a temporary worsening of symptoms (often blurred vision or weakness) when body temperature rises, as with exercise, a hot bath, hot weather or fever. Heat slows conduction in demyelinated fibres; symptoms settle when the person cools down. It is not a relapse.

Charcot's triad in MS: nystagmus, intention tremor and scanning (staccato) speech. These are cerebellar and brainstem signs.

Visual, cognitive and behavioural disturbances (7 marks in W18)

AreaFeaturesWhat the physiotherapist does
VisualOptic neuritis, double vision, nystagmus, blurred or jumping vision, poor colour visionGood lighting, high-contrast markings on steps, uncluttered spaces; refer for eye care; gaze stability exercises if appropriate
CognitiveSlow processing, poor memory, attention and planningShort, simple instructions; written programmes; practise in quiet surroundings; avoid dual tasks at first

6Investigations

  • MRI of brain and spine: plaques near the ventricles, in the cortex, brainstem, cerebellum and cord.
  • Diagnosis (McDonald criteria, 2024 revision): typical lesions in at least two of five regions (around the ventricles, cortex or just beneath it, brainstem and cerebellum, spinal cord, and now the optic nerve). Proof of new lesions over time is no longer always needed. CSF oligoclonal bands, or a raised kappa free light chain index, support the diagnosis.
  • Visual evoked potentials: slowed in optic nerve involvement.
  • Blood tests exclude mimics (B12 deficiency, neuromyelitis optica antibodies).

7Medical and surgical management

  • Relapse: high-dose methylprednisolone for 3–5 days speeds recovery; it does not change the long-term course.
  • Disease-modifying therapy to reduce relapses and new lesions: interferon beta, glatiramer acetate, teriflunomide, dimethyl fumarate, fingolimod, natalizumab, ocrelizumab and others. Ocrelizumab is also used in primary progressive MS.
  • Symptoms: baclofen or tizanidine for spasticity; fampridine for walking speed in some; drugs for bladder, pain and depression; botulinum toxin for focal spasticity.

8Complications and prevention

ComplicationWhat to look forPhysio role
FallsWeakness, spasticity, ataxia, poor vision, fatigue, heatBalance and strength training, aids, home safety

9PT assessment

  • History: course of MS, date of last relapse, medicines, fatigue pattern through the day, heat sensitivity, bladder, falls, work and home.
  • Disability: EDSS.
  • Motor: strength, tone (Modified Ashworth Scale), range, coordination (finger-nose, heel-shin).
  • Sensory, vision and cognition screening.
  • Balance and gait: Berg Balance Scale, Timed 25-Foot Walk, Timed Up and Go, 6-minute walk, 12-item MS Walking Scale.
  • Fatigue: Modified Fatigue Impact Scale or Fatigue Severity Scale.
  • Upper limb: Nine-Hole Peg Test.

Worked example from a real paper (W19, S24 and S25; 7 + 8 marks)

"Write ICF and physiotherapy management with long and short term goals for a 29 year old unmarried female executive working in a multinational company, diagnosed as a case of multiple sclerosis 2 years back. She is suffering from a second relapse. She was discharged from the hospital 1 week back. She is moderately functionally dependent in transfers and hygiene."

Spot the ICF clues before you write

second relapse means relapsing-remitting MS, recovering now. discharged 1 week back means early recovery after steroids: start gently and grade up. moderately dependent in transfers and hygiene is the activity limitation. executive in a multinational company means a desk job with travel and long hours: plan fatigue management and return to work.

12PT management

Principles: exercise is safe and helpful at every level; grade it to the person's EDSS and fatigue; keep cool; train balance and function; pace activity; support work and family life (7 marks in S19 and W20).

By phase and stage

  • During a relapse: rest the affected function, keep range, position well, and maintain gentle activity as tolerated; avoid overheating.
  • Recovery after a relapse: grade back up: transfers, balance, walking, strength and endurance; short sessions with rests.
  • Mild to moderate disability (EDSS 0–6.5): aerobic and resistance training at the doses below; balance and gait training; fatigue management; work support.
  • Severe disability (EDSS 7–9): short daily sessions; upper-body exercise; standing frame; transfers; seating and pressure care; breathing; carer training.

Exercise doses (Kalb et al., 2020)

DisabilityAerobicResistance
EDSS 0–6.52–3 times a week, 10–30 minutes at moderate intensity (40–60% of maximum heart rate, or perceived exertion 11–13 on the 6–20 scale)2–3 times a week; 1–3 sets of 8–15 repetitions; 5–10 exercises
EDSS 7.0–9.0Up to 20 minutes a day (less at higher scores), 3–7 days a week, with rests; arm exercise in short intervalsUpper body 3 times a week, 3 sets of 10 as able

Overall target for everyone: at least 150 minutes a week of exercise or of everyday physical activity, reached gradually.

Classic answer vs current evidence: exercise in MS

Classic

Avoid exertion and heat because exercise worsens MS and fatigue.

Current

Exercise and lifestyle physical activity are safe for people with MS and should be promoted at every disability level. Heat may briefly worsen symptoms in heat-sensitive people, so cool them rather than stop exercise (Kalb et al., 2020).

Write in the exam

"Graded aerobic and resistance exercise 2–3 times a week, with pacing and cooling, aiming at 150 minutes of activity a week."

Other techniques

  • Spasticity: daily stretching, standing, positioning; check triggers (bladder, skin, infection).
  • Balance and gait: static and dynamic balance, stepping, turning, walking with a second task when safe; AFO for foot drop; walking aids as needed.
  • Ataxia and intention tremor: proximal stability, Frenkel's exercises, slow controlled practice with visual feedback; weighted cuffs on the wrist may reduce tremor for some tasks.
  • Fine motor control (S18 case): grip and pinch strength, task practice (writing, buttoning, eating) with adapted pens and cutlery.
  • Fatigue: energy conservation (plan, prioritise, pace, sit for tasks), rest breaks, exercise at the coolest time of day.
  • Heat: pre-cooling (cool drink, cool shower, fan), cool room, cooling vest; avoid hot packs and hot baths.
  • Bladder: timed voiding and pelvic floor training (see the bladder page).

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Avoid overheating: cool room, cool water, breaks; no hot packs on numb skin.
  • Fatigue: stop before exhaustion; if the person is worse the next day, reduce the dose.
  • A new symptom lasting more than 24 hours without fever or heat may be a relapse: refer.
  • Fever or urine infection can cause a pseudo-relapse; treat the cause.
  • Falls: guard during balance work; check vision and footwear.
  • After steroid courses: bones may be weaker; watch blood sugar and mood.

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • MS is not contagious or inherited in a simple way; most people continue to work and live full lives.
  • Daily home exercise plus everyday activity, building towards 150 minutes a week.
  • Energy conservation at home and at work: sit for tasks, plan the day, rest before tiredness, keep things within reach.
  • Keep cool in summer; avoid very hot baths.
  • Workplace adjustments: flexible hours, home working, a seat near the toilet, a cool office, less travel.
  • Stop smoking; healthy weight; take disease-modifying medicines regularly; report new symptoms.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • The course varies widely. Better outlook: female, young onset, sensory or optic onset, good recovery from the first relapse, few early relapses.
  • Poorer outlook: older onset, motor or cerebellar onset, frequent early relapses, a progressive course from the start.
  • Disease-modifying medicines have reduced relapses and slowed disability for many people.
  • Physiotherapy is given in blocks after relapses and when function changes; discharge from a block when goals are met and the person exercises on their own.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWhat is Uhthoff's phenomenon?

A temporary worsening of MS symptoms with a rise in body temperature (exercise, hot bath, hot weather, fever), because heat slows conduction in demyelinated fibres. It settles on cooling and is not a relapse. Manage with pre-cooling, a cool room and breaks.

3 mDescribe Charcot's triad in multiple sclerosis.

Nystagmus, intention tremor and scanning (staccato) speech, from cerebellar and brainstem lesions.

7 mDescribe the stages of multiple sclerosis and physiotherapy for any one stage.

Stages: clinically isolated syndrome; relapsing-remitting (relapses with recovery); secondary progressive (steady worsening after a relapsing phase); primary progressive (worsening from the start). Disability stages by EDSS: mild (0–4), moderate (4.5–6.5), severe (7 or more).

Physiotherapy for relapsing-remitting MS with mild to moderate disability: aerobic exercise 2–3 times a week for 10–30 minutes at moderate intensity; resistance training 2–3 times a week, 1–3 sets of 8–15; balance and gait training; stretching for spasticity; energy conservation and cooling; a home programme towards 150 minutes a week; graded return after relapses.

LAQA 23-year-old engineering student with relapsing-remitting MS for 3 years walks with support and has difficulty grooming, writing and using a spoon. Write short- and long-term goals and physiotherapy for his impairments. (6 + 9, S18)

Goals (6): short-term: steadier walking with a stick; better grip and control for writing and eating with adapted tools; manage fatigue during college hours. Long-term: walks on campus without an aid or with one stick; writes for a full exam; independent grooming; exercises 150 minutes a week.

Physiotherapy (9): aerobic and resistance training at the Kalb doses; balance and gait training; proximal stability and Frenkel's exercises for ataxia; fine motor practice with thick pens, weighted cutlery and task repetition; stretching for spasticity; energy conservation and cooling; travel planning on public transport; a home programme and review after any relapse.

MCQA person with MS develops blurred vision after a hot shower that clears in an hour. This is:A. A new relapseB. Uhthoff's phenomenonC. Lhermitte's signD. Optic atrophy

B. Heat-related, temporary and not a relapse.

MCQThe most common course of MS at onset is:A. Primary progressiveB. Secondary progressiveC. Relapsing-remittingD. Progressive-relapsing

C. About 85% start as relapsing-remitting MS.

MCQFor a person with MS at EDSS 3, a suitable aerobic dose is:A. 10–30 minutes, 2–3 times a week, moderate intensityB. 60 minutes daily at maximal effortC. No aerobic exerciseD. Only passive movements

A. Kalb et al. (2020) for EDSS 0–6.5.
