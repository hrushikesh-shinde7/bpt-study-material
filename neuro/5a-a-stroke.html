5A(a)Stroke: types, arterial syndromes, assessment and physiotherapy

Stroke was in 18 of the 20 papers (2016–2026): a long case in 11 of them, and a 7-mark question on hemiplegic gait, the hemiplegic shoulder, the spastic hand, the acute stage, motor relearning or the circle of Willis. Every answer follows one path: where the lesion is, what it does to movement and daily life, and the task practice that drives recovery.

My revision

1Definition

  • Stroke (WHO): rapidly developing clinical signs of focal (or global) disturbance of brain function, lasting more than 24 hours or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than a vascular one.
  • Current tissue-based definition (AHA/ASA, 2013): death of brain, spinal cord or retinal cells caused by ischaemia, shown on imaging or pathology, or by symptoms lasting 24 hours or more; bleeding into the brain or subarachnoid space is included.
  • Transient ischaemic attack (TIA): a short episode of neurological dysfunction from focal ischaemia without infarction on imaging. A TIA is a warning: the risk of stroke is highest in the next few days.
  • Hemiplegia is paralysis of one side of the body; hemiparesis is weakness of one side. Both are on the side opposite the brain lesion, because the motor pathway crosses in the medulla.

2Classification and the cerebral circulation

By pathology

Ischaemic: thromboticClot on an atherosclerotic plaque in a large arteryOnset during sleep or rest; stepwise worsening; earlier TIAs

Haemorrhagic: intracerebralSmall artery ruptures into brain tissue (hypertension)Headache, vomiting, falling consciousness, raised BP
Haemorrhagic: subarachnoidAneurysm bursts into the space around the brain"Worst headache of my life", neck stiffness

In Western series about 85% of strokes are ischaemic. Indian registries record a larger share of haemorrhages, about a fifth to a third of all strokes.

By time course and recovery phase

  • Classic terms: TIA, stroke in evolution (still worsening), completed stroke.
  • Recovery phases (Stroke Recovery and Rehabilitation Roundtable, 2017): hyperacute 0–24 hours; acute 1–7 days; early subacute 7 days to 3 months; late subacute 3–6 months; chronic after 6 months.

The circle of Willis (7 marks in S20)

For the 7-mark answer: draw the ring; list its vessels; say what it supplies (forebrain from the carotids, brainstem, cerebellum and occipital lobes from the vertebrobasilar system); and give its function: a safety connection between the right and left sides and between the front and back circulations. Most aneurysms causing subarachnoid haemorrhage arise at its junctions, especially the anterior communicating artery.

Arterial syndromes (types of stroke, 3 marks in S26)

ArteryMain features (opposite side unless stated)

Oxfordshire (Bamford) classification by clinical picture: total anterior circulation (weakness, field loss and a higher cortical deficit together), partial anterior circulation (two of the three, or a cortical deficit alone), lacunar, and posterior circulation strokes.

3Causes and risk factors

  • Non-modifiable: older age, male sex, family history, previous stroke or TIA.
  • Modifiable: in the INTERSTROKE study (32 countries, 2016), ten factors accounted for about 90% of stroke risk: hypertension (the most important), smoking, diabetes, physical inactivity, poor diet, psychosocial stress and depression, abdominal obesity, alcohol, cardiac causes such as atrial fibrillation, and abnormal blood lipids.
  • Causes to think of in a young patient: rheumatic heart disease, cerebral venous thrombosis (for example after childbirth), tuberculous meningitis with vasculitis, arterial dissection, drug misuse, clotting disorders.

Indian context: in the ICMR hospital-based stroke registry (30 hospitals, 2020–2022) about 1 in 7 patients was younger than 45, and the average age was about 59. Uncontrolled hypertension, tobacco in any form, and diabetes are the targets in nearly every Indian case.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. Ischaemia: blood flow stops, so the central core of brain tissue dies within minutes. Around it, the penumbra is starved but still alive for some hours; reopening the artery early saves it ("time is brain").
  2. Haemorrhage: the blood clot compresses and irritates the brain and raises intracranial pressure.
  3. Damage to the motor cortex or its pathway (the corticospinal tract through the internal capsule) produces an upper motor neuron syndrome:
    • Negative features: weakness, loss of dexterity and selective movement, fatigue.
    • Positive features: spasticity, brisk reflexes, clonus, Babinski sign, associated reactions, and movement locked into synergy patterns.
    • Adaptive features: stiffness and contracture of muscles kept short; learned non-use of the affected arm; general deconditioning.
  4. Other areas damaged according to the artery: sensation, vision, speech, swallowing, perception (neglect, apraxia, pushing), cognition and mood.
  5. Recovery: swelling settles and nearby or opposite brain areas take over (neuroplasticity). Plasticity is use-dependent: repeated, task-specific practice strengthens the new pathways, and disuse lets them fade. Most spontaneous recovery happens in the first 3 months.
Why does the arm usually recover less than the leg after an MCA stroke?

The MCA supplies the part of the motor cortex controlling the face and arm, while the leg area lies on the inner surface supplied by the ACA. The hand also depends heavily on the direct corticospinal pathway for fine, independent finger movement, whereas walking can use other pathways and both sides of the brain.

5Clinical features

  • Sudden onset; the public warning sign is BE-FAST: Balance, Eyes, Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call for help.
  • Motor: hemiplegia or hemiparesis; tone is often low at first (flaccid stage), then spasticity develops in the antigravity muscles; synergy patterns; brisk reflexes and an extensor plantar response.
  • Sensory and visual: loss of touch and position sense, homonymous hemianopia.
  • Communication and swallowing: aphasia, dysarthria, dysphagia (aspiration risk).
  • Perception and cognition: neglect, apraxia, pusher syndrome, memory and attention problems (see cognition and perception).
  • Others: shoulder pain and subluxation, bladder incontinence, depression, emotionalism, fatigue, central post-stroke pain.

Synergy patterns (flexion synergy: 3 marks in S18)

SynergyComponents
Upper limb flexionScapular retraction and elevation; shoulder abduction and external rotation; elbow flexion (the strongest part); forearm supination; wrist and finger flexion
Upper limb extensionScapular protraction; shoulder adduction and internal rotation (pectoralis major, the strongest part); elbow extension; forearm pronation; wrist and finger flexion
Lower limb flexionHip flexion (the strongest part), abduction and external rotation; knee flexion; ankle dorsiflexion and inversion; toe extension
Lower limb extensionHip extension, adduction and internal rotation; knee extension; ankle plantarflexion and inversion; toe flexion

The typical spastic posture combines the stronger components: arm held in flexion with the shoulder adducted and internally rotated; leg in extension with the foot pointed and turned in.

Pusher syndrome (3 marks in S20 and W24)

The patient pushes with the non-paretic arm and leg towards the paretic side and resists any attempt to correct the posture, so they fall towards the weak side in sitting and standing. It is linked with a disturbed sense of upright body posture, often after right-hemisphere or thalamic lesions, and is graded with the Scale for Contraversive Pushing. Treatment uses vision to show what is vertical (a mirror, a door frame, the therapist's arm), reaching towards the strong side, and sitting or standing with the strong side against a wall or high table, which removes the pushing surface.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Stroke unit care by a specialist team reduces death and dependency for every type of stroke.
  • Ischaemic stroke (AHA/ASA 2026): clot-dissolving drug (tenecteplase or alteplase) within 4.5 hours of onset, and in selected patients up to 9 hours when imaging shows brain still worth saving; mechanical thrombectomy for a blocked large artery, within 6 hours and in selected patients up to 24 hours; antiplatelet drugs (a short course of two antiplatelets after a minor stroke); later statins, blood-pressure control, anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation, and carotid surgery for a tight narrowing.
  • Haemorrhagic stroke: lowering blood pressure, reversing anticoagulants, and surgery when needed (removing the clot, decompressive craniectomy, clipping or coiling an aneurysm).
  • Swallowing: nothing by mouth until a swallow screen is passed.
What this means for physio

After thrombolysis or thrombectomy, follow the unit's protocol for observations and blood-pressure limits before mobilising. After a craniectomy, the side without bone must not take pressure; the patient wears a helmet when mobile.

9PT assessment

Subjective

  • Date and type of stroke, side, hand dominance, treatment given (thrombolysis, surgery); risk factors and medicines.
  • Function before the stroke; job, home (stairs, lift, Indian or Western toilet, floor-level sitting), family support, patient's own goals.

Objective

  • Consciousness and cognition: GCS; MMSE or MoCA; ability to follow commands.
  • Communication and perception: aphasia; neglect (line bisection, cancellation tests); pushing; apraxia.
  • Sensation: touch, pain, position sense, stereognosis.
  • Tone: Modified Ashworth Scale (0, 1, 1+, 2, 3, 4).
  • Motor control: Brunnstrom stages (below); Fugl-Meyer Assessment (arm 66 points, leg 34); STREAM; grip and pinch.
  • Trunk and balance: sitting and standing balance; Berg Balance Scale (out of 56; below about 45 suggests a fall risk); Functional Reach; Timed Up and Go.
  • Gait: observational gait analysis by phase; 10-metre walk test (speed); 6-minute walk (endurance).
  • Shoulder: subluxation (finger-breadths below the acromion), pain on movement (0–10), range.
  • Function: Barthel Index (0–100) or FIM; bed mobility, transfers, stairs.

Brunnstrom stages of motor recovery

StageWhat you see
1Flaccid; no voluntary movement
2Spasticity begins; synergies appear as associated reactions or minimal voluntary movement
3Voluntary movement only within synergies; spasticity at its peak
4Some movements out of synergy (for example hand behind the back); spasticity declining
5More complex movements independent of synergy; spasticity waning
6Isolated joint movements with near-normal coordination; spasticity minimal

Not every patient passes through every stage, and recovery can stop at any stage.

10PT diagnosis by ICF

Every stroke long case asks for ICF with clinical reasoning, or for impairments, before goals and treatment.

Worked example from a real paper (S23, 5 + 7 + 3 marks)

"Write down ICF with clinical reasoning for a case of Lt. MCA infarct diagnosed 3 months back. Patient is 55 year old male and he is an auto driver by profession and a chronic diabetic. Plan short and long term goals with treatment strategies for the same. Patient walks independently with the help of a stick but has difficulty carrying out activities of daily living with his upper limb."

Spot the ICF clues before you write

Left MCA means a right hemiparesis, arm more than leg, and probably aphasia (left hemisphere). 3 months means the end of the early subacute phase: recovery is slowing, so practice must be intensive. auto driver means his livelihood needs a working right hand and driving clearance. chronic diabetic means foot care, sugar checks and slower healing. walks with a stick means the leg has recovered more than the arm.

12PT management

Principles in every phase: task-specific practice (practise the real task), repetition and intensity, progression, feedback, and practice outside therapy time. NICE (2023) recommends offering at least 3 hours a day, 5 days a week of combined therapy to those who can take part.

Phase 1: acute stage (7 marks in S17)

  • Timing: start once medically stable. The AVERT trial (2015) found that very early, high-dose out-of-bed activity in the first 24 hours reduced the chance of a good outcome at 3 months, so begin with short, frequent sessions and build up.
  • Positioning every 2 hours: lying on the affected side (shoulder brought forward, not lain on), lying on the unaffected side (affected arm forward on a pillow), supine (pillow under the affected shoulder and hip), and supported sitting as soon as possible. Head up 30° when there is a swallowing or chest risk.
  • Chest care: deep breathing, assisted cough, positioning; watch for aspiration.
  • Shoulder protection: support the arm in sitting; teach staff and family never to lift the patient by the arm.
  • Range of motion of all affected joints daily, gently, within pain-free range.
  • Early functional training: rolling to both sides, lying to sitting over the edge of the bed, sitting balance, sit to stand, standing, and short walks with help.
  • DVT prevention: active movements, early mobility, intermittent pneumatic compression when immobile.

Phase 2: subacute rehabilitation

  • Trunk and balance: reaching in sitting beyond arm's length, weight shift in standing, stepping, perturbations; a formal falls-prevention programme for every patient (AHA/ASA 2026); tai chi and dual-task training are reasonable.
  • Sit to stand and transfers: feet back, trunk forward, equal weight; practise from different heights.
  • Gait training: overground walking practice, treadmill training with or without body-weight support, an ankle-foot orthosis for foot drop, and walking aids only as needed.
  • Upper limb: task practice of reach, grasp, release and manipulation; constraint-induced movement therapy for those with some active wrist and finger extension (in the EXCITE trial the higher-functioning group had at least 20° active wrist and 10° finger extension; a mitt was worn on the strong hand for about 90% of waking hours, with 6 hours a day of task practice for 2 weeks); mirror therapy added to usual therapy (reasonable, AHA/ASA 2026); electrical stimulation of wrist and finger extensors; bilateral arm training.
  • Strength and fitness: progressive resistance training and aerobic exercise (doses in phase 3).
  • Sensory and perceptual training: sensory re-education of the hand; visual scanning for neglect; the pusher programme (section 5).
  • Spasticity: positioning, daily stretching and active use; botulinum toxin injection into overactive arm or leg muscles, followed by stretching and task practice, is recommended for focal spasticity (AHA/ASA 2026, Class 1).

Hemiplegic gait and its training (7 marks in S18, S19, S21, S22 and S25)

PhaseWhat goes wrongMain reason
StanceShort stance on the weak leg; knee snaps back (hyperextension) or stays bent; trunk leans; little hip extension at the end of stance; weak push-offWeak hip extensors and quadriceps, spastic or tight calf, poor balance
SwingStiff knee; foot drop and inversion; circumduction or hip hiking to clear the footWeak hip flexors and dorsiflexors, spastic quadriceps and calf (extensor synergy)
OverallSlow, short, asymmetric steps; arm held in flexion; high energy costWeakness, spasticity, poor selective control, fear of falling
  • Training: treat the cause (strengthen hip extensors, quadriceps and dorsiflexors; stretch the calf); practise each part (weight shift onto the weak leg, step-ups, stepping forwards and backwards, knee control in small ranges); then the whole task: repetitive walking practice overground and on a treadmill, faster as able; AFO or electrical stimulation of the peroneal nerve for foot drop; walking outdoors, on slopes and with obstacles.
  • Through the phases of recovery (S25): in the flaccid stage the patient stands and steps in the parallel bars with knee support; in the synergy stage walking is dominated by the extensor synergy (stiff knee, pointed foot, circumduction), so training breaks the synergy (knee bending with the hip extended, dorsiflexion with the knee straight); as selective control returns, the gait becomes faster and more symmetrical and training moves to the community.

The hemiplegic shoulder (7 marks in S16 and S26)

  • Problems: inferior subluxation in the flaccid stage (the weight of the arm stretches the capsule); pain; spasticity of the internal rotators and adductors; shoulder-hand syndrome.
  • Prevent: support the arm on a pillow, arm trough or lap tray in sitting; correct positioning in bed; handle the arm with the scapula moved forward; never pull on the arm during transfers.
  • Treat: range of motion within the pain-free range with the scapula mobilised first; active and task practice as movement returns; electrical stimulation of supraspinatus and deltoid for subluxation; botulinum toxin for a spastic, painful shoulder (reasonable, AHA/ASA 2026); manage shoulder-hand syndrome early.
  • Do not use overhead pulley exercises: the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline classes them as harmful because they increase shoulder pain.

Classic answer vs current guideline: overhead pulleys

Classic

Overhead pulleys and self-assisted overhead exercises to keep shoulder range.

Current (AHA/ASA 2026)

Recommended against (Class 3: Harm) for patients with subluxation, weakness or shoulder pain.

Write in the exam

"Gentle pain-free range with the scapula mobilised; avoid overhead pulleys, which cause shoulder pain."

The spastic hand (7 marks in W16 and S24; 3 techniques: 3 marks in S17)

  • Stretch and position: slow, sustained stretch of wrist and finger flexors; weight-bearing through the open hand in sitting.
  • Reduce tone before practice: slow rhythmic rotation of the trunk and shoulder, prolonged icing, reflex-inhibiting positions (see managing tone).
  • Active practice: grasp and release of objects of different sizes; electrical stimulation of wrist and finger extensors; mirror therapy; CIMT if eligible.
  • Medical: botulinum toxin to finger and wrist flexors followed by therapy.
  • Splints: resting splints and taping do not prevent wrist and finger spasticity (AHA/ASA 2026, no benefit). Use a splint only for a specific reason, such as a developing contracture or hygiene, and check the skin.

The flaccid upper limb (7 marks in S22)

  • Protect the shoulder (support, positioning, careful handling).
  • Stimulate activity: tapping, quick stretch, brushing and vibration over the muscles to be facilitated (Rood); weight-bearing through the arm; placing and holding the arm in different positions.
  • Electrical stimulation of shoulder and wrist muscles; mirror therapy; bilateral arm training.
  • Active-assisted, then active task practice as the first movements return.

Motor Relearning Programme for the upper limb (7 marks in W18)

Carr and Shepherd's programme trains real tasks in four steps: (1) analyse the task and find the missing components; (2) practise the missing components (for example forward reach with elbow extension, wrist extension while grasping, opening the hand to release); (3) practise the whole task (reach, grasp, move and release a glass); (4) transfer the training into daily life with practice outside therapy. Avoid unnecessary muscle activity and do not let the strong hand take over. More detail on the neurotherapeutic approaches page.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • Check the unit's blood-pressure limits, especially after thrombolysis or a haemorrhage; avoid exercise when resting BP is above about 200/110 mmHg.
  • No oral food or drink until a swallow screen is passed; position upright.
  • Protect the shoulder; never pull the affected arm; no overhead pulleys.
  • Craniectomy: no pressure on the side without bone; helmet when mobile.
  • Neglect and sensory loss: check the skin and position of the affected limbs; approach from the neglected side in training, not in dangerous tasks.
  • Anticoagulants: gentle handling; watch for bruising.
  • Diabetes: check sugar before exercise if on insulin; inspect the feet.
Stop and call for help if
  • New or worsening weakness, speech or vision change, severe sudden headache, falling consciousness (a new stroke or bleeding)
  • Chest pain, palpitations, breathlessness or dizziness
  • A seizure
  • Signs of DVT or pulmonary embolism

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

Secondary prevention

  • Take blood-pressure, sugar, cholesterol and antiplatelet or anticoagulant medicines every day; check BP regularly.
  • Stop smoking and all tobacco (bidi, gutka, khaini); limit alcohol; less salt and fried food; more vegetables and fruit.
  • Regular exercise as prescribed; reduce sitting time.
  • Know the warning signs (BE-FAST) and call for help at once if they come back.

Caregiver training and home

  • Safe transfers and positioning; how to support the shoulder; how to help without taking over.
  • Home changes: a commode or raised toilet seat if there is an Indian toilet; grab bars; a firm chair with arms; remove loose rugs; a bed at a height that allows the feet to rest flat on the floor.
  • A written home exercise programme with pictures: arm task practice, standing balance, walking time each day.
  • Return to work and driving only after assessment; look for adapted or alternative work early.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Most recovery happens in the first 3 months, and it continues more slowly for months after; improvement in function is possible even in the chronic stage with training.
  • Early predictors of arm recovery: in the EPOS study (2010), patients with some voluntary finger extension and shoulder abduction within 72 hours had a 98% chance of regaining some dexterity at 6 months, against 25% for those without.
  • Poorer outcome: older age, severe stroke, loss of consciousness at onset, severe neglect, poor sitting balance, persistent incontinence, little social support.
  • Discharge: safe transfers and mobility at the expected level; caregiver trained; home adapted; home programme understood; follow-up and community rehabilitation arranged.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mClinical features of anterior cerebral artery syndrome.

Opposite-side weakness and sensory loss of the leg more than the arm; urinary incontinence; apathy and slowed thinking; grasp reflex; difficulty starting to walk.

3 mComponents of the flexion synergy of the spastic upper limb.

Scapular retraction and elevation; shoulder abduction and external rotation; elbow flexion (strongest); forearm supination; wrist and finger flexion.

7 mDescribe hemiplegic gait and its physiotherapy management.

Stance: short weight-bearing on the weak side, knee hyperextension or flexion, reduced hip extension and push-off. Swing: stiff knee, foot drop and inversion, circumduction or hip hiking. Overall slow, short, asymmetric steps. Causes: weakness, spasticity (extensor synergy), poor control, contracture, poor balance. Management: strengthen hip extensors, quadriceps, dorsiflexors; stretch the calf; part practice (weight shift, step-ups, knee control); whole-task repetitive walking overground and on a treadmill; AFO or peroneal stimulation; community walking.

MCQA patient 6 weeks after stroke has painful shoulder subluxation. Which is contraindicated?A. Arm trough in sittingB. Electrical stimulation of supraspinatus and deltoidC. Overhead pulley exercisesD. Pain-free range with scapular mobilisation

C. Overhead pulleys increase shoulder pain; the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline recommends against them.

MCQWhich finding at 72 hours best predicts that the arm will regain some dexterity?A. Normal reflexesB. Some voluntary finger extension and shoulder abductionC. Low toneD. Intact pain sensation

B. In EPOS, patients with both had a 98% chance of some dexterity at 6 months.

MCQA patient leans and pushes towards the weak side and resists correction. The best first strategy is:A. Pull him upright repeatedlyB. Use a mirror or door frame to show vertical and reach towards the strong sideC. Strap him to the chairD. Wait until it resolves

B. Visual feedback of vertical and reaching to the strong side retrain the sense of upright; forcing correction increases pushing.

LAQA 45-year-old right-handed civil engineer had a right MCA fronto-parietal infarct 3 months ago. He cannot stand and has difficulty in all upper limb activities because of spasticity. He works from a home office on the 5th floor, and smokes and drinks socially. Write the structural and functional impairments, short- and long-term goals, and techniques to improve upper limb control. (3 + 5 + 7, W25)

Impairments (3): structural: right fronto-parietal infarct. Functional: left hemiplegia, arm more than leg; upper limb spasticity; poor sitting and standing balance; likely left-sided neglect and sensory loss after a right parietal lesion; risk of shoulder pain and subluxation.

Goals (5): short-term (4–6 weeks): steady sitting while reaching; sit-to-stand and standing with support; full pain-free shoulder range; left hand used to hold paper while writing. Long-term (3–6 months): walk indoors with an AFO and a cane; use the left hand as a helper at the computer; resume home-office work; stop smoking.

Upper limb control (7): support the arm on a trough or table and position it daily; prolonged stretch, not overhead pulleys; weight-bearing through the open hand; many repetitions of task-specific reaching, grasping and release at the desk; mirror therapy; modified constraint-induced therapy once he has 20° of wrist and 10° of finger extension; electrical stimulation of wrist and finger extensors; botulinum toxin with stretching for focal spasticity, if the doctor advises it.

Last recall check. Draw the circle of Willis and the ICF model, list the gait problems by phase, and write the aerobic exercise dose.
