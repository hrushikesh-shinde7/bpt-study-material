5B(1)Cerebral palsy: types, assessment, ICF and physiotherapy

Cerebral palsy was a long case in 13 of the 20 papers (2016–2026), more than any other topic, and the same 5-year-old athetoid girl was asked four times. Short questions ask for causes, types, orthoses, carrying positions and the posture and movement problems of each type. Every answer rests on one idea: the brain lesion does not progress, but the child's muscles and bones keep growing around it, so active, goal-directed practice and prevention of deformity must start early.

My revision

1Definition

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a group of permanent disorders of the development of movement and posture, causing activity limitation, that are due to non-progressive disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain. The motor disorder is often accompanied by disturbances of sensation, perception, cognition, communication and behaviour, by epilepsy, and by secondary musculoskeletal problems (international consensus, Rosenbaum et al., 2007).

  • Non-progressive lesion, changing picture: the brain injury does not worsen, but the child's posture, tone and deformities change with growth.
  • How common: about 3 per 1000 children in Indian studies (2.95 per 1000, 2019 meta-analysis). In high-income countries it is about 1.6 per 1000 live births and falling; the limited data from low- and middle-income countries show rates as high as 3.4 per 1000.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. A non-progressive brain lesion in the developing brain disturbs the control of movement and posture.
  2. Primary impairments: abnormal tone (spastic, fluctuating or low), weakness, poor selective motor control, poor balance and postural control, sensory and perceptual problems.
  3. Secondary impairments develop with growth: spastic, weak muscles do not lengthen as fast as the bones grow, so contractures appear (calf, hamstrings, hip adductors and flexors); bones twist (femoral anteversion, tibial torsion); the hips migrate out of the socket; the spine curves (scoliosis).
  4. Compensations (tertiary): toe walking, crouching, W-sitting, using one hand only.
  5. Associated problems: epilepsy, intellectual disability, vision and hearing loss, speech and swallowing difficulty, drooling, constipation, low bone density.
Why do contractures get worse when the brain lesion does not?

Muscle grows in length in response to stretch during normal daily movement. A spastic, weak muscle is rarely stretched through its full range, so it fails to keep up as the bone grows, especially in growth spurts. This is why stretching through active use, standing, casting and orthoses must continue through childhood.

5Clinical features by type

Posture and movement problems: spastic diplegia (7 marks in S26)

  • Legs much more affected than arms; hips flexed, adducted and internally rotated; knees flexed; ankles in equinus.
  • Sitting: on the sacrum with posterior pelvic tilt and a rounded back (tight hamstrings), or W-sitting for stability.
  • Standing and walking: scissoring, toe walking, crouch or jump knee pattern, in-toeing; arms held up for balance (high guard).
  • Often born preterm with periventricular leukomalacia; intelligence frequently normal.

Posture and movement problems: athetoid (dyskinetic) CP (7 marks in S26)

  • Involuntary, writhing movements that increase with effort, excitement and attempts to move; fluctuating tone from low to high.
  • Poor head control and poor midline control; the asymmetrical tonic neck reflex persists, turning the head pulls the body into asymmetry.
  • Poor proximal stability (little co-contraction), so the child cannot hold a position and grades movement poorly; sudden extensor thrusts push the child out of the chair.
  • Drooling, unclear speech and feeding difficulty; hearing loss after kernicterus; intelligence often normal.
  • Walking, if achieved, is late, unsteady and wide-based, with the arms held high.

Spastic hemiplegia

  • One side, arm more than leg; fisted hand with the thumb in the palm; equinus or equinovarus foot.
  • A clear hand preference before 12 months of age is an early warning sign.
  • Learned non-use of the affected hand; seizures more common.

Spastic quadriplegia

  • All four limbs and the trunk; poor head control; often intellectual disability, epilepsy and feeding problems.
  • Spinal deviations (3 marks in W25): neuromuscular scoliosis (long C-shaped curve), pelvic obliquity, kyphosis, increased lumbar lordosis with hip flexion contracture, and windswept hips.

Impairments in hypotonic CP (3 marks in S26)

Low tone with weak antigravity muscles; poor head and trunk control; hypermobile joints; "frog-leg" lying posture; very delayed milestones; shallow breathing and a weak cough; feeding difficulty; low endurance.

6Investigations and early diagnosis

  • Early diagnosis (Novak et al., 2017): CP can be diagnosed or flagged as "high risk" before 6 months' corrected age. Before 5 months: MRI plus Prechtl's General Movements Assessment (absent "fidgety" movements). After 5 months: MRI plus the Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination (HINE). Early diagnosis allows early intervention while the brain is most plastic.
  • MRI: periventricular leukomalacia, infarcts, basal ganglia injury, malformations.
  • Hip X-ray (anteroposterior pelvis) for hip surveillance: the migration percentage shows how much of the femoral head lies outside the socket.
  • EEG for seizures; hearing and vision tests; genetic and metabolic tests when the history or course is unusual.

Differential diagnosis (part of the syllabus): progressive conditions must be excluded, such as muscular dystrophy, spinal muscular atrophy, metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders, brain tumours and hereditary spastic paraplegia. Loss of skills, family history or a worsening course points away from CP.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Spasticity: botulinum toxin injections into overactive muscles, followed by stretching, casting, orthoses and task practice; oral baclofen; intrathecal baclofen pump for severe generalised spasticity; selective dorsal rhizotomy for selected children with spastic diplegia.
  • Orthopaedic surgery: tendon lengthening (Achilles, hamstrings, adductors); bone surgery for torsion; several procedures in one sitting (single-event multilevel surgery) in walking children; hip surgery for displacement; spinal fusion for severe scoliosis.
  • Other: antiepileptic drugs; nutrition support, sometimes gastrostomy; drugs for drooling and dystonia.

9PT assessment

  • History: pregnancy, birth (gestation, weight, delayed cry, NICU stay, jaundice), milestones, seizures, feeding, vision, hearing, school, family's goals, home (floor living, carrying, toilet).
  • Observation: posture in lying, sitting and standing; involuntary movements; how the child moves and plays.
  • Gross motor: GMFCS level; Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM-66 or GMFM-88); milestones; head and trunk control.
  • Tone: Modified Ashworth Scale; Modified Tardieu Scale (catch angle at fast speed against full range at slow speed); primitive reflexes and postural reactions.
  • Range and contractures: Thomas test (hip flexors), hip abduction, popliteal angle (hamstrings), ankle dorsiflexion with knee straight and bent (calf); spine.
  • Strength and selective motor control.
  • Balance and gait: sitting and standing balance; observational gait analysis (scissoring, equinus, crouch); walking aids.
  • Hand function: MACS level; grasp, release, bimanual use.
  • Others: cognition, communication, vision, hearing, behaviour; equipment; parents' understanding and goals.

12PT management

Principles: family-centred, goal-directed (goals the child and family choose), active, task-specific practice through play, started early, with a home programme and prevention of deformity.

What the evidence supports (Novak et al., 2019 review)

Rated effective ("green"): constraint-induced movement therapy and bimanual training for the hand, goal-directed and task-specific training, treadmill training with or without body-weight support, strength training, fitness training, mobility training, weight-bearing, casting, hippotherapy, environmental enrichment, home programmes, botulinum toxin with therapy, and hip surveillance. Rated not effective ("red") for motor goals: neurodevelopmental therapy in its original passive form, and sensory integration.

Classic answer vs current evidence: NDT (Bobath)

Classic

Inhibit abnormal tone and reflexes with handling (reflex-inhibiting patterns, key points of control) before facilitating normal movement.

Current

Passive NDT does not improve motor function. Use active, goal-directed task practice; handling only to set the child up for active movement.

Write in the exam

"Goal-directed, task-specific active training through play, with handling and positioning to support it, and a home programme."

By stage of childhood

  • Infancy (early intervention): parent coaching; positioning and carrying; head and trunk control in prone, sitting and supported standing; reaching and play; feeding position; environmental enrichment.
  • Pre-school: sitting, standing and walking practice; standing frames; orthoses; walkers; play and transfers; casting or botulinum toxin when spasticity limits progress.
  • School age: strength and fitness training, treadmill walking, CIMT and bimanual training for hemiplegia, gait training, school adaptations and sport.
  • Adolescence: fitness and weight control, managing contractures through growth spurts, independence and transition to adult services.

Management for the athetoid (dyskinetic) child

  • Proximal stability: weight-bearing through the arms (prone on elbows, four-point kneeling) and legs (kneeling, standing frame); approximation through the shoulders and hips; holding positions against gentle resistance (co-contraction).
  • Midline control: head and hands in midline, symmetrical sitting; play with both hands together.
  • Graded movement: slow, small-range movements with feedback; avoid excitement and effort that increase involuntary movement.
  • Seating: stable chair with a pelvic belt, foot support and a table; hips, knees and ankles at about 90°.
  • Standing frame, walker or gait trainer when ready; communication and feeding with the team.

Gait training in spastic diplegia (S18 long case)

  • Reduce the barriers: stretch and cast tight calves and hamstrings; botulinum toxin to adductors or calves when indicated; strengthen hip extensors and abductors, quadriceps and calf.
  • Correct the alignment: AFO for equinus; ground-reaction AFO for crouch; abduction during standing practice to stop scissoring.
  • Practise walking: standing balance and weight shift, stepping with a wide base, walking in the parallel bars, then with a walker (posterior walkers encourage an upright posture), treadmill training with or without body-weight support, then outdoors and in school.
  • Fitness and strength for endurance; regular review of range in growth spurts.

Trunk control and ambulation in hypotonic CP (S22 long case)

  • Head and trunk extension in prone over a wedge or ball; supported sitting with gradually less support; reaching in sitting; weight shift.
  • Joint compression (approximation) and weight-bearing to build co-contraction; quick-stretch and tapping over trunk extensors; bouncing on a ball.
  • Supported standing in a frame; then cruising along furniture and walking with a walker.
  • Parent education with low-cost equipment (rolled towels, a corner seat, a wooden standing box), suited to a poor family.

Home-care advice to parents (W17 long case)

  • Positioning through the day: side lying, prone over a wedge, supported sitting; avoid lying always on the back and avoid W-sitting.
  • Carrying and handling as above; feeding upright with the head slightly forward; dressing the stiffer limb first and undressing it last.
  • Daily play that practises head control, reaching, rolling and sitting; standing frame time.
  • No baby walkers; regular review of hips, splints and skin; follow the home programme.

Supported standing (dose)

A review of standing programmes (Paleg et al., 2013) suggests standing 60–90 minutes a day, 5 days a week to improve bone density; 60 minutes a day with the hips in 30–60° of total abduction for hip stability; 45–60 minutes a day for hip, knee and ankle range; and 30–45 minutes a day for spasticity.

13Precautions and contraindications

Take care
  • Seizures: know the child's seizure plan; avoid triggers such as fatigue.
  • Low bone density: gentle handling and stretching; no forceful range in non-walking children.
  • Hips: do not force abduction; report pain or loss of abduction.
  • Swallowing: feed upright; no feeding in lying.
  • Skin under casts and orthoses: check daily for red marks.
  • After botulinum toxin: increase stretching and practice in the following weeks; watch for unusual weakness.
  • Fatigue: short, frequent, playful sessions.

14Education and home programme

  • Explain that CP does not get worse, but deformities can be prevented with daily practice and positioning.
  • Teach handling, carrying, feeding, bathing and dressing positions; give a short written or picture home programme the family can fit into daily routines.
  • Low-cost equipment: a corner seat, wedge, standing box or frame, adapted spoon and cup.
  • Encourage inclusive schooling, play with other children and sport; advise the school on seating, toilets and access.
  • Help the family get a disability certificate and benefits; support the parents' own wellbeing.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • The GMFCS level predicts long-term gross motor function. Children reach about 90% of their gross motor potential by about 5 years of age, and those at more severe levels reach it earlier (Rosenbaum et al., 2002).
  • Sitting independently by 2 years of age is a strong sign of later walking: in a classic study, every child who sat by 2 years walked eventually (Molnar and Gordon, 1976).
  • Associated problems (epilepsy, intellectual disability, feeding difficulty) and severe quadriplegia shorten life expectancy; most children with milder CP live into adulthood.
  • Therapy continues in blocks through childhood around goals; discharge from an episode when its goals are met and the family manages the home programme.

3 mEnumerate three prenatal causes of cerebral palsy.

Maternal infection (rubella, cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis); multiple pregnancy; placental insufficiency with poor fetal growth. (Also maternal pre-eclampsia or thyroid disease, brain malformations, Rh incompatibility.)

3 mEnumerate three spinal deviations in a child with quadriplegic CP.

Neuromuscular scoliosis (long C-curve) with pelvic obliquity; kyphosis; increased lumbar lordosis (with hip flexion contracture). Windswept hips often go with them.

7 mExplain the posture and movement problems of a child with athetoid cerebral palsy.

Involuntary writhing movements that increase with effort and excitement; fluctuating tone; poor head and midline control with persistent ATNR; poor proximal stability and co-contraction, so the child cannot hold positions or grade movements; sudden extensor thrusts; drooling, unclear speech, feeding difficulty, possible hearing loss; late, unsteady, wide-based walking with arms held high; intelligence often normal.

MCQA child walks in a crouch with good hip and knee range. The most suitable orthosis is:A. Posterior leaf spring AFOB. Ground-reaction AFOC. Supramalleolar orthosisD. Hinged AFO without a stop

B. The anterior shell of a ground-reaction AFO pushes the knee back in stance.

MCQWhich approach is rated not effective for improving motor function in CP?A. Goal-directed trainingB. Constraint-induced movement therapyC. Passive neurodevelopmental therapyD. Treadmill training

C. Passive NDT was rated red in the 2019 evidence review; the others are green.

MCQWhich tool classifies the gross motor function of a child with CP?A. MACSB. GMFCSC. CFCSD. Modified Ashworth Scale

B. GMFCS grades gross motor function (levels I–V). MACS grades hand use and CFCS grades communication; the Ashworth scale measures tone.

LAQA 2-year-old girl with hypotonic CP rolls to either side and comes to sitting with moderate support. Her parents are uneducated and poor. Describe her impairments with clinical reasoning, and the physiotherapy for trunk control and ambulation. (7 + 8, S22)

Impairments with reasoning (7): low tone and weak antigravity muscles, so poor head and trunk control; poor co-contraction around hips and shoulders, so she cannot hold sitting; hypermobile joints; delayed milestones; poor sitting balance and protective reactions; possible feeding and breathing difficulty; risk of hip and foot deformity from lying in a frog-leg posture.

Physiotherapy (8): prone over a wedge or roll for head and trunk extension; supported sitting with less support each week, reaching in sitting and weight shift; approximation through shoulders and hips, and bouncing on a ball, for co-contraction; kneeling and supported standing in a low-cost wooden standing box; then cruising along furniture and a walker. Teach the parents with pictures, using a rolled towel, a corner seat and daily play routines. Protect hypermobile joints and do not overstretch.

Last recall check. Draw the three distributions, write the GMFCS levels, and list the orthoses with their uses.
