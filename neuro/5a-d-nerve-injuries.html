5A(d)Peripheral nerve injuries and the brachial plexus: classification, deformities, splints and physiotherapy

Nerve injuries appeared in 14 of the 20 papers: the classification of nerve injury was a 3-mark question five times, splints for radial palsy three times, and Erb's palsy or a plexus case often enough to expect one. Every answer follows the nerve's recovery: protect and splint while the muscle is denervated, then re-educate movement and sensation as the regrowing nerve (about 1 mm a day) reaches each muscle.

My revision

1Definition

  • A peripheral nerve injury is damage to a peripheral nerve that causes a lower motor neuron (LMN) loss in its area: weakness and wasting of the muscles it supplies, loss of reflexes, sensory loss and loss of sweating.
  • A brachial plexus injury damages the C5–T1 roots, trunks or cords that supply the upper limb, usually by traction.
  • Because a peripheral nerve can regrow, many injuries recover, slowly, from the injury site downwards.

2Classification and grading

Seddon's three types: neurapraxia (conduction block), axonotmesis (axon cut, sheaths intact) and neurotmesis (nerve divided). Sunderland split axonotmesis into degrees II–IV by how much connective tissue is cut.

4Pathophysiology to impairments

  1. When an axon is cut, the part below the injury breaks down (Wallerian degeneration) over days, so the muscles lose their nerve supply.
  2. Denervated muscle becomes flaccid and wastes; reflexes are lost; the skin loses sensation and sweating (dry, shiny, cool skin).
  3. Because the nerve regrows from the injury site at about 1 mm a day (about 2.5 cm a month), muscles close to the injury recover first and distant muscles last.
  4. A Tinel's sign (tingling when the nerve is tapped) moves down the limb as the regrowing axons advance.
  5. If reinnervation takes too long, muscle fibres and motor end-plates degenerate, so proximal injuries far from the hand recover less.
  6. Axons that regrow into the wrong tubes cause poor localisation of touch and imprecise movement.

Claw hand (7 marks in W19)

Claw hand is hyperextension of the knuckle (MCP) joints with flexion of the finger (IP) joints, because the lumbricals and interossei are paralysed: the long extensors pull the knuckles back and the long flexors bend the fingers, unopposed. An ulnar lesion alone claws the ring and little fingers; median and ulnar lesions together claw all four fingers. Other causes: Klumpke's palsy, leprosy, Volkmann's contracture.

6Investigations

  • Nerve conduction studies and EMG: show the site and severity; denervation potentials (fibrillations) appear about 2–3 weeks after injury, so studies are most useful from then on, and repeated studies show reinnervation before it is visible.
  • Imaging: X-rays for fractures; ultrasound or MRI of the nerve; MRI or CT myelography of the neck for root avulsion (pseudomeningocele) in plexus injuries.
  • Bedside: sensory mapping, Tinel's sign tracking, sweat (ninhydrin) test.

7Medical and surgical management

  • Neurapraxia and axonotmesis: conservative care with splints and exercise; watch for recovery with Tinel's sign and repeated nerve studies.
  • Clean cuts: early primary repair; gaps: nerve grafts (often the sural nerve).
  • Brachial plexus: exploration with repair, grafting or nerve transfers (for example a branch of the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve for shoulder movement, or an ulnar nerve fascicle to the biceps branch for elbow flexion). Results are best when surgery is done within 6 months of injury, and about 3 months is usually appropriate (Martin et al., 2018). Late cases are treated with muscle or tendon transfers.
  • Tendon transfers when a nerve does not recover: for example for wrist drop, foot drop or claw hand.
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome: splint and education first; steroid injection; surgical release for severe or persistent cases (thenar wasting, constant numbness).
  • Pain: medicines for nerve pain.

9PT assessment

  • History: mechanism, date, surgery, dominant hand, job, pain.
  • Inspection: posture of the limb, deformity, wasting, skin and nails, sweating, swelling.
  • Motor: manual muscle testing of every muscle the nerve supplies, in order from proximal to distal; trick movements.
  • Sensory: map light touch and pinprick; two-point discrimination; monofilaments; Tinel's sign and how far it has moved.
  • Special tests: Froment's sign, card test, pen test, Ochsner's clasp test; for carpal tunnel: Phalen's test, Tinel's sign at the wrist, carpal compression test.
  • Function: grip and pinch strength, Nine-Hole Peg Test, the DASH questionnaire; gait for foot drop.

Worked example from a real paper (W16, 7 + 8 marks)

"Discuss the ICF with clinical reasoning and short term rehabilitation goals and techniques of a 35 years old shopkeeper, who suffered right sided Erb's palsy during a road traffic accident 5 months back. He complains of difficulty in using his right upper extremity. He has a dependent wife and 2 children."

Spot the ICF clues before you write

Erb's palsy means C5–C6 (upper trunk): shoulder abduction, external rotation and elbow flexion are lost. 5 months back means some recovery may have started, and the surgical window (about 6 months) is closing: check for recovery and refer if there is none. shopkeeper lifting and reaching to shelves is his work. dependent wife and 2 children means he is the sole earner.

12PT management

Principles: protect and support the denervated part; keep joints mobile; keep the skin safe; re-educate each muscle as it is reinnervated; retrain sensation; return the person to work.

By phase

  • Denervation (protective) phase: splints in a functional position (cock-up splint, AFO, sling or shoulder support); daily passive range of motion, gently, to every joint; prevent overstretching of paralysed muscles; oedema control; skin protection; strengthening of muscles that work; education about the expected recovery time.
  • Reinnervation phase (Tinel's sign advancing, first flicker of movement): muscle re-education one muscle at a time, with EMG biofeedback or electrical stimulation as a cue; gravity-eliminated active-assisted, then active, then resisted exercise; short sessions to avoid fatigue; stop trick movements.
  • Functional phase: grip and pinch strengthening; task practice; work simulation; sensory re-education; endurance.
  • After nerve transfers: start by contracting the donor muscle to fire the new target (for example squeezing the fingers to bend the elbow after an ulnar fascicle transfer), then practise the new movement on its own.

Sensory re-education

  1. Protective stage: teach the person to look at the hand while using it, test bath water with the other hand, and avoid sharp and hot objects.
  2. Desensitisation for hypersensitive skin: graded textures (cotton, then towel, then rougher), tapping, vibration.
  3. Discriminative stage: localise touch with the eyes open, then closed; recognise textures, shapes and everyday objects without looking.

Dynamic cock-up splint: uses and principle (S25)

Uses: radial nerve palsy (wrist, finger and thumb drop), after extensor tendon repair, and after tendon transfers for radial palsy. Principle: the splint replaces the paralysed extensors. A static wrist support keeps the wrist extended so the flexors grip strongly, and elastic slings on an outrigger return the fingers and thumb to extension after each active grasp. It allows function, prevents overstretching of the extensors and stops the flexors shortening.

Specific lesions

  • Radial palsy (7 marks in S18 and W18): static cock-up at night and a dynamic splint by day; range of motion of the wrist and fingers; stop the flexors shortening; re-educate wrist and finger extensors as recovery comes (brachioradialis and wrist extensors first, then finger and thumb extensors); grip activities with the wrist supported.
  • Claw hand (7 marks in W19): knuckle-bender splint; passive stretching of the knuckle joints into flexion; intrinsic re-education (finger spreading, "table-top" position); protect the numb little finger; tendon transfer if no recovery.
  • Foot drop (3 marks in S16) and peroneal nerve injury (7 marks in W25): AFO; daily calf stretching; dorsiflexor and evertor re-education as recovery comes, with functional electrical stimulation if available; gait training to replace the high-stepping pattern; protect the numb top of the foot; tendon transfer if there is no recovery.
  • Erb's palsy (7 marks in S17, S21, W21 and W24): support the arm; keep full shoulder range, especially external rotation and abduction, and supination; prevent subluxation; re-educate deltoid, spinati, biceps and supinator in gravity-eliminated positions first (lying, a powder board, a sling suspension); biofeedback; functional reaching; after surgery, follow the surgeon's protocol.
  • Klumpke's palsy (7 marks in S25): anti-claw splint; range of the fingers and wrist; intrinsic and long flexor re-education; sensory protection and re-education of the inner hand; check for Horner's syndrome.

Carpal tunnel syndrome (7 marks in S22)

  • Neutral wrist splint at night for short-term relief (moderate evidence); day or full-time wear if night wear alone fails.
  • Education: pathology, risk factors, avoiding sustained wrist bending and forceful gripping, keyboard and mouse habits.
  • Manual therapy to the neck and upper limb, a trial of superficial heat, interferential current or diathermy, and splinting with stretching may help in mild to moderate cases (weak evidence).
  • Do not use low-level laser, iontophoresis or magnets (moderate evidence) or thermal ultrasound (weak evidence). Nerve gliding has conflicting evidence.
  • Refer for injection or surgery if there is thenar wasting, constant numbness or no response.

Recommendations from the APTA carpal tunnel guideline (2019). The guideline was revised in 2026; check the new edition for any changes.

13Precautions, contraindications and stop criteria

Take care
  • No heat, ice or electrical treatment over numb skin without close checks; teach skin inspection.
  • After nerve repair, protect the repair as the surgeon advises (no stretch on the nerve for the first weeks).
  • Check splints for pressure points over bony areas and numb skin.
  • Do not overstretch paralysed muscles or a subluxing shoulder; no traction on a flail arm.
  • Avoid overworking newly reinnervated muscles.
  • Watch for signs of CRPS (burning pain, swelling, colour change).

14Education, lifestyle and home programme

  • Explain how slowly nerves regrow (about 2.5 cm a month) so the person keeps exercising for months.
  • Daily home range-of-motion and strengthening programme; splint wearing and care.
  • Skin protection: check the skin, use gloves and long handles in the kitchen, test water with the other hand.
  • Work: modify tasks and tools; rearrange the workplace; plan a staged return.
  • Carpal tunnel: posture at work, breaks, tool and keyboard changes.

15Prognosis and discharge criteria

  • Better outcome: neurapraxia or low-degree injury; young age; a distal injury close to the muscles; a clean cut repaired early; a pure motor or pure sensory nerve.
  • Worse outcome: crush or traction injuries over a long segment; root avulsion; old age; long delay before repair; a proximal injury far from the hand muscles.
  • In brachial plexus injuries, the upper plexus recovers better than the lower, and avulsed roots do not recover without transfer.
  • Discharge when recovery has plateaued, the person uses the limb in daily life and work, and knows the home programme and skin care.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWrite Sunderland's classification of nerve injury.

I: conduction block, myelin damage (neurapraxia). II: axon cut, endoneurium intact. III: axon and endoneurium cut, perineurium intact. IV: only the epineurium intact (neuroma in continuity). V: complete division (neurotmesis). Degrees I and II recover fully; III partly; IV and V need surgery.

3 mDescribe the orthosis used for radial nerve injury.

A static cock-up splint holding the wrist in about 20–30° of extension (rest and night), and a dynamic (lively) cock-up splint with an outrigger and elastic finger slings for daytime function: the person grips actively and the elastic straightens the fingers and thumb again.

7 mDescribe Erb's palsy and its physiotherapy.

Features: C5–C6 upper trunk traction injury; loss of shoulder abduction and external rotation, elbow flexion and supination; "waiter's tip" posture; sensory loss over the outer arm and forearm; biceps reflex lost.

Physiotherapy: support the arm and prevent subluxation; daily full passive range, especially external rotation, abduction and supination; protect numb skin; re-educate deltoid, spinati, biceps and supinator in gravity-eliminated positions with biofeedback as recovery comes, then active and resisted; functional reaching and lifting; refer for nerve transfer if no recovery by about 3–6 months; rehabilitation after surgery.

LAQA 21-year-old fell from a tree onto an outstretched hand 3 weeks ago. He cannot make a fist, straighten his elbow or move his thumb, with sensory loss on the inner arm and forearm. Identify the part of the plexus, write goals and physiotherapy with orthoses. (2 + 6 + 7, S22)

Level (2): lower plexus, C7–T1 (middle and lower trunks).

Goals (6): short-term: full range of fingers, wrist and elbow; no claw contracture; protected numb skin; splints worn correctly. Long-term: active grip and release; elbow extension against gravity; useful hand for daily tasks and study or work.
