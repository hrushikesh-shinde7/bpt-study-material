GCognition, perception, apraxia and aphasia: assessment and physiotherapy

These problems came up in 7 papers: the types of apraxia were a 7-mark question three times, and aphasia, neglect, figure-ground discrimination and combined cortical sensations were 3-mark questions. They matter because a patient who cannot plan a movement, understand an instruction or notice one side of the world will not benefit from exercises taught in the usual way: you must change how you teach.

1Apraxia

Asked for 7 marks in S23, S24 and W24, and for 3 marks in S22.

Apraxia is the inability to carry out learned, purposeful movements on command, even though strength, sensation, coordination, comprehension and willingness are adequate. It usually follows a left parietal or frontal lesion.

TypeWhat failsExample
IdeomotorCannot do a movement on command or by imitation, though the person knows what to do and may do it automatically (left parietal)Cannot show how to comb hair when asked, but combs spontaneously in the morning
Ideational (conceptual)Loses the idea or sequence of a task; misuses objects (left parietal, diffuse damage)Puts toothpaste on the comb; tries to drink tea before pouring it
Limb-kineticClumsy, imprecise fine finger movements (premotor cortex)Cannot button a shirt or pick up a coin, without weakness
ConstructionalCannot copy, draw or build two- or three-dimensional shapes (parietal, either side)Cannot copy a cube or arrange blocks to a pattern
DressingCannot dress correctly (often right parietal, with neglect)Puts the shirt on inside out or one arm only
Buccofacial (oral)Cannot do facial movements on command (left frontal; often with Broca's aphasia)Cannot blow out a candle when asked, but does it when the candle is lit
Gait apraxiaCannot start or organise walking though leg movements are normal in lying (frontal lobe, hydrocephalus)Feet seem "glued to the floor"

Assessment

  • Ask the person to pantomime tool use ("show me how you brush your teeth"), imitate meaningless and meaningful gestures, use real objects, and perform a multistep task (making tea).
  • Note errors: wrong movement, body part used as the tool, wrong order, perseveration.

Physiotherapy

  • Practise real tasks with real objects in their natural setting (drink from a real cup in the ward), rather than abstract movements on command.
  • Strategy training: teach the person to talk themselves through each step, with pictures or written step cards.
  • Errorless learning and chaining: guide the correct movement; teach the steps one at a time (forward or backward chaining).
  • Physical guidance rather than long verbal instructions; reduce cues gradually.
  • Gesture training for ideomotor apraxia.

2Aphasia

Asked for 3 marks in S23 (Broca's and Wernicke's) and S25 (aphasia and its types).

Aphasia is a loss or impairment of language (speaking, understanding, reading or writing) from brain damage, usually of the left hemisphere. It differs from dysarthria, which is a motor speech disorder with normal language.

TypeSpeech and comprehensionLesion
Broca's (expressive, non-fluent)Slow, effortful, short phrases with missing small words (telegraphic); comprehension fairly good; repetition poor; the person knows and is frustratedLeft inferior frontal gyrus; often with right arm weakness
Wernicke's (receptive, fluent)Fluent but empty or meaningless speech with wrong and made-up words; poor comprehension and repetition; often unaware of the problemLeft posterior superior temporal gyrus
ConductionFluent, comprehension good, but repetition very poorArcuate fasciculus
GlobalAll language functions severely affectedLarge left MCA territory lesion
AnomicFluent with good comprehension, but word-finding difficultyVarious, often mild
Transcortical motor or sensoryLike Broca's or Wernicke's, but repetition is preservedAreas around the language centres

How physiotherapy changes

  • Speak slowly in short, simple sentences; one instruction at a time; ask yes or no questions.
  • Use gestures, demonstration, pictures and physical guidance; check understanding by watching the action, not by "yes".
  • Reduce noise and distraction; give time to respond; do not finish sentences for the person or speak as if to a child.
  • Work with the speech and language therapist; use the same communication aids.

3Unilateral neglect

Asked for 3 marks in S19 (assessment of unilateral visual neglect).

  • Unilateral neglect is a failure to report, respond or orient to stimuli on the side opposite a brain lesion, not caused by weakness or sensory loss. It is commonest and most severe after right parietal lesions (left neglect).
  • Signs: eats food from one half of the plate; shaves or dresses one side; bumps into door frames on the left; ignores people on the left; the head and eyes turn to the right; may deny the weak arm belongs to them.
  • Neglect vs hemianopia: in hemianopia the person knows about the field loss and turns the head to compensate; in neglect the person is unaware and does not compensate.

Assessment (S19)

TestHowSign of neglect
Line bisectionMark the middle of several horizontal linesMarks shifted towards the good side
Cancellation tests (star, letter, Albert's lines)Cross out all targets on a sheetTargets missed on the neglected side
Drawing and copyingDraw a clock, copy a flower or houseOne half missing or crowded
Reading and writingRead a paragraph; write a sentenceStarts reading mid-line; writing drifts to one side
Functional observationEating, dressing, wheelchair use; Catherine Bergego ScaleOne side ignored in daily tasks

Physiotherapy

  • Visual scanning training: anchor the eyes on a coloured line at the edge of the neglected side, then scan across.
  • Approach and talk from the neglected side once the person can tolerate it; place items progressively towards that side.
  • Limb activation: move the neglected arm in the neglected space; trunk rotation towards the neglected side.
  • Other options with some evidence: prism adaptation, mirror therapy; the evidence for neglect treatments remains limited.
  • Safety: check the neglected limb's position (arm caught in wheels, foot off the footplate).

4Perceptual disorders and cortical sensations

Figure-ground discrimination (3 marks in W18)

The inability to pick out an object from its background. The person cannot find a white shirt on a white bed, a brake on the wheelchair, or a step edge on a patterned floor (usually right parietal-occipital damage). Assess by asking the person to find an item in a cluttered drawer or picture. Treat by reducing clutter, adding colour contrast (bright tape on brakes and step edges), scanning practice, and gradually busier settings.

Combined (cortical) sensations (3 marks in W18)

Sensations that need intact primary sensation plus integration in the parietal cortex:

  • Stereognosis: recognising an object (coin, key) by touch with the eyes closed.
  • Graphaesthesia: recognising a number or letter drawn on the skin.
  • Two-point discrimination: telling one touch from two close points.
  • Tactile localisation: pointing to where you were touched.
  • Barognosis: judging weight; recognising texture.
  • Bilateral simultaneous stimulation: feeling touches on both sides at once (one side "disappears" in extinction).

Other perceptual problems

  • Body scheme disorders: poor awareness of body parts and their relations (somatoagnosia), right-left confusion, finger agnosia.
  • Spatial relations: position in space, depth perception (misjudging steps and kerbs), topographical disorientation (getting lost).
  • Agnosias: visual object agnosia; prosopagnosia (faces).
  • Anosognosia: unawareness or denial of the paralysis.
  • Pusher syndrome: see the stroke page.

5Cognition

  • Domains: arousal, attention, memory, executive function (planning, problem-solving, judgement), processing speed.
  • Screening tools: Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE, 30 points; low scores suggest impairment, adjusted for education); Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, 30 points, more sensitive to mild and executive problems).
  • How physiotherapy changes: short, structured sessions at the same time each day; one-step instructions; repetition; errorless learning; memory aids (diary, checklists, phone reminders); a quiet room; involve family; check safety awareness before independent practice.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mDifferentiate Broca's and Wernicke's aphasia.

Broca's: non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic speech; comprehension fairly good; aware; left inferior frontal lesion. Wernicke's: fluent but meaningless speech with paraphasias; poor comprehension; often unaware; left posterior superior temporal lesion. Repetition is poor in both.

3 mHow do you assess unilateral visual neglect?

Line bisection (marks shifted to the good side); cancellation tests (targets missed on one side); clock drawing and figure copying (half missing); reading (starts mid-line); observation of eating, dressing and wheelchair use (Catherine Bergego Scale).

7 mDescribe apraxia and its types with examples.

Definition; ideomotor (cannot comb on command but does it automatically); ideational (wrong sequence or object use: toothpaste on the comb); limb-kinetic (clumsy buttoning); constructional (cannot copy a cube); dressing (shirt inside out); buccofacial (cannot blow out a candle on command); gait apraxia (feet glued to the floor). Assessment and physiotherapy as in the skeleton.

LAQA patient with a right parietal stroke eats only the right half of his meal and keeps hitting his left arm on door frames. Plan assessment and physiotherapy. (Practice case)

Assessment: line bisection, cancellation, drawing, reading; observe daily tasks; separate neglect from hemianopia; check sensation and anosognosia. Physiotherapy: visual scanning with an anchor on the left; limb activation in left space; trunk rotation to the left; approach and place items from the left as tolerated; mirror therapy or prism adaptation if available; safety for the left arm and foot in the wheelchair; teach family; repeat tests to track change.
