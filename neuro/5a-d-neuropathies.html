5A(d)Peripheral neuropathies: diabetic foot, leprosy, alcoholic neuropathy, neuropathic pain and CRPS

These neuropathies came up in 9 of the 20 papers, mostly as 7-mark questions on the diabetic foot (four times), plus long cases on diabetic neuropathy and leprosy. Every answer protects a foot or hand that can no longer feel: test sensation, prevent ulcers and deformity, keep the person active safely, and treat pain with movement as well as medicine.

1Diabetic neuropathy and the diabetic foot

Asked for 7 marks in W17, W19, W22 and S23 (classification and diabetic foot care) and as a long case in S16. India has about 101 million adults with diabetes (11.4%; ICMR-INDIAB, 2023), so this is a daily problem in practice.

Definition and types (ADA, 2017)

GroupTypes
Diffuse neuropathyDistal symmetric polyneuropathy (the commonest: "glove and stocking"; small-fibre, large-fibre or mixed); autonomic neuropathy (heart rate and blood pressure, stomach and bowel, bladder and sexual function, sweating)
MononeuropathyA single cranial or limb nerve (for example the third cranial nerve, median, ulnar or peroneal nerve); several nerves (mononeuritis multiplex)
RadiculopathyLumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy (diabetic amyotrophy: painful thigh weakness and wasting); thoracic radiculopathy

Causes and key features

  • Long-standing, poorly controlled blood sugar damages nerves and their blood supply; the longest nerves fail first, so symptoms start in the toes and rise.
  • Sensory: numbness, tingling, burning pain, "walking on cotton wool"; loss of protective sensation; poor position sense, so balance worsens in the dark.
  • Motor: weak intrinsic foot muscles, so the toes claw, slippers slip off unnoticed and pressure rises under the metatarsal heads.
  • Autonomic: dry, cracked skin (no sweating); orthostatic hypotension; resting tachycardia.
  • The diabetic foot: neuropathy plus peripheral artery disease plus deformity leads to ulcers, infection and amputation. A hot, swollen foot without pain may be a Charcot foot (neuropathic joint destruction).

Medical point that matters for physiotherapy

  • Good control of blood sugar, blood pressure and lipids; stop smoking.
  • Investigations (S16, 3 marks): blood sugar and HbA1c; the 10 g monofilament test and a 128 Hz tuning fork; nerve conduction studies; foot pulses and the ankle-brachial index; vitamin B12 (low on metformin), kidney and thyroid tests.
  • Ulcers: offloading with a total contact cast or a non-removable knee-high walker for plantar ulcers; infection and artery disease need urgent specialist care.
  • Ulcer grading: Wagner grades 0–5 (0 at-risk foot, 1 superficial ulcer, 2 deep ulcer, 3 with abscess or bone infection, 4 gangrene of part of the foot, 5 gangrene of the whole foot).

Foot risk categories and screening (IWGDF, 2023)

CategoryFindingsScreen
0, very lowNo loss of protective sensation (LOPS), no peripheral artery disease (PAD)Once a year
1, lowLOPS or PADEvery 6–12 months
2, moderateLOPS and PAD, or either with foot deformityEvery 3–6 months
3, highLOPS or PAD with a past ulcer, an amputation or end-stage kidney diseaseEvery 1–3 months

ICF summary: the S16 case

"A 50 year old female is a known case of diabetes since last 8 years. She complains of numbness in bilateral lower extremities and while walking she feels as if she is walking on cotton. She also complains of inability to hold her footwear (slippers) while walking. Symptoms are gradually progressing. Plan investigations. Plan short term goals and long term goals. Write physiotherapy management for her." (3 + 5 + 7)

  • Impairments: loss of protective sensation and position sense in both feet; weak intrinsic and toe muscles (cannot grip slippers); possible clawing and high-pressure areas; poor balance.
  • Activities: unsteady walking, worse in the dark and on uneven ground; footwear falls off; risk of falls.
  • Participation: housework, market and religious outings, family roles.
  • Environment: loose slippers and barefoot walking at home are barriers; family and diabetes clinic are facilitators.
  • Functional diagnosis: a 50-year-old woman with progressive diabetic distal symmetric polyneuropathy, with loss of protective sensation and weak foot muscles, causing unsteady walking and a high risk of falls and foot ulcers.

Physiotherapy by key problem

ProblemTechnique and doseWhy
Ulcer riskDaily foot inspection with a mirror; proper-fitting protective footwear (no barefoot walking, socks only or thin slippers); callus and nail care; screening by risk categoryThe person cannot feel injuries or high pressure
Weak foot and ankle, stiff jointsFoot-ankle exercise programme (toe flexion and extension, ankle range and strength), preferably supervisedImproves range and strength and may lower plantar pressure (IWGDF: may be considered)
Poor balanceStatic and dynamic balance training with vision, then on varied surfaces; safe home lightingVision and training compensate for lost position sense; reduces falls
Low activity and poor glucose controlWalking and other weight-bearing activity in proper footwear, building up to an extra 1000 steps a day; non-weight-bearing exercise (cycling, swimming) if an ulcer is presentFoot risk is not a barrier to training if footwear is right (IWGDF 2023); activity improves glucose control
Burning painGraded activity, education; TENS may be tried; medicines from the doctorSee neuropathic pain below
Ulcer presentOffloading (total contact cast or non-removable walker), crutches or a wheelchair; keep the rest of the body activePressure relief is the key to healing

Precautions

  • No heat, ice or electrical treatment over numb feet without close checks.
  • Check the feet after every exercise session; stop weight-bearing exercise if a new red area or blister appears.
  • Check blood sugar before exercise; carry sugar for hypoglycaemia; watch orthostatic hypotension.
  • A hot, swollen foot: suspect Charcot foot or infection and refer the same day.

Exam points

  • Classify (diffuse, mononeuropathy, radiculopathy), then give diabetic foot care: inspection, footwear, skin and nail care, exercise, offloading, education.
  • Name the 10 g monofilament and the IWGDF risk categories.

2Leprosy neuropathy

Asked as a long case in W25 and for 7 marks (leprous neuritis and lepromatous leprosy) in W22. India reported about 59% of the world's new cases in 2023 (107,851 of 182,815).

Definition and types

  • A chronic infection with Mycobacterium leprae that affects the skin and peripheral nerves.
  • WHO groups for treatment: paucibacillary (1–5 skin lesions, smear negative) and multibacillary (more than 5 lesions, nerve involvement or smear positive).
  • Ridley–Jopling spectrum: tuberculoid, borderline tuberculoid, mid-borderline, borderline lepromatous, lepromatous.

Causes and key features

  • Hypopigmented or reddish skin patches with loss of sensation; thickened nerves (ulnar at the elbow, common peroneal at the fibular neck, posterior tibial, median, facial, great auricular).
  • Deformities: claw hand (ulnar and median), wrist drop (rarely radial), foot drop (peroneal), clawed toes and plantar ulcers (posterior tibial), lagophthalmos (facial).
  • Lepromatous leprosy (W22): many symmetrical lesions and nodules, thickened facial skin ("leonine face"), loss of eyebrows, a collapsed nose, glove-and-stocking anaesthesia, symmetrical nerve thickening, eye damage; skin smears strongly positive.
  • Leprous neuritis (W22): a painful, tender, swollen nerve with new loss of sensation or power, often during a reaction (type 1, reversal reaction; or type 2, erythema nodosum leprosum); "silent neuritis" causes loss without pain.
  • WHO disability grade: 0, no anaesthesia or visible damage; 1, anaesthesia without visible deformity; 2, visible deformity or damage (or severe eye problems).

Medical point that matters for physiotherapy

  • WHO (2018) multidrug therapy for all patients: rifampicin, dapsone and clofazimine, for 6 months (paucibacillary) or 12 months (multibacillary). Contacts may receive single-dose rifampicin.
  • Neuritis is treated with a course of oral steroids; the limb is rested during acute neuritis.
  • Reconstructive surgery (tendon transfers for claw hand, foot drop and lagophthalmos) once the disease and neuritis are controlled.

ICF summary: the W25 case

"A 42 year old labourer comes to the PT department with complaints of loss of sensation and power in both hands since past one month. On observation some red coloured patches were noted on his skin. He stays in a slum area with his wife and two children and is the only earning member. Explain the diagnosis with clinical reasoning. Plan short term and long term goals with physiotherapy management." (5 + 10)

  • Diagnosis with reasoning: leprosy, probably multibacillary (nerve involvement in both hands), with ulnar and median neuropathy. Reasoning: skin patches with sensory loss; weakness and numbness of both hands; nerve thickening to be checked at the elbows and wrists; living in a crowded area. Confirm with skin smear and nerve examination. Also rule out other neuropathies (diabetic, hereditary) and cervical radiculopathy.
  • Impairments: hand intrinsic weakness, early clawing, sensory loss in both hands, dry skin; possible acute neuritis.
  • Activities and participation: difficulty gripping tools and lifting; risk of burns and cuts; cannot work as a labourer; family income at risk; stigma.
  • Environment: free multidrug therapy and government leprosy services (facilitators); slum living, manual work with hot or sharp tools, stigma (barriers).

Physiotherapy by key problem

ProblemTechniqueWhy
Monitoring nerve functionVoluntary muscle testing and sensory testing (monofilaments) of hands, feet and eyes at diagnosis and at every visitDetects new nerve damage early so steroids can be started
Acute neuritisRest the limb in a splint in a functional position until pain settles; no exercise of the inflamed nerve; then gentle range and graded exerciseMovement over an inflamed nerve can increase damage
Claw handKnuckle-bender splint; passive stretching of the fingers; intrinsic exercises; prepare for and rehabilitate after tendon transferPrevents fixed contractures and keeps the hand useful
Dry, numb skinSelf-care: soak the hands and feet in water, scrape thick skin, then oil; daily inspection; padded tool handles and glovesPrevents cracks, burns and ulcers
Foot drop and plantar anaesthesiaAFO; protective microcellular rubber footwear; walking short distances; ulcer carePrevents trips and plantar ulcers
Work and stigmaTool changes, work planning, vocational training, family counsellingKeeps the sole earner working; reduces isolation

Precautions

  • Do not exercise an acutely painful, tender nerve; refer for steroids.
  • Protect numb skin from heat (cooking, smoking, hot tools) and from splint pressure.
  • Leprosy is not spread by touching; treated patients quickly become non-infectious.

Exam points

  • Skin patch with lost sensation plus thickened nerves equals leprosy until proven otherwise.
  • Physiotherapy answers: nerve function monitoring, neuritis rest, splints, self-care (soak, scrape, oil), footwear, surgery rehabilitation, stigma.

3Alcoholic and other neuropathies

Asked for 7 marks in S25.

Definition and key features

  • A length-dependent, mainly axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy from long-term heavy drinking, made worse by poor nutrition (especially thiamine deficiency).
  • Burning, painful feet; numbness in a glove-and-stocking pattern; lost ankle reflexes; sensory ataxia (worse in the dark); distal weakness and foot drop in severe cases; muscle wasting; autonomic problems.
  • Other causes of polyneuropathy: vitamin B12 deficiency, chronic kidney disease, hypothyroidism, drugs (isoniazid without pyridoxine, some chemotherapy), HIV, hereditary neuropathy (Charcot–Marie–Tooth).

Medical point

Stop alcohol; thiamine and other vitamins; a good diet; medicines for nerve pain. Recovery is slow and often partial.

Physiotherapy by key problem

  • Sensory ataxia and falls: balance training with visual feedback; a stick; good lighting; Frenkel's exercises.
  • Distal weakness: progressive strengthening of ankle and hand muscles; AFO for foot drop.
  • Pain: graded activity; TENS trial; education.
  • Foot protection: daily inspection and footwear, as for the diabetic foot.
  • General fitness and support to stay off alcohol.

Precautions

  • Watch for withdrawal, low blood sugar and liver disease; protect numb skin.

4Neuropathic pain

Asked for 7 marks in W21.

  • Definition (IASP): pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system.
  • Features: burning, shooting or electric pain; pins and needles; pain from light touch (allodynia); more pain from a mildly painful stimulus (hyperalgesia); numbness in the same area.
  • Causes: diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, nerve injury, radiculopathy, spinal cord injury, stroke, multiple sclerosis, phantom limb pain.
  • Medicines (prescribed by the doctor): first-line gabapentin or pregabalin, duloxetine, or amitriptyline.

Physiotherapy

  • Explain pain in plain words: hurt does not always mean harm; nerves can become over-sensitive.
  • Graded activity and regular exercise, with pacing to avoid boom-and-bust.
  • Desensitisation for allodynia: graded textures and pressure, from tolerable to less tolerable.
  • Sensory discrimination training, graded motor imagery and mirror therapy for CRPS and phantom pain.
  • TENS may be tried for short-term relief; relaxation and sleep advice.

5Complex regional pain syndrome (reflex sympathetic dystrophy)

Asked for 7 marks in S21.

Definition and types

  • A painful condition of a limb, usually after an injury, surgery or immobilisation (often a wrist fracture), in which the pain is out of proportion to the injury and the limb shows sensory, vasomotor, sweating, swelling, motor and trophic changes.
  • Type I (formerly reflex sympathetic dystrophy): no definite nerve injury. Type II (formerly causalgia): with a nerve injury.

Key features and diagnosis (Budapest criteria)

  • Continuing pain disproportionate to the injury.
  • At least one symptom in three of four groups, and at least one sign in two of four groups: sensory (allodynia, hyperalgesia); vasomotor (temperature or colour change); sudomotor and swelling (oedema, sweating change); motor and trophic (stiffness, weakness, tremor, hair, nail or skin changes).
  • No other diagnosis explains the picture.

Physiotherapy

  • Encourage gentle movement and functional use of the limb as early as possible; avoid prolonged immobilisation.
  • Graded motor imagery (left/right recognition, imagined movement, then mirror therapy); evidence suggests possible benefit.
  • Desensitisation and tactile discrimination training.
  • Oedema control: elevation, active pumping exercises, retrograde massage or compression if tolerated.
  • Graded exercise and return to normal activities and work; pacing.
  • Work with the team: medicines, psychology, occupational therapy.

Precautions

  • Do not force painful passive stretching; it can flare symptoms.
  • Never splint for long periods without movement.

6Nerve root lesions (radiculopathy)

  • Causes: disc prolapse, spondylosis with foraminal narrowing, tumours, herpes zoster; diabetic radiculopathy.
  • Features: pain along a dermatome (sciatica, brachialgia), sensory change in the dermatome, weakness of muscles in the myotome, and reduced reflexes at that level (for example C6: biceps and brachioradialis reflexes; L5: big toe extension; S1: ankle reflex).
  • Peripheral nerve vs root: a root lesion follows a dermatome and myotome; a nerve lesion follows the nerve's own area. A root lesion may weaken muscles supplied by different nerves from the same root.
  • Red flags: bladder or bowel loss and saddle numbness (cauda equina), progressive weakness: refer urgently.
  • Physiotherapy: education, staying active, directional preference exercises, neural mobilisation, traction where appropriate, strengthening, ergonomic advice.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mList three types of diabetic neuropathy.

Distal symmetric polyneuropathy; autonomic neuropathy; mononeuropathy (single or multiple nerves); radiculopathy such as diabetic amyotrophy.

3 mWrite three features of lepromatous leprosy.

Many symmetrical skin lesions and nodules with a thickened "leonine" face; loss of eyebrows and a collapsed nose; glove-and-stocking anaesthesia with symmetrical nerve thickening; strongly positive skin smears.

7 mPhysiotherapy management of the diabetic foot.

Assess sensation (10 g monofilament, tuning fork), pulses, deformity, skin, footwear and the IWGDF risk category. Teach daily inspection with a mirror, washing and drying between the toes, moisturising, nail care. Prescribe protective footwear; no barefoot walking, socks only or thin slippers. Foot-ankle exercises and balance training. Graded walking in proper footwear, adding about 1000 steps a day; non-weight-bearing exercise if an ulcer is present. Offloading (total contact cast or non-removable walker) for plantar ulcers. Precautions: no heat on numb feet, check blood sugar, refer a hot swollen foot.

LAQA 50-year-old diabetic woman has numb feet, feels she walks on cotton and cannot keep her slippers on. Plan investigations, goals and physiotherapy. (3 + 5 + 7, S16)

Investigations (3): fasting sugar and HbA1c; monofilament and tuning fork; nerve conduction studies; foot pulses and ankle-brachial index; vitamin B12, kidney and thyroid tests.

Goals (5): short-term: daily foot care routine, proper footwear, safer walking with balance training, no new skin damage. Long-term: no ulcers or falls, active lifestyle with walking every day, good sugar control.

Physiotherapy (7): education and self-inspection; protective footwear with a back strap; foot-ankle strengthening (toe grip, ankle range); balance training; graded walking programme; home safety and lighting; pain management if needed; screening every 6–12 months (IWGDF risk 1).

MCQThe standard screening tool for loss of protective sensation in the diabetic foot is:A. PinwheelB. 10 g Semmes–Weinstein monofilamentC. Two-point discriminatorD. Reflex hammer

B. A 128 Hz tuning fork or the Ipswich touch test can be used when it is not available.

MCQThe WHO (2018) treatment for multibacillary leprosy is:A. Dapsone alone for 6 monthsB. Rifampicin, dapsone and clofazimine for 12 monthsC. Rifampicin and dapsone for 6 monthsD. Steroids only

B. Paucibacillary leprosy gets the same three drugs for 6 months.

MCQIn acute leprous neuritis, the first physiotherapy step is:A. Vigorous stretchingB. Resting the limb in a splint and referring for steroidsC. Resisted exercise of the affected musclesD. Heat over the nerve

B. Exercise over an inflamed nerve can increase damage.
