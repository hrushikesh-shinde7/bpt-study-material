3(d)Theories of ageing: definition and types of ageing, biological (programmed and damage) and psychosocial theories

"Theories of ageing" was asked in 9 of 20 papers, almost always as a 7-mark question that says "enumerate and explain any three". Learn the classification tree first, then three theories in detail: one programmed, one damage, one psychosocial, each with a line on what it means for physiotherapy.

My revision

1Ageing and its types

Define ageing, mention types of ageing and enumerate genetic theories (W16, 7 marks).

  • Ageing: the progressive, time-related decline in the structure and function of the body that reduces the ability to adapt to stress and raises the risk of disease and death. It is universal, progressive, intrinsic (from within) and harmful.
TypeMeaning
ChronologicalAge in years since birth
Biological (physiological)Age of the body's systems; two people of 70 may differ greatly
PsychologicalChanges in memory, learning, personality and coping
SocialChanging roles: retirement, grandparenthood, widowhood
FunctionalWhat the person can do compared with others of the same age
Primary and secondaryPrimary: changes due to age itself. Secondary: changes added by disease, inactivity and lifestyle
  • Usual and successful ageing (Rowe and Kahn): successful ageing means a low probability of disease and disability, high physical and cognitive function, and active engagement with life. Physiotherapy aims for successful ageing (see the fun-to-failure model).

2Classification of the theories

Classic answer vs current view: one theory or many causes

Classic

Separate, competing theories: ageing is either programmed or the result of damage.

Current

Ageing is described as a set of interacting hallmarks of ageing: nine in 2013, twelve in the 2023 update. They are genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, disabled autophagy, deregulated nutrient-sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation and dysbiosis (López-Otín et al., 2023). The old theories live on inside them: free radical damage within mitochondrial dysfunction, the telomere theory within telomere attrition and senescence, the immunological theory within chronic inflammation.

Write in the exam

"No single theory explains ageing. Modern research sees it as interacting hallmarks such as DNA damage, telomere shortening, mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation."

3Programmed (genetic) theories

"Enumerate genetic theories of ageing" (W16).

TheoryIdeaEvidence or example
Genetic clock (programmed longevity)Lifespan is set by genes that switch on and off in sequenceEach species has a typical maximum lifespan; long-lived families; progeria shows early ageing from one gene
Hayflick limit (cellular senescence)Normal cells divide a limited number of times and then stop (senescence)Human fibroblasts in culture stop after about 40–60 divisions (Hayflick and Moorhead, 1961); senescent cells build up in old tissues
Telomere theoryTelomeres (protective ends of chromosomes) shorten at each division; when too short, the cell stops dividingExplains the Hayflick limit; the enzyme telomerase restores telomeres in germ and cancer cells
Endocrine (neuroendocrine; Dilman, 1971)A biological clock in the hypothalamus and pituitary drives ageing through hormonesFall in growth hormone, sex hormones and melatonin; menopause; insulin and IGF-1 signalling affects lifespan in animals
Immunological (Walford, 1969)The immune system declines on a programme (thymus shrinks; immunosenescence), so infections, cancer and autoimmunity riseOlder people get more pneumonia and shingles and respond less to vaccines. The chronic low-grade inflammation of old age was named "inflammaging" (Franceschi et al., 2000)

Telomere theory in full (any one theory, 7 marks in S21)

  • Background: Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) found that normal human fetal fibroblasts stop dividing after about 50 population doublings: the Hayflick limit. Cells from adults divide fewer times.
  • Telomeres: repeats of the DNA sequence TTAGGG, with their proteins, that cap the ends of each chromosome like the plastic tip of a shoelace. They stop the chromosome ends being treated as broken DNA.
  • End-replication problem: DNA polymerase cannot copy the very end of a chromosome, so a little telomere is lost at every division (predicted by Olovnikov, 1971–73, and Watson, 1972).
  • Shortening: telomeres of cultured fibroblasts shorten with each division (Harley et al., 1990). In blood cells they are about 9.5 kb long at birth (Factor-Litvak et al., 2016) and shortened by about 27 base pairs a year in adult women (Valdes et al., 2005).
  • Senescence: when telomeres become critically short, the cell stops dividing or dies. Senescent cells build up in old tissues, which lose their power to renew and repair.
  • Telomerase: the enzyme that rebuilds telomeres. It is active in germ cells and most cancers but low in most body cells. Adding telomerase to normal human cells let them pass their normal lifespan by at least 20 doublings (Bodnar et al., 1998). Blackburn, Greider and Szostak won the 2009 Nobel Prize for showing how telomeres and telomerase protect chromosomes.
Evidence forEvidence against and limits
Telomeres shorten with cell division and with ageMice have telomeres up to 150 kb long, far longer than ours, yet live only about 3.5 years
Telomeres of obese women were 240 base pairs shorter, and each pack-year of smoking was equal to about 5 base pairs lost (Valdes et al., 2005)Telomerase is switched on in about 90% of tumours (Kim et al., 1994), so lengthening telomeres carries a cancer risk
Women under the highest stress had telomeres shorter by the equal of at least 10 years of ageing (Epel et al., 2004)A meta-analysis of 9 exercise trials found no overall effect on telomere length (Sánchez-González et al., 2024)
In twins, the most active had telomeres 200 nucleotides longer than the least active (Cherkas et al., 2008)Short telomeres explain the limit on cell division, not every feature of ageing

Meaning for physiotherapy: regular activity, not smoking, weight control and stress management go with longer telomeres. Trials have not yet shown that exercise lengthens them, so the main reason to exercise is still function.

4Damage or error (stochastic) theories

TheoryIdeaEvidence or example
Free radical (Harman, 1956)Reactive oxygen species made during normal metabolism damage DNA, proteins and cell membranes; damage to mitochondria makes more radicals (mitochondrial theory)Oxidative damage rises with age; lipofuscin (age pigment) builds up; antioxidant enzymes protect
Wear and tear (Weismann, 1882)Cells and tissues wear out with repeated use, like a machineCartilage wear in osteoarthritis; but the body also repairs, so this is only part of the story
Cross-linking or glycation (Bjorksten, 1942)Proteins such as collagen and elastin form cross-links (helped by glucose: advanced glycation end products), so tissues stiffenStiff skin, arteries, tendons, lens (cataract), more in diabetes
DNA damage (somatic mutation)Mutations build up in body cells and are not fully repairedCancer risk rises with age; DNA-repair disorders age early
Error catastrophe (Orgel, 1963)Errors in making proteins accumulate until cells failFaulty proteins in old cells; largely not supported as the main cause
Rate of living (Rubner, 1908; Pearl, 1928)The faster the metabolism, the shorter the lifeSmall animals with fast metabolism live shorter; birds are an exception

Free radical theory in full (7 marks in S21)

  • Proposed by Denham Harman in 1956. In 1972 he pointed to the mitochondria as the main site, the mitochondrial free radical theory.
  • Free radical: an atom or molecule with an unpaired electron, so it reacts readily with whatever it meets. The main reactive oxygen species (ROS) are the superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide (not itself a radical, but it forms one) and the hydroxyl radical.
  • Source: normal metabolism. Electrons leak from the respiratory chain in the mitochondria and react with oxygen; enzymes such as NADPH oxidase also make superoxide. The often-quoted 1–2% of oxygen turned into superoxide comes from isolated mitochondria; the true rate in the body is lower and debated (Murphy, 2009).
  • Damage: peroxidation of membrane lipids; oxidised proteins (carbonyls) that lose function; DNA damage such as 8-oxo-deoxyguanosine, which causes mutations. Damaged mitochondria leak more radicals, a vicious circle.
  • Defences: the antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase, catalase and glutathione peroxidase; dietary antioxidants such as vitamin C, vitamin E and beta-carotene; repair systems.
  • Ageing follows when damage outruns defence and repair. Lipofuscin, the "age pigment", is oxidised and cross-linked protein and lipid that lysosomes cannot digest; it builds up in old cells.
Evidence forEvidence against and limits
Oxidative damage to lipids, proteins and DNA rises with ageAntioxidant pills do not prolong life: in 68 trials with 232,606 people, beta-carotene, vitamin A and vitamin E slightly raised mortality (Bjelakovic et al., 2007)
Lipofuscin builds up in old cellsNaked mole-rats live more than 28 years, about 8 times longer than mice, despite more oxidative damage (Andziak et al., 2006)
Cells have a whole system of antioxidant enzymes to limit the damageIn mice, only 1 of 18 genetic changes to antioxidant defences altered lifespan (Pérez et al., 2009)

Conclusion: oxidative damage contributes to ageing but is not its single cause.

Exercise and free radicals: a single hard bout raises radical production for a short time, but regular training lowers the oxidative challenge and raises the body's own antioxidant enzymes, an adaptive (hormesis) response (Radak et al., 2008). High-dose antioxidant pills blunt this: vitamin C 1000 mg with vitamin E 400 IU a day blocked the training gain in insulin sensitivity (Ristow et al., 2009). Advise regular moderate exercise, a diet rich in fruit and vegetables, no smoking, and no antioxidant pills for this purpose.

Wear-and-tear theory in full

  • Proposed by August Weismann, in a lecture given in 1881 and printed in 1882 ("On the duration of life").
  • Idea: the body is like a machine. Cells and tissues wear out with repeated use, and the damage of daily living builds up until organs fail. Weismann held that body cells have only a limited power to divide and replace worn parts.
  • Examples: worn articular cartilage in osteoarthritis of the weight-bearing joints; stiffer, less elastic tendons and discs.
  • Related idea: the rate-of-living theory (Rubner, 1908; Pearl, 1928): the faster the metabolism, the faster the wear and the shorter the life.
  • Limits: unlike a machine, the body repairs itself, and tissues get stronger with use: bone, muscle and tendon adapt to load. Disuse causes more loss than use. The theory does not explain why species age at such different rates.
  • Meaning for physiotherapy: protect joints from excess load (weight control, good technique, aids), but keep them moving and loaded within limits.

5Psychosocial theories

TheoryIdeaComment
Disengagement (Cumming and Henry, 1961)Older people and society withdraw from each other naturally and by mutual agreementCriticised: withdrawal is not inevitable and often harms health
Activity (Havighurst, 1961)Staying physically and socially active and replacing lost roles leads to successful ageing and life satisfactionBasis of exercise groups, day centres and volunteering
Continuity (Atchley, 1989)People adapt best by keeping the habits, interests and relationships of their earlier lifePlan activities that fit the person's past (gardening, temple visits, walking group)
Erikson's eighth stageOld age is the stage of ego integrity versus despair: looking back on life with acceptance or regretReminiscence, respect and purposeful roles support integrity

6What the theories mean for physiotherapy

  • Ageing has many causes acting together; no single theory explains it, and much of the decline is secondary (inactivity, disease), which physiotherapy can change.
  • Free radical: regular moderate exercise increases the body's own antioxidant defences; antioxidant pills do not slow ageing and may blunt training gains; avoid smoking and excess sun.
  • Telomere: regular activity, no smoking, weight control and stress management go with longer telomeres.
  • Cross-linking: daily stretching and range of motion counter tissue stiffness; good diabetes control slows glycation.
  • Wear and tear: joint protection, weight control and strengthening reduce joint load; but disuse is worse than use.
  • Immunological and endocrine: exercise improves immune function and insulin sensitivity; vaccination advice; care with infection in groups.
  • Activity and continuity: group exercise, walking clubs and meaningful roles; plan programmes around the person's past interests.
  • Disengagement: recognise withdrawal and low mood as risks, not a normal part of ageing; encourage social participation.

3 mEnumerate three theories of ageing. (W22)

Programmed: Hayflick limit or telomere theory (cells divide a limited number of times as telomeres shorten). Damage: free radical theory (Harman 1956: reactive oxygen species damage cell components). Psychosocial: activity theory (Havighurst: staying active brings successful ageing). Any three with one line each.

7 mEnumerate theories of ageing and explain any three. (W20)

Define ageing. Tree: programmed (genetic clock, Hayflick, telomere, endocrine, immunological), damage (free radical, wear and tear, cross-linking, DNA damage, error catastrophe, rate of living), psychosocial (disengagement, activity, continuity, Erikson). Explain free radical, telomere and activity theories with evidence and PT relevance.

7 mEnumerate theories of ageing and describe any one. (S21)

Define ageing; draw the tree. Describe the free radical theory: Harman 1956, mitochondrial version 1972; free radical defined; superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl; made by normal metabolism in mitochondria; damage to membrane lipids, proteins and DNA; vicious circle; defences (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase, vitamins C and E); lipofuscin; evidence for and against (antioxidant trials, naked mole-rat); PT meaning (regular exercise raises own defences). Or describe the telomere theory: Hayflick limit about 50 doublings, TTAGGG caps, end-replication problem, senescence, telomerase, Nobel 2009, evidence and limits.

7 mDefine ageing. Mention types of ageing. Enumerate genetic theories of ageing. (W16)

Definition and features of ageing. Types: chronological, biological, psychological, social, functional; primary and secondary. Genetic (programmed) theories: genetic clock, Hayflick limit, telomere, endocrine, immunological, with one line each.
