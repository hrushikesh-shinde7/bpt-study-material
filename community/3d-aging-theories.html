3(d)Theories of ageing: definition and types of ageing, biological (programmed and damage) and psychosocial theories

"Theories of ageing" was asked in 9 of 20 papers, almost always as a 7-mark question that says "enumerate and explain any three". Learn the classification tree first, then three theories in detail: one programmed, one damage, one psychosocial, each with a line on what it means for physiotherapy.

My revision

1Ageing and its types

Define ageing, mention types of ageing and enumerate genetic theories (W16, 7 marks).

  • Ageing: the progressive, time-related decline in the structure and function of the body that reduces the ability to adapt to stress and raises the risk of disease and death. It is universal, progressive, intrinsic (from within) and harmful.
TypeMeaning
ChronologicalAge in years since birth
Biological (physiological)Age of the body's systems; two people of 70 may differ greatly
PsychologicalChanges in memory, learning, personality and coping
SocialChanging roles: retirement, grandparenthood, widowhood
FunctionalWhat the person can do compared with others of the same age
Primary and secondaryPrimary: changes due to age itself. Secondary: changes added by disease, inactivity and lifestyle
  • Usual and successful ageing (Rowe and Kahn): successful ageing means a low probability of disease and disability, high physical and cognitive function, and active engagement with life. Physiotherapy aims for successful ageing (see the fun-to-failure model).

2Classification of the theories

3Programmed (genetic) theories

"Enumerate genetic theories of ageing" (W16).

TheoryIdeaEvidence or example
Genetic clock (programmed longevity)Lifespan is set by genes that switch on and off in sequenceEach species has a typical maximum lifespan; long-lived families; progeria shows early ageing from one gene
Hayflick limit (cellular senescence)Normal cells divide a limited number of times and then stop (senescence)Human fibroblasts in culture stop after about 40–60 divisions (Hayflick and Moorhead, 1961); senescent cells build up in old tissues
Telomere theoryTelomeres (protective ends of chromosomes) shorten at each division; when too short, the cell stops dividingExplains the Hayflick limit; the enzyme telomerase restores telomeres in germ and cancer cells
Endocrine (neuroendocrine)A biological clock in the hypothalamus and pituitary drives ageing through hormonesFall in growth hormone, sex hormones and melatonin; menopause; insulin and IGF-1 signalling affects lifespan in animals
ImmunologicalThe immune system declines on a programme (thymus shrinks), so infections, cancer and autoimmunity riseOlder people get more pneumonia and shingles and respond less to vaccines

4Damage or error (stochastic) theories

TheoryIdeaEvidence or example
Free radical (Harman, 1956)Reactive oxygen species made during normal metabolism damage DNA, proteins and cell membranes; damage to mitochondria makes more radicals (mitochondrial theory)Oxidative damage rises with age; lipofuscin (age pigment) builds up; antioxidant enzymes protect
Wear and tear (Weismann)Cells and tissues wear out with repeated use, like a machineCartilage wear in osteoarthritis; but the body also repairs, so this is only part of the story
Cross-linking or glycation (Bjorksten, 1942)Proteins such as collagen and elastin form cross-links (helped by glucose: advanced glycation end products), so tissues stiffenStiff skin, arteries, tendons, lens (cataract), more in diabetes
DNA damage (somatic mutation)Mutations build up in body cells and are not fully repairedCancer risk rises with age; DNA-repair disorders age early
Error catastrophe (Orgel, 1963)Errors in making proteins accumulate until cells failFaulty proteins in old cells; largely not supported as the main cause
Rate of living (Pearl, 1928)The faster the metabolism, the shorter the lifeSmall animals with fast metabolism live shorter; birds are an exception

5Psychosocial theories

TheoryIdeaComment
Disengagement (Cumming and Henry, 1961)Older people and society withdraw from each other naturally and by mutual agreementCriticised: withdrawal is not inevitable and often harms health
Activity (Havighurst, 1961)Staying physically and socially active and replacing lost roles leads to successful ageing and life satisfactionBasis of exercise groups, day centres and volunteering
Continuity (Atchley, 1989)People adapt best by keeping the habits, interests and relationships of their earlier lifePlan activities that fit the person's past (gardening, temple visits, walking group)
Erikson's eighth stageOld age is the stage of ego integrity versus despair: looking back on life with acceptance or regretReminiscence, respect and purposeful roles support integrity

6What the theories mean for physiotherapy

  • Ageing has many causes acting together; no single theory explains it, and much of the decline is secondary (inactivity, disease), which physiotherapy can change.
  • Free radical: regular moderate exercise increases the body's own antioxidant defences; avoid smoking and excess sun.
  • Cross-linking: daily stretching and range of motion counter tissue stiffness; good diabetes control slows glycation.
  • Wear and tear: joint protection, weight control and strengthening reduce joint load; but disuse is worse than use.
  • Immunological and endocrine: exercise improves immune function and insulin sensitivity; vaccination advice; care with infection in groups.
  • Activity and continuity: group exercise, walking clubs and meaningful roles; plan programmes around the person's past interests.
  • Disengagement: recognise withdrawal and low mood as risks, not a normal part of ageing; encourage social participation.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mEnumerate three theories of ageing. (W22)

Programmed: Hayflick limit or telomere theory (cells divide a limited number of times as telomeres shorten). Damage: free radical theory (Harman 1956: reactive oxygen species damage cell components). Psychosocial: activity theory (Havighurst: staying active brings successful ageing). Any three with one line each.

7 mEnumerate theories of ageing and explain any three. (W20)

Define ageing. Tree: programmed (genetic clock, Hayflick, telomere, endocrine, immunological), damage (free radical, wear and tear, cross-linking, DNA damage, error catastrophe, rate of living), psychosocial (disengagement, activity, continuity, Erikson). Explain free radical, telomere and activity theories with evidence and PT relevance.

7 mDefine ageing. Mention types of ageing. Enumerate genetic theories of ageing. (W16)

Definition and features of ageing. Types: chronological, biological, psychological, social, functional; primary and secondary. Genetic (programmed) theories: genetic clock, Hayflick limit, telomere, endocrine, immunological, with one line each.

MCQThe Hayflick limit refers to:A. Maximum heart rate in old ageB. The limited number of divisions of normal cellsC. The upper limit of free radicalsD. The age of retirement

B.

MCQStiffening of collagen with age is best explained by the:A. Disengagement theoryB. Cross-linking theoryC. Continuity theoryD. Rate of living theory

B.

MCQThe theory that staying active and socially involved leads to successful ageing is the:A. Activity theoryB. Wear and tear theoryC. Disengagement theoryD. Error catastrophe theory

A.
