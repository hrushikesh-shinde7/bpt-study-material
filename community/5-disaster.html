1What a disaster is, and its types

Types of disaster and principles of disaster management (W19, S24, 7 marks).

  • Definition (Disaster Management Act, 2005): a catastrophe, mishap, calamity or grave occurrence, from natural or man-made causes, or by accident or negligence, that causes substantial loss of life, human suffering, or damage to property or the environment, and is beyond the coping capacity of the affected community.
  • Hazard is the threat (an earthquake); vulnerability is how easily people are harmed (weak buildings, poverty, disability); disaster happens when a hazard meets a vulnerable community; risk = hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity.
TypeExamples (India)
Natural: geophysicalEarthquakes (Latur 1993, Bhuj 2001), tsunami (2004), landslides
Natural: hydrological and weatherFloods (Kerala 2018), cyclones (Odisha 1999), cloudbursts, droughts, heat and cold waves
Natural: biologicalEpidemics and pandemics (COVID-19)
Man-made: technologicalIndustrial and chemical accidents (Bhopal gas leak 1984), fires, building collapse, nuclear accidents
Man-made: transport and crowdsRail and road accidents, stampedes
Man-made: conflictTerrorism, bomb blasts, war, riots

Disasters may also be classified by onset (sudden, like an earthquake, or slow, like drought) and by scale (local, state, national).

2Principles of disaster management

  • Comprehensive: all hazards, all phases, all people affected.
  • Progressive: anticipate future disasters and act before them (preparedness, mitigation).
  • Risk-driven: priorities set by hazard and vulnerability assessment.
  • Integrated and coordinated: all levels of government, agencies, NGOs and communities work under one plan and command.
  • Collaborative: trust and partnership among organisations and the community.
  • Flexible: adapt to each situation.
  • Professional: trained people, standards, evidence.
  • Community participation and inclusion: local people, women, older people and persons with disability involved in planning; "build back better" during recovery (Sendai Framework).

Disaster management in India

  • Disaster Management Act, 2005: National Disaster Management Authority (chaired by the Prime Minister), State and District Disaster Management Authorities; National Disaster Response Force (NDRF); National Institute of Disaster Management.
  • National Disaster Management Plan (2016) aligned with the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030).

3The disaster management cycle

Different steps of the disaster management cycle (S26, long answer).

PhaseActivities
Mitigation (prevention)Earthquake-resistant buildings, flood walls, land-use rules, industrial safety, accessible design, risk mapping
PreparednessDisaster plans for hospitals and communities, early warning systems, stockpiles, training in first aid and triage, mock drills, lists of vulnerable people (elderly, disabled) and how to evacuate them
ResponseSearch and rescue, triage, first aid, evacuation, field hospitals, shelter, food, water, sanitation, prevention of epidemics, psychological first aid
RecoveryRehabilitation of the injured, reconstruction of homes and services, livelihoods, mental health care, long-term disability support

4Triage

Triage approach (S26, long answer).

  • Triage: sorting casualties by urgency so the most people survive when resources are limited.
  • START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) takes under a minute per adult and uses colour tags.
TagMeaningExamples
Red (immediate)Life-threatening but can be saved with quick treatmentAirway problems, severe bleeding, shock, tension pneumothorax
Yellow (delayed)Serious but stable; can wait hoursClosed fractures, stable spinal injury, moderate burns
Green (minor)Walking woundedSprains, cuts, bruises
Black (expectant or dead)Dead, or injuries not survivable with available resourcesNot breathing after airway opening

Children are triaged with JumpSTART, which adjusts the breathing criteria.

5Role of physiotherapists in disaster management

Role of physiotherapists in disaster management (S26, long answer).

  • Common injuries: fractures, spinal cord injury, traumatic amputation, crush injury, peripheral nerve injury, head injury, burns, soft-tissue injuries; later, chronic pain, disability and psychological trauma. Earthquakes produce many spinal cord injuries and fractures.
PhaseRole of the physiotherapist
Mitigation and preparednessBe part of hospital and community disaster plans; train in first aid, triage, safe lifting and spinal handling; keep stocks of crutches, walkers, wheelchairs, splints; identify and plan evacuation for persons with disability and older people; teach communities safe evacuation and fall-safe behaviour
Response (acute)Support triage and casualty handling; safe transfer and positioning of spinal injuries; splinting and positioning of fractures; chest physiotherapy and breathing exercises; early mobilisation; pressure-sore prevention; pre- and post-amputation care; burns positioning; assistive devices at discharge so beds free up; psychological first aid. WHO standards ask field hospitals for at least one rehabilitation professional per 20 beds
Recovery (long term)Rehabilitation of spinal cord injury, amputation, fractures, nerve and brain injury; prostheses, orthoses and wheelchairs; home and shelter accessibility; community-based rehabilitation; vocational rehabilitation and return to work; follow-up of complications; support for mental health referral
ThroughoutRecord keeping and data, coordination with the team, care of the rescuers' own health, research and advocacy for inclusive disaster plans
