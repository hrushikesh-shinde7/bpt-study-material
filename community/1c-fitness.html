Field tests in athlete training (7 marks in S16)

A field test is a test done away from the laboratory that needs neither extensive training nor expensive equipment (National Strength and Conditioning Association, NSCA). A good test is valid: it measures what it claims to measure. It is also reliable: it gives the same result when repeated. Physiotherapists use field tests for pre-season screening, to set training loads, to track progress and to decide return to play after injury.

Order and conditions of testing

Test the least tiring items first, so fatigue from one test does not spoil the next. The NSCA order is:

  1. Non-fatiguing tests: height, weight, flexibility, skinfolds, vertical jump.
  2. Agility tests (T-test, Illinois).
  3. Maximal power and strength tests (1RM).
  4. Sprint tests.
  5. Local muscular endurance tests (push-ups).
  6. Fatiguing anaerobic tests (RAST).
  7. Aerobic capacity tests (beep test, Yo-Yo, Cooper run), last or on another day.
  • Standardise: the same warm-up, surface, footwear, time of day and order each time; record the weather.
  • Tester: trained, gives clear instructions and a demonstration, allows full rest between maximal attempts.
  • Repeat at set points (pre-season, mid-season, after injury) and compare with the athlete's own earlier scores.

3Energy systems, aerobic and anaerobic exercise, and the MET

Energy systems (W16, 7 marks); aerobic and anaerobic exercise with examples and the MET (W22, 7 marks); aerobic vs anaerobic (W24) and what is aerobic exercise (S21), 3 marks.

Energy systems in detail (7 marks in W16)

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the only fuel a muscle can use directly. Stored ATP lasts only a few seconds of maximal contraction, so three systems rebuild it all the time.

SystemHow it rebuilds ATPRate, capacity and limits
Phosphagen (ATP–PC)Creatine phosphate passes its phosphate to ADP, helped by the enzyme creatine kinase. One step, no oxygen.Fastest rate but the smallest store, so it leads in all-out efforts of a few seconds. Creatine phosphate is rebuilt aerobically within minutes of rest.
Fast (anaerobic) glycolysisGlucose or glycogen is split to pyruvate in the cytoplasm, and pyruvate becomes lactate. Net 2 ATP per glucose, or 3 from muscle glycogen.Fast, with a medium store. Acid builds up in hard efforts and adds to fatigue.
Oxidative (aerobic)Pyruvate and fatty acids are burnt in the mitochondria (Krebs cycle and electron transport chain) to carbon dioxide and water. About 30–32 ATP per glucose (older books say 36–38); fat gives much more per molecule.Slowest rate but the largest store, so it leads at rest and in efforts longer than a few minutes.
Main energy system by length and intensity of effort (NSCA)
Length of effortIntensityMain system
0–6 sExtremely highPhosphagen
6–30 sVery highPhosphagen and fast glycolysis
30 s to 2 minHighFast glycolysis
2–3 minModerateFast glycolysis and oxidative
Over 3 minLowOxidative
  • Rate against capacity: the faster a system makes ATP, the less it can make in total. The phosphagen system is fastest and smallest; the oxidative system is slowest and largest.
  • Fuel mix: the respiratory exchange ratio (carbon dioxide out ÷ oxygen in) is about 0.7 when burning fat and 1.0 when burning carbohydrate. Carbohydrate use rises as intensity rises.
  • Lactate threshold: the intensity at which blood lactate starts to rise sharply. It begins at about 50–60% of VO₂max in untrained people and 70–80% in trained athletes (NSCA).
  • Oxygen deficit: at the start of exercise oxygen uptake lags behind demand, so the anaerobic systems cover the gap.
  • EPOC (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption): the extra oxygen used after exercise to restore the body to its resting state: rebuild creatine phosphate, clear lactate, and bring temperature and heart rate down.

The MET and how to use it (7 marks in W22)

  • MET (metabolic equivalent): the energy used sitting quietly at rest, 3.5 ml O₂/kg/min (about 1 kcal per kg per hour). Activities are graded as multiples of this.
  • Bands (Compendium of Physical Activities): sedentary 1.0–1.5 METs, light 1.6–2.9, moderate 3.0–5.9, vigorous 6.0 or more.
  • Energy cost: kcal per minute = METs × 3.5 × body mass (kg) ÷ 200. A 60 kg person walking briskly (4.8 METs) uses 4.8 × 3.5 × 60 ÷ 200, about 5 kcal a minute, or about 150 kcal in 30 minutes.
  • Weekly volume: MET-minutes = METs × minutes. Brisk walking for 30 minutes on 5 days gives 4.8 × 150 = 720 MET-min. ACSM advises 500–1000 MET-min a week (Garber et al., 2011).
  • Uses: grade and prescribe intensity; express weekly volume; compare activities; estimate energy use for weight control; choose activities by their MET cost in cardiac rehabilitation.
  • Limit: 3.5 ml/kg/min is an average. In 769 adults measured resting uptake averaged 2.6 ml/kg/min, so the standard MET overestimated it by about 35% (Byrne et al., 2005). Treat MET values as estimates and check effort with heart rate or RPE (rating of perceived exertion).

4Principles of training and exercise prescription

General principles of exercise and its phases (S16, 7 marks); principles of aerobic training (S19) and the FITT principle (W20), 3 marks; principles of exercise prescription (W25, 7 marks).

Principles of training

  • Overload: the body adapts only when stressed beyond its usual level.
  • Progression: increase the load gradually (usually duration first, then frequency, then intensity; about 10% a week).
  • Specificity: the body adapts to the exact type of training (aerobic training builds endurance, not strength).
  • Reversibility: gains are lost when training stops ("use it or lose it").
  • Individuality: people respond differently; prescribe for the person's age, fitness, disease and goals.
  • Recovery: adaptation happens during rest; too little rest leads to overtraining.

  • Flexibility: 2–3 days a week, each stretch held 10–30 seconds (30–60 in older adults), repeated to total about 60 seconds.
  • Neuromotor (balance, agility): 2–3 days a week, especially for older adults.
  • Heart rate target (Karvonen): resting HR + (maximum HR − resting HR) × intensity.

Steps of exercise prescription (W25)

  1. Screen: history, risk factors, medicines, red flags; medical clearance when needed.
  2. Test fitness (section 2) and set goals with the person.
  3. Write the FITT-VP for aerobic, resistance, flexibility and balance work.
  4. Teach warm-up, cool-down, self-monitoring (talk test, RPE, pulse) and warning signs.
  5. Progress, review and retest every 4–6 weeks; adjust for adherence and enjoyment.

A training programme also has stages: an initial stage (first few weeks, low intensity, building the habit), an improvement stage (the next few months, steady progression) and a maintenance stage (keeping gains long term).

5Circuit training

Define aerobic exercise and explain circuit training (S25, 7 marks).

Definition and method (7 marks in S25)

  • Definition: a series of exercises (stations) done one after another with little rest, working different muscle groups and adding aerobic stations; the whole set is repeated.
  • Origin: developed by R. E. Morgan and G. T. Adamson at the University of Leeds in 1953, and described in their book Circuit Training (1957).
  • Stations: about 10–15 exercises, set out so that upper body, lower body, trunk and aerobic work alternate.
  • Work: 30–40 s or 12–15 reps at each station, at 40–60% of 1RM for a weight circuit.
  • Rest: 15–30 s while moving to the next station.
  • Rounds: 1–3 circuits, about 30 minutes in all, with a warm-up before and a cool-down after.
  • Progress by adding a round, lengthening work time, shortening rest or adding load, one at a time.

Types of circuit

TypeHow it is set upMain effect
Circuit weight trainingLight weights, many repetitions, short rest. A classic study used 10 stations at 40–55% of 1RM, as many repetitions as possible in 30 s, 15 s to change, 3 circuits, 3 days a week (Wilmore et al., 1978)Muscular endurance and strength, with a modest aerobic gain
Aerobic circuitAerobic bouts (step-ups, skipping, cycling) between resistance stations, or a very light load with the heart rate kept at 70–80% of maximumCardiorespiratory fitness as well as muscular endurance
High-intensity circuit12 body-weight exercises, 30 s each with 10 s to change, repeated 2–3 times (Klika and Jordan, 2013)Fitness in a short time for healthy, fit adults
Circuit class therapyTask-practice stations (sit-to-stand, stepping, walking, reaching) for a group of patients, supervised by therapistsMobility and walking, for example after stroke
Sport-specific circuitSkills and movements of the sport at each stationFitness for the demands of that sport

What it achieves

  • Aerobic fitness: early circuit weight training studies raised VO₂max by an average of 5.1% in men and 8.0% in women (Gettman and Pollock, 1981). When the heart rate was kept at 70–80% of maximum (20% of 1RM, 10 stations, 40 s work and 20 s rest, 3 rounds), VO₂max rose 11%, close to the 12% after endurance training (Kaikkonen et al., 2000).
  • Energy cost: about 5.0 METs at moderate effort, 6.0 with body-weight exercises and 7.5 when vigorous with minimal rest (Compendium, 2024).
  • Patients: in a Cochrane review after stroke (17 trials, 1297 people), circuit class therapy improved the six-minute walk distance by about 61 m and gait speed by 0.15 m/s (English et al., 2017).
AdvantagesDisadvantages
Strength, muscular endurance and some aerobic fitness in one short sessionSmaller strength gains than heavy, low-repetition training

Precautions: teach correct technique first; avoid breath-holding; adapt stations for joint problems; monitor heart rate or RPE in patients.

6Inactivity: sedentary lifestyle and bed rest

Hazards of a sedentary lifestyle (W18, 7 marks); deconditioning effects of bed rest (S16, 7 marks; W16, 3 marks).

Hazards of a sedentary lifestyle (7 marks in W18)

Two different things make up a sedentary lifestyle, and the exam answer should define both.

  • Sedentary behaviour: any waking behaviour with an energy use of 1.5 METs or less while sitting, reclining or lying, such as desk work, driving and watching television (Tremblay et al., 2017).
  • Physical inactivity: not doing enough physical activity to meet current recommendations (Tremblay et al., 2017).
  • They are separate risks. A person can meet the activity guidelines and still sit for most of the day, and long sitting still harms metabolic health (Panahi and Tremblay, 2018).

Size of the problem

  • World: 31% of adults (1.8 billion) were insufficiently active in 2022, up from 23% in 2000. The share is projected to reach about 35% by 2030 (Strain et al., 2024; WHO).
  • India: about 49% of adults were insufficiently active in 2022: 57% of women and 42% of men (WHO Global Health Observatory).
  • ICMR-INDIAB study: in four Indian regions, 54.4% of adults were inactive and only 13.7% highly active. Inactivity was higher in towns (65.0%) than in villages (50.0%), and about 92% did no recreational activity at all (Anjana et al., 2014).
  • Deaths: people who are insufficiently active have a 20–30% higher risk of death than those who are sufficiently active (WHO). Worldwide, inactivity causes about 6% of coronary heart disease, 7% of type 2 diabetes, 10% each of breast and colon cancer, and 9% of premature deaths, more than 5.3 million deaths in 2008 (Lee et al., 2012).

  • All-cause death: high sitting time raised the hazard ratio to 1.24, and the effect was larger in people who did little exercise (Biswas et al., 2015).
  • Dose: risk rises more steeply above about 6–8 h a day of sitting and 3–4 h a day of television (Patterson et al., 2018).
  • Can exercise cancel it? In over 1 million adults, the least active who sat more than 8 h a day had the highest risk (hazard ratio 1.59). About 60–75 min a day of moderate activity seemed to remove the extra risk of long sitting. Television for 3 h or more a day stayed harmful except in the most active quarter (Ekelund et al., 2016).

Physiotherapy prescription: sit less, move more

  • Meet the guideline: 150–300 min a week of moderate or 75–150 min of vigorous activity, plus muscle strengthening on 2 or more days (WHO, 2020).
  • Limit sitting: WHO (2020) advises adults to limit sedentary time and replace it with activity of any intensity, including light activity. It sets no numerical limit. People who sit a lot should aim above the recommended activity levels.
  • Break up sitting often: in overweight adults, a 2-minute walk every 20 minutes over 5 hours lowered the rise in blood glucose after a drink by 24–30% and insulin by 23% (Dunstan et al., 2012).
  • Desk workers: build up to at least 2 h a day of standing and light walking in working hours, then 4 h a day. Alternate sitting and standing work, with a sit-stand desk if possible, and avoid long static standing too (Buckley et al., 2015). See desk workers.
  • Practical habits: stand for phone calls, walk to a colleague instead of phoning, take the stairs, hold walking meetings, walk or cycle part of the commute, and do household chores or stretches during television breaks.
  • Behaviour change: set small goals, use a step counter or phone reminder, and enlist the family or workplace; workplace and school activity programmes and community awareness.

7Exercise in diabetes and hypertension

Precautions when prescribing fitness exercise for a diabetic (S18, 3 marks); effects of exercise in a hypertensive person (W25, 7 marks).

Diabetes: precautions

  • Dose: at least 150 minutes a week of moderate to vigorous aerobic activity over at least 3 days, with no more than 2 days in a row without activity; resistance training 2–3 non-consecutive days a week (ADA, 2016).
  • Low sugar (hypoglycaemia): check blood glucose before and after exercise if on insulin or sulphonylureas; carry fast sugar; take carbohydrate or reduce insulin as advised; do not inject insulin into the exercising limb; lows can come 6–15 hours later and the risk lasts up to 48 hours.
  • High sugar: do not exercise with high blood glucose and ketones.
  • Feet: inspect daily, good footwear and socks; non-weight-bearing exercise (cycling, swimming) if there is an ulcer.
  • Eyes: with severe or unstable retinopathy avoid vigorous and heavy resistance exercise, jumping, jarring, head-down positions and breath-holding.
  • Autonomic neuropathy: use RPE rather than heart rate; avoid quick position changes; drink enough; avoid heat.
  • Exercise at a regular time, often 1–2 hours after a meal; exercise with a partner who knows the signs of hypoglycaemia.

Hypertension: effects of exercise (7 marks in W25)

Hypertension is common in India: 35.5% of adults in the ICMR-INDIAB survey (Anjana et al., 2023). In NFHS-5 (2019–21), 21.3% of women and 24.0% of men aged 15 and over had a raised BP or were on treatment. Exercise is a first-line lifestyle treatment for all of them.

  • During exercise: in dynamic aerobic work, systolic BP rises with the workload while diastolic BP stays about the same or falls a little, because the working muscles' vessels dilate. Heavy isometric holds and breath-holding (Valsalva) raise both sharply.
  • After one session: BP stays lower for up to 22 hours (post-exercise hypotension) (Pescatello et al., 2004).
  • After training: resting BP falls most in people with hypertension, by about 5–8 mmHg, against 2–4 mmHg with prehypertension and 1–2 mmHg with normal BP (US Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee, 2018). In hypertensive groups, endurance training lowered BP by 8.3/5.2 mmHg (Cornelissen and Smart, 2013).
  • Other gains: fewer cardiovascular events, better fitness and mood, less need for drugs.

How exercise lowers BP

  • Nerves and hormones: after endurance training, plasma noradrenaline fell by 29% and plasma renin activity by 20% (Cornelissen and Fagard, 2005).
  • Vessels: systemic vascular resistance fell by 7.1% (Cornelissen and Fagard, 2005). Endothelial function (flow-mediated dilatation) improves with aerobic, resistance and combined training (Ashor et al., 2015). Aerobic training lowers arterial stiffness; resistance training alone does not (Ashor et al., 2014).
  • Metabolism: weight and waist fall and insulin sensitivity improves (Cornelissen and Fagard, 2005).

Classic answer vs current practice: resistance and isometric exercise in hypertension

Classic

Aerobic exercise only; avoid weights and isometric exercise because they raise BP.

Current

Dynamic resistance training lowers resting BP about as much as aerobic training, and isometric training lowered it most in meta-analyses. The danger is breath-holding and maximal straining, not resistance itself.

Write in the exam

"Aerobic exercise on most days plus resistance training 2–3 days a week at moderate load, breathing out on effort; no breath-holding or maximal lifts."

Precautions

  • Do not start exercise if resting BP is very high (above 200/110 mmHg) until it is controlled. During an exercise test, stop if BP rises above 250/115 mmHg (American Heart Association, 2013).
  • Avoid breath-holding and heavy isometric straining at maximal effort.
  • Long warm-up and cool-down: vasodilators and alpha-blockers cause dizziness after exercise.
  • Use RPE with beta-blockers, which blunt the heart rate; watch for dehydration and low potassium with diuretics. See hypertension in CVR.

3 mFITT principle of aerobic training. (W20)

Frequency: 5 or more days moderate or 3 or more vigorous. Intensity: 40–59% HRR (RPE 12–13) moderate, 60–89% vigorous. Time: 30–60 minutes (150 a week) moderate or 20–60 (75 a week) vigorous. Type: rhythmic large-muscle activity such as walking, cycling, swimming. Add volume and progression (FITT-VP).

3 mPrecautions while prescribing fitness exercise for a diabetic patient. (S18)

Check glucose before and after if on insulin or sulphonylureas; carry fast sugar; no exercise with high glucose and ketones; avoid injecting the exercising limb; watch for delayed hypoglycaemia; foot inspection and footwear, non-weight-bearing with ulcers; avoid jarring, head-down and breath-holding with severe retinopathy; RPE with autonomic neuropathy; hydration.

7 mEnergy systems. (W16)

ATP is the only direct energy for contraction and is stored for seconds. ATP–PC: creatine phosphate rebuilds ATP without oxygen; fastest, smallest store; all-out efforts of a few seconds (sprint, jump). Fast glycolysis: glucose to lactate, net 2 ATP; leads from about 30 s to 2 min (400 m). Oxidative: carbohydrate and fat burnt in mitochondria, about 30–32 ATP per glucose; slowest, largest store; at rest and beyond 2–3 min (walking, marathon). All overlap; intensity and duration decide the lead system (NSCA table). Add lactate threshold, oxygen deficit and EPOC, then training: work-to-rest 1:12 to 1:20 for phosphagen, 1:3 to 1:5 for glycolysis, continuous or 1:1 to 1:3 for aerobic. Draw the time line.

7 mDiscuss the effects of an exercise intervention on a hypertensive individual. (W25)

During exercise systolic BP rises, diastolic stays level; post-exercise hypotension for up to 22 hours; after training resting BP falls 5–8 mmHg in hypertension; all types work, isometric most (Edwards 2023); mechanisms (noradrenaline and renin down, lower vascular resistance, better endothelium, less stiffness, weight and insulin sensitivity); prescription (aerobic 5–7 days at 40–59% heart rate reserve for 30 min or more, resistance 2–3 days at 60–70% 1RM); precautions (resting BP above 200/110, Valsalva, drugs: beta-blockers, vasodilators, diuretics; long cool-down).
