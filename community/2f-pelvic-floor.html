2(f)Urogenital dysfunction: the pelvic floor, incontinence, prolapse and gynaecological conditions

Incontinence and prolapse were asked in 16 of 20 papers. One idea runs through all of it: the pelvic floor is a muscle sling that holds up the bladder, uterus and rectum and closes their openings, so teaching a woman to find, strengthen and time that muscle is the first treatment for leaking and for a mild prolapse.

1The pelvic floor

Muscles of the pelvic floor were a 3-mark question in S16, and every answer on this page starts here.

LayerMuscles
Pelvic diaphragm (deep)Levator ani: pubococcygeus (with puborectalis, which loops behind the rectum), iliococcygeus; and coccygeus (ischiococcygeus)
Deep perineal pouchDeep transverse perineal, external urethral sphincter (with compressor urethrae and urethrovaginal sphincter)
Superficial perineal pouchBulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus, superficial transverse perineal
OthersExternal anal sphincter; the perineal body is the central tendon where they meet
  • Nerve supply: pudendal nerve (S2–S4) and direct branches of S3–S4 to levator ani.
  • Fibres: mostly slow-twitch for constant support, with fast-twitch fibres that close the openings quickly when pressure rises (cough, sneeze, lift).
  • Functions: support the pelvic organs; keep urine, wind and stool in (continence); relax to let them out; sexual function; work with the diaphragm, transversus abdominis and multifidus to control abdominal pressure and steady the spine and pelvis; guide the baby's head during birth.

2How the bladder fills and empties

Physiology of micturition was the first 5 marks of the W19 long answer.

NerveSpinal levelAction
Sympathetic (hypogastric nerve)T10–L2Relaxes the detrusor and closes the bladder neck: storage
Parasympathetic (pelvic nerve)S2–S4Contracts the detrusor: emptying
Somatic (pudendal nerve)S2–S4Contracts the external sphincter and pelvic floor: voluntary holding
  1. Filling: the bladder stretches at low pressure; sympathetic tone and the sphincters keep it closed; stretch signals rise to the brain; the first desire to void is felt at about half capacity (normal capacity about 400–600 ml).
  2. Holding: the frontal cortex inhibits the pontine micturition centre until it is convenient; the pelvic floor can be contracted to hold on.
  3. Voiding: the pontine centre switches over: the sphincters and pelvic floor relax, the detrusor contracts, and the bladder empties completely.

The same circuit explains the spinal cord injury bladder (see bladder and bowel in Neuro).

3Urinary incontinence: types

Types of stress incontinence and their PT (S19, 7 marks).

TypeDefinition (ICS)Typical cause
Stress urinary incontinence (SUI)Involuntary leakage on effort or exertion, or on sneezing or coughingWeak pelvic floor and urethral support after childbirth, menopause, chronic cough, obesity, heavy lifting
Urgency urinary incontinence (UUI)Leakage accompanied by or immediately preceded by urgencyOveractive detrusor (overactive bladder), irritants (caffeine), infection, neurological disease
MixedLeakage with urgency and also with exertion, sneezing or coughingBoth; treat the more troublesome first
Overflow and othersDribbling from an over-full bladder that does not empty; continuous leakage; functional incontinenceOutlet obstruction or weak detrusor; fistula; poor mobility or cognition (cannot reach the toilet in time)

Types of stress incontinence

  • By severity (Stamey grades): grade 1, leakage with coughing, sneezing or lifting; grade 2, with smaller rises in pressure such as walking, standing up or climbing stairs; grade 3, leakage regardless of activity or position.
  • By mechanism: urethral hypermobility (the bladder neck and urethra drop on effort because the support is weak) or intrinsic sphincter deficiency (the urethral closure itself is weak, as after surgery or with severe atrophy).
  • PT: pelvic floor muscle training is first-line for all; add "the knack" for grade 1, biofeedback or electrical stimulation for a very weak or unaware muscle, and lifestyle change. Grade 3 or sphincter deficiency needs the urogynaecologist (surgery, bulking agents), with PT before and after.

4Assessment of incontinence

Principles of incontinence assessment (W19), tests for incontinence (S25, 3 marks), the PERFECT scheme (W20, 3 marks).

History

  • When it leaks (cough, lift, on the way to the toilet), how much, how often, pads used; frequency by day and night; urgency; difficulty emptying; bowel habit and constipation.
  • Obstetric history (number of births, big baby, long labour, forceps, tears), menopause, surgery, chronic cough, weight, drugs (diuretics), diabetes or neurological disease, fluid and caffeine intake, effect on life and sexual function.
  • Red flags for referral: blood in urine, recurrent infection, pain, a palpable bladder, neurological signs, a large prolapse.

Tests

  • Bladder diary (frequency–volume chart) for at least 3 days: fluid in, voids and volumes, leaks and their triggers.
  • Questionnaires: ICIQ-UI short form for severity and bother.
  • Pad test: weigh a pad worn during set activities; in the 1-hour test a gain of 1 g or more is positive.
  • Cough stress test: leakage seen at the moment of a cough with a comfortably full bladder.
  • Q-tip test: a cotton swab in the urethra swings more than 30° on straining in urethral hypermobility.
  • Digital vaginal examination of the pelvic floor (with consent, by a trained physiotherapist): PERFECT and the Modified Oxford scale; perineometer or EMG biofeedback for a number.
  • Medical tests: urine test for infection, post-void residual by ultrasound, urodynamics before surgery.

The PERFECT scheme (Laycock and Jerwood, 2001)

LetterWhat is measured
P: PowerStrength of a maximal contraction on the Modified Oxford scale: 0 none, 1 flicker, 2 weak, 3 moderate with lift, 4 good against resistance, 5 strong against strong resistance
E: EnduranceSeconds a maximal contraction is held before it fades (up to 10)
R: RepetitionsNumber of those holds repeated with a short rest between (up to 10)
F: FastNumber of quick 1-second contractions in a row (up to 10)
ECT: Every Contraction TimedThe results are timed and recorded so they become her own exercise programme
Remember

P-E-R-F-ECT: Power, Endurance, Repetitions, Fast, Every Contraction Timed

A woman graded P3 E5 R4 F6 starts with 5-second holds repeated 4 times, then 6 quick contractions, and builds up from there.

5Physiotherapy for incontinence

Kegel's exercises (S24), deferment techniques (W19), interferential therapy (W18) and continence-promoting advice (S18), each 3 marks.

Pelvic floor muscle training (Kegel's exercises)

  • Described by the gynaecologist Arnold Kegel in 1948. Supervised training for at least 3 months is the first-line treatment for stress and mixed incontinence (NICE). Women with stress incontinence who trained were about eight times more likely to report cure than untreated women (Dumoulin et al., 2018).
  • Teach: "squeeze and lift as if stopping wind and the flow of urine", without holding the breath, squeezing the buttocks or pulling the tummy in hard. Check the contraction by vaginal palpation where possible. Stopping the urine stream is only for finding the muscle once, not an exercise.
  • Dose: slow holds of up to 10 seconds, repeated up to 10 times, and up to 10 quick 1-second contractions; at least 8 contractions 3 times a day (NICE), set from her PERFECT result.
  • Progress from lying to sitting to standing, then during functional tasks (lifting, climbing stairs, jumping).
  • The knack: a strong contraction just before and during a cough, sneeze or lift.

Bladder training and urge deferment (urgency and mixed)

  • Bladder training for at least 6 weeks (NICE): void by the clock, then lengthen the gap by about 15 minutes at a time towards 3–4 hours.
  • Deferment when the urge comes: stop and stand still or sit down (pressure on the perineum helps); do several quick strong pelvic floor contractions; breathe slowly and relax; distract the mind (count backwards); curl the toes or cross the legs; when the urge fades, walk calmly to the toilet.
  • Cut down caffeine, fizzy drinks and alcohol; no "just in case" visits.

Adjuncts

MethodUsePoint to write
BiofeedbackVaginal pressure probe (perineometer) or EMG shows the contraction on a screenHelps women who cannot feel the muscle; not needed routinely (NICE)
Electrical stimulationVaginal electrode produces a pelvic floor contraction; higher frequencies (about 50 Hz) for weak muscles in SUI, low (about 10 Hz) to calm an overactive bladderUse when she cannot contract the muscle at all, always with active training
Interferential therapyMedium-frequency currents meet in the pelvis and produce a low-frequency effect deep in the pelvic floor with little skin discomfort; one classic set-up uses one electrode under the ischial tuberosities and one over the anterior perineum (Laycock and Green, 1988)An older method with weak evidence; only alongside training; avoid in pregnancy, infection, bleeding, cancer in the area or with a pacemaker
Vaginal conesWeighted cones held in the vagina while walkingThe muscle must contract to keep the cone in

Continence-promoting advice

  • Drink normally (about 1.5–2 litres a day unless advised otherwise); do not cut fluids, which concentrates urine and irritates the bladder.
  • Reduce caffeine, fizzy drinks and alcohol.
  • Avoid constipation and straining: fibre, fluids, a footstool on the toilet.
  • Lose weight if overweight (NICE suggests weight loss when BMI is over 30); stop smoking; treat chronic cough.
  • Sit fully on the toilet, relax and empty completely; do not hover.
  • Do the knack before lifting, coughing or sneezing; lift with help and with the load close.
  • Keep up pelvic floor exercises for life, especially in pregnancy, after birth and around menopause.

6Uterine and vaginal prolapse

Asked as 7 marks in S20, S21, W22 and W25, 3 marks in W21, and in the W16 long case (58-year-old with grade I prolapse).

  • Definition: descent of one or more of the front wall of the vagina, the back wall, the uterus (cervix) or the vault after hysterectomy.
CompartmentType
AnteriorCystocele (bladder), urethrocele (urethra), cystourethrocele
Middle (apical)Uterine prolapse (uterovaginal); vault prolapse after hysterectomy
PosteriorRectocele (rectum), enterocele (small bowel in the pouch of Douglas)

Degrees of uterine prolapse

Clinical degreeWhat is seenPOP-Q stage
First degreeThe cervix comes down but stays inside the vaginaStage I: lowest point more than 1 cm above the hymen
Second degreeThe cervix comes out through the vaginal opening on strainingStage II: within 1 cm above or below the hymen
Third degree (procidentia)The whole uterus lies outside the vaginal openingStage III: more than 1 cm below the hymen; stage IV: complete eversion
  • Causes: childbirth (many births, long labour, big baby, instruments, pushing before full dilatation), loss of oestrogen after menopause, chronic rise in abdominal pressure (cough, constipation, heavy lifting, obesity), ageing and inherited tissue weakness.
  • Symptoms: something coming down, dragging or heaviness worse by evening, low backache, urinary leakage or incomplete emptying, difficulty emptying the bowel, discomfort in intercourse.

Physiotherapy for prolapse

  • Pelvic floor muscle training, individually taught and supervised, for at least 16 weeks is the first option for a symptomatic prolapse (NICE): it reduced prolapse symptoms in the POPPY trial (Hagen et al., 2014).
  • Effects of exercise (W25): training improved the prolapse by one POP-Q stage in 19% of women against 8% of controls, raised the bladder and rectum and reduced symptoms (Brækken et al., 2010). It also helps the leaking that often comes with prolapse, and support of the spine and pelvis. Activities that strain the pelvic floor (heavy lifting, straining at stool, repeated breath-held sit-ups, high-impact jumping) can worsen the dragging feeling, so they are modified, not every activity stopped.
  • Lifestyle: weight loss, treat constipation and cough, safe lifting with the knack.
  • Pessary (fitted by a doctor) for women who are unfit for or do not want surgery; PT continues with it.
  • Surgery (vaginal hysterectomy with repair, colporrhaphy) for large prolapse, with PT before and after (section 8).

7Bowel and anorectal problems in pregnancy and after birth

S18 long answer: bowel and anorectal dysfunction in the antenatal and postnatal periods, with reasons, assessment and management.

ProblemReason
Constipation (antenatal)Progesterone slows the bowel; iron tablets; the growing uterus presses on the bowel; less activity
HaemorrhoidsRaised pressure in pelvic veins plus straining at stool; worsened by pushing in labour
Constipation and painful defecation (postnatal)Fear of opening the bowels with episiotomy stitches or a tear, pain relief drugs, less food and fluid in labour
Anal (faecal or flatus) incontinence and urgencyTear of the anal sphincter (third or fourth degree), stretch injury of the pudendal nerve in a long second stage or forceps delivery, weak pelvic floor
  • Assessment: bowel habit and diary, stool type (Bristol Stool Form Scale), straining, urgency, leakage of wind or stool, perineal pain, obstetric details (tear, episiotomy, instruments); inspection of the perineum; pelvic floor assessment; refer for anal sphincter assessment after a tear.
  • Defecation position: feet on a footstool so the knees are above the hips, lean forward with straight back, relax the belly out, breathe out and do not strain; support the stitches with a clean pad held on the perineum.
  • Diet: fibre, fluids, regular routine; stool softeners as prescribed after a sphincter tear.
  • Pelvic floor and anal sphincter exercises from the first days after birth (gently), progressed over weeks; physiotherapy after repair of a sphincter tear can help (RCOG); biofeedback and electrical stimulation for persistent weakness.
  • Pain relief for the perineum: ice packs wrapped in cloth, positioning (side-lying, cushion), early walking.
  • Advice on lifting the baby, avoiding straining, and when to seek help. See labour and postnatal care and pregnancy.

8Gynaecological surgery, cancer and PCOD

Indications for hysterectomy (S23, 3 marks); a 23-year-old with PCOD, BMI 34 and back pain (S26 long case); malignancy is named in the syllabus.

Hysterectomy

  • Indications: fibroids with heavy bleeding or pressure (the commonest), abnormal uterine bleeding not controlled by drugs, adenomyosis and endometriosis, uterovaginal prolapse, cancer of the uterus, cervix or ovary, chronic pelvic pain from pelvic inflammatory disease, and life-threatening bleeding after childbirth.
  • Types: total, subtotal (cervix left), radical (Wertheim's, with lymph nodes) for cancer; abdominal, vaginal or laparoscopic.
  • PT after surgery: breathing exercises and supported coughing, leg exercises against thrombosis, early walking, gentle pelvic floor and deep abdominal exercises, getting out of bed by rolling to the side, no heavy lifting for about 6 weeks, graded return to activity.

Gynaecological cancer

  • Breast cancer is now the commonest cancer in Indian women and cervical cancer is second; see mastectomy and oncological surgery for the breast.
  • PT problems: wound and chest care after surgery; leg lymphoedema after pelvic node removal (compression, exercise, skin care, manual lymph drainage); cancer-related fatigue (graded aerobic and strength exercise); bladder, bowel and sexual problems and vaginal narrowing after radiotherapy (pelvic floor work, dilators as advised); bone loss if the ovaries are removed (see menopause and osteoporosis).

Polycystic ovary disease (PCOD or PCOS)

  • Diagnosed by two of three: irregular or absent ovulation, signs or tests of raised androgens (acne, hair growth), polycystic ovaries on ultrasound (international guideline, 2023).
  • Linked with weight gain, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes risk, infertility, anxiety and depression.
  • PT role: lifestyle change is the first treatment; aerobic activity of 150–300 minutes a week at moderate intensity or 75–150 minutes vigorous, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 non-consecutive days, and less sitting; even modest weight loss improves periods and ovulation; core and back exercises and posture for back pain; stress management; diet with a dietitian. See obesity.

S26 ICF in brief: health condition, PCOD with obesity (BMI 34) and mechanical low back pain; body functions, back pain, excess weight, low fitness, weak core, stress; activities, prolonged sitting and standing, exercise; participation, studies or work, social life, plans for pregnancy; environment, food habits at home and outside; personal, young, motivated by fertility. Goals: short term, pain relief and a daily walking habit; long term, 5–10% weight loss over 6 months, regular cycles, pain-free activity.

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mDeferment techniques for urge incontinence. (W19)

When the urge comes: stop and stand still or sit down; do several quick strong pelvic floor contractions; breathe slowly and relax; distract the mind (count backwards); press on the perineum or curl the toes; once the urge passes, walk calmly to the toilet. Used within bladder training, which lengthens the gap between voids by about 15 minutes at a time.

3 mPERFECT method of pelvic floor muscle grading. (W20)

Laycock and Jerwood (2001), by digital vaginal examination: Power on the Modified Oxford scale 0–5; Endurance, seconds a maximal contraction is held (up to 10); Repetitions of that hold (up to 10); Fast, number of 1-second contractions (up to 10); Every Contraction Timed, so the result becomes her exercise programme.

7 mTypes and degrees of urogenital prolapse; explain briefly its management. (W22)

Types by compartment: anterior (cystocele, urethrocele), middle (uterine, vault), posterior (rectocele, enterocele). Degrees: first, cervix descends within the vagina; second, cervix comes out through the introitus on straining; third (procidentia), the whole uterus outside; POP-Q stages 0–IV. Management: supervised PFMT for at least 16 weeks for stage I–II (POPPY trial; one-stage improvement in 19% vs 8%, Brækken 2010), the knack, lifestyle (weight, constipation, cough, safe lifting), pessary by the gynaecologist, surgery for large prolapse with pre- and post-operative PT.

LAQExplain the physiology of micturition. Describe the principles of incontinence assessment. Write the objective methods for incontinence management. (5 + 5 + 5, W19)

Physiology: sympathetic T10–L2 relaxes the detrusor and closes the bladder neck (storage); parasympathetic S2–S4 contracts the detrusor (voiding); pudendal S2–S4 controls the external sphincter; the pontine micturition centre coordinates, the cortex decides when. Assessment: history (type, triggers, obstetric, bowel, drugs), bladder diary, questionnaire, pad test, cough stress test, Q-tip test, PERFECT, urine test and residual volume, urodynamics if surgery is planned. Objective methods: PFMT with measured dose, biofeedback (perineometer, EMG), electrical stimulation, vaginal cones, bladder training with a diary, and re-measurement with the diary, pad test and PERFECT.
