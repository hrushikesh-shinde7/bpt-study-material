7The project synopsis: choosing a topic, writing each section, and presenting and defending it in the practical exam

The synopsis carries 25 of the 80 practical marks (30 in the preliminary exam). It is the plan of the study you will do during internship, on any physiotherapy topic, under a teacher's guidance, and you present and defend it before the examiners. A clear question, a design that fits it, and honest answers about its limits score more than a complicated plan you cannot explain.

My revision

1What the synopsis is and how it is marked

  • Synopsis: a short, complete plan of a research study: what you will ask, why, and exactly how you will answer it. You will carry out the study in internship.
  • Topic: any area of physiotherapy (musculoskeletal, neuro, cardiorespiratory or community).
  • Marks: 25 in the university practical (with the long case 50 and journal 5); 30 in the preliminary exam. The syllabus expects introduction, aims and objectives, methods and methodology, and review of literature.
  • What examiners look for: a clear and answerable question, a design that fits it, correct definitions of variables, feasible sample and setting, valid outcome measures, a sensible analysis plan, ethics, and your ability to explain every choice.

2Choosing the research question

FINER criteria for a good question
LetterMeaning
F: FeasibleEnough participants, time (internship months), skills, equipment and money
I: InterestingTo you, your guide and clinicians
N: NovelAdds something: a new population, setting (Indian, rural), outcome or comparison
E: EthicalSafe, with consent; approvable by the ethics committee
R: RelevantUseful for practice, policy or future research
  • PICO shapes an intervention question: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. Example: in desk workers with neck pain (P), does deep neck flexor training (I) compared with stretching (C) reduce Neck Disability Index scores at 4 weeks (O)?
  • Good internship-sized topics: prevalence surveys (for example low back pain in bus conductors, incontinence in postnatal women, falls risk in a senior citizens' club), correlation studies (for example BMI and knee pain), and small comparative studies of two physiotherapy techniques.
  • Avoid: very rare conditions, long follow-up, expensive equipment, vague titles ("a study on back pain").

3The sections of a synopsis

SectionWhat to write
TitleSpecific: design, intervention or exposure, population, outcome. "Effect of X versus Y on Z in [population]: a randomised controlled trial"
IntroductionThe problem and how common it is (with numbers), its impact, current treatments, the gap in knowledge
Need for the studyWhy this study, here and now; who will benefit
Review of literature10–20 recent, relevant studies summarised (author, year, design, sample, result), grouped by theme, ending with the gap your study fills
Research question and hypothesesNull hypothesis (no difference or no association) and alternative hypothesis
Aim and objectivesOne broad aim; specific, measurable objectives ("to find the effect of X on pain using VAS")
MethodologySection 4
EthicsInstitutional ethics committee approval, written informed consent in the local language, confidentiality, right to withdraw, risks and how they are handled; registration with the Clinical Trials Registry–India for a trial
Timeline and budgetGantt chart of months (approval, data collection, analysis, writing); costs
ReferencesOne style throughout (usually Vancouver, numbered)
AnnexuresConsent form, information sheet, assessment proforma, questionnaires, exercise protocol

4Methodology

Study designs

DesignUseExample
Cross-sectional surveyPrevalence and associations at one timePrevalence of neck pain in IT workers
CorrelationalRelationship between two variablesBMI and knee pain in homemakers
Case–controlCompare past exposures of people with and without a conditionOccupational factors in women with and without prolapse
CohortFollow exposed and unexposed people forwardFalls over 6 months in seniors with and without fear of falling
Quasi-experimental (pre–post)Effect of an intervention without randomisationPelvic floor training in a group of postnatal women
Randomised controlled trialCompare interventions with random allocation; strongest for cause and effectTwo exercise programmes for desk workers

Population and sample

  • Setting and population: where and who (for example women 3–6 months postpartum attending a PHC).
  • Inclusion and exclusion criteria: clear, measurable, with reasons.
  • Sampling: probability (simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster) or non-probability (convenience, purposive, snowball); say which and why; random allocation method (sealed envelopes, computer list) for trials.
  • Sample size: calculated from a previous study's result (prevalence, or mean difference and SD) with significance 5% and power 80%, using a formula or software, plus 10–20% for drop-outs; state the source.

Variables and outcome measures

  • Independent variable (intervention or exposure) and dependent variables (outcomes); control likely confounders.
  • Outcome measures that are valid and reliable, with references: VAS or NPRS (pain), goniometer (range), Neck Disability Index, Oswestry, Berg, TUG, 6MWT, ICIQ, PERFECT, questionnaires validated in the local language.
  • Procedure: step by step, so another person could repeat it: assessment, intervention dose (frequency, intensity, time, type, duration of the programme), when outcomes are measured, who measures (blinding).

Statistical analysis plan

QuestionTest (normal data)Test (non-normal or ordinal)
Before vs after in one groupPaired t-testWilcoxon signed-rank
Two independent groupsUnpaired t-testMann–Whitney U
Three or more groupsANOVAKruskal–Wallis
Two categorical variablesChi-square testFisher's exact test (small numbers)

Describe data with mean and SD (or median and range) and percentages; significance at p < 0.05; name the software.

5Presenting and defending the synopsis

The presentation (about 8–10 minutes)

  1. Title, your name, guide.
  2. The problem in two or three lines with one number.
  3. Gap from the literature (three or four key studies).
  4. Aim, objectives, hypotheses.
  5. Design, setting, sample and sample size, criteria.
  6. Outcome measures and procedure (a flowchart of the study).
  7. Analysis, ethics, timeline.
  8. Expected outcome and how it will help practice.

Questions examiners often ask

  • Why this topic? What is new about it?
  • Why this design and not another? What are its limitations?
  • How did you calculate the sample size?
  • Define your variables. Which is dependent and which independent?
  • Why this outcome measure? Is it valid and reliable? How exactly will you measure it?
  • How will you reduce bias (randomisation, blinding, standard instructions)?
  • What is your null hypothesis? Which test will you use, and why?
  • How will you take consent? What if a participant is harmed or drops out?
  • Can you finish it in internship? Where will you get the participants?

Tips

  • Know every reference you cite (author, year, finding).
  • Admit limits honestly and say how you will handle them.
  • Carry the consent form, proforma and questionnaire.
  • Keep slides simple: one idea per slide, large text, a flowchart of the method.
  • Your own work: write it yourself with your guide; copying another student's synopsis is easy for examiners to detect and fails the viva.

6Exam corner

Keywords to underline

FINER PICO null and alternative hypothesis aim and objectives inclusion and exclusion criteria sample size with power 80% probability and non-probability sampling valid and reliable outcome measures paired and unpaired t-test informed consent ethics committee CTRI

Draw these

  • A study flowchart: assessed for eligibility, excluded, randomised, two groups, outcomes, analysis
  • A Gantt chart of your internship months

Viva questions

  1. What does FINER stand for?
  2. Difference between an aim and an objective?
  3. Difference between reliability and validity?
  4. When would you use a Mann–Whitney U test?
  5. What is a confounding variable? Give one from your study.
  6. What is informed consent, and what must the information sheet contain?

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mWhat makes a good research question?

FINER: feasible, interesting, novel, ethical, relevant; framed with PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome); specific and measurable within the time available.

7 mList the sections of a research synopsis with one line on each.

Title; introduction; need for the study; review of literature; research question and hypotheses; aim and objectives; methodology (design, setting, population, criteria, sampling, sample size, variables, outcome measures, procedure, statistics); ethics; timeline and budget; references; annexures (consent, proforma, questionnaires).

MCQTo compare pain scores before and after treatment in the same group (normal data), you would use:A. Unpaired t-testB. Paired t-testC. Chi-square testD. ANOVA

B.

MCQThe design that best shows cause and effect between a treatment and an outcome is:A. Cross-sectional surveyB. Case reportC. Randomised controlled trialD. Correlational study

C.

MCQChoosing every 5th patient from a register is:A. Convenience samplingB. Systematic samplingC. Snowball samplingD. Purposive sampling

B.
