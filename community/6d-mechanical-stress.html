6(b iv)Mechanical and psychological stress at work: desk workers, drivers, standing jobs, labourers and job stress

This is the most asked part of the Community paper: 17 of 20 papers, with 16 long cases about an office worker, driver, teacher, goldsmith, watchman, labourer or tailor in pain. Every answer follows the same path: analyse the job, find the posture, load or stress that causes the problem, then treat the person and change the work.

My revision

1How work causes pain

  • Work-related musculoskeletal disorders build up from many small strains (cumulative microtrauma) rather than one injury; see cumulative trauma disorders.
  • Physical risk factors: awkward or static posture held for long; repetition; force (lifting, gripping); vibration; contact pressure; cold; too little rest.
  • Personal factors: low fitness, obesity, previous injury, smoking, age.
  • Psychosocial factors: high demands with little control, tight deadlines, monotony, poor support, job insecurity; they raise muscle tension and the chance that pain persists.
  • The physiotherapist's method: job and task analysis (see ability management), find the risk factors, treat the impairments, change the workstation or the way of working, and teach self-management and fitness.

2Sedentary desk work and computer users

Executives, clerks, bank staff, computer operators and engineers: long cases in W16, S21, W21, W25 and S26.

  • Problems: neck and shoulder pain with a forward head and rounded shoulders; thoracic stiffness; low back pain from slumped sitting; wrist and hand symptoms (carpal tunnel syndrome) from keyboard and mouse use; eye strain and headache; weight gain and low fitness.

Ergonomic advice

  • Screen: directly in front, about an arm's length away (50–100 cm), top line at or slightly below eye level; laptops raised on a stand with a separate keyboard and mouse.
  • Chair: height so the feet are flat and thighs level; backrest with lumbar support; armrests that let the shoulders relax.
  • Keyboard and mouse: close, at elbow height, wrists straight; mouse beside the keyboard.
  • Document holder beside the screen; headset for long calls instead of holding the phone with the neck.
  • Breaks: change position often; a short movement break every 30–60 minutes; rest the eyes regularly by looking into the distance.

Physiotherapy

  • Neck and shoulder strengthening (deep neck flexors, scapular retractors and lower trapezius) and general fitness: in office workers with neck pain, strengthening and general fitness training reduced pain, with moderate-quality evidence, and ergonomic changes alone had weaker support (Chen et al., 2018).
  • Thoracic mobility and pectoral stretching; posture awareness; workstation exercises during the day (see workstation exercises).
  • Pain relief in the acute phase (heat, TENS, manual therapy) only as a bridge to exercise.
  • Regular physical activity, weight control and a plan for flare-ups.

4Constant standing

Musculoskeletal hazards of prolonged standing (S21, 7 marks); watchman (S23) and traffic police (S24) long cases; surgeons (S26, 7 marks).

  • Effects reported consistently: low back pain, leg pain and swelling, fatigue and muscle pain, and pooling of blood in the legs (varicose veins); foot pain and plantar heel pain (Waters and Dick, 2015).
  • Why: static loading of the back and legs, little calf-muscle pumping, hard floors, poor footwear.
  • Prevention: change position often and walk when possible (movement was the most effective measure); sit–stand options or a leaning stool; anti-fatigue mats; supportive cushioned footwear and insoles; a footrest to alternate feet; compression stockings for those with swelling; calf-raise and ankle-pump exercises during the shift; rest breaks.

Surgeons: ergonomic evaluation and modifications (7 marks in S26)

Surgeons hold one posture for hours, with the neck bent over the field and the arms held still. A meta-analysis of 21 studies (5,828 surgeons and interventionalists) found a pooled prevalence of pain of 35–60%, depending on the questionnaire. Of those with a work-related disorder, 12% needed leave, a change in practice or early retirement (Epstein et al., 2018).

Ergonomic evaluation uses the same steps as any workplace evaluation (see ability management):

  1. Symptom survey: the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire asks about pain in each body region over the past 12 months and 7 days.
  2. Observe and video whole operations: the posture of the neck, trunk and arms, how long each posture is held, and when breaks happen.
  3. Measure the set-up: table height against the surgeon's elbow height, monitor position and height, loupes or microscope, foot pedals, floor and footwear.
  4. Score the postures: RULA suits static neck and arm work; REBA scores the whole body (see risk assessment tools).
  5. Recommend changes, equipment and layout first, then re-evaluate: a lower score and fewer symptoms show they worked.

Modifications for every surgeon

  • Micro-breaks: guided stretching breaks of 1.5–2 min every 20–40 min reduced pain in the neck, back, shoulders, wrists, knees and ankles. They did not lengthen the operation, and 87% of surgeons planned to continue them (Park et al., 2017). The American College of Surgeons advises range-of-motion micro-breaks every 30–60 min.
  • Standing: a theatre-grade anti-fatigue mat; weight on both legs, not one; supportive shoes with arch support and a firm heel grip; compression stockings for leg swelling.
  • Instruments: size and handle shape chosen for the surgeon's hand; ratchets or trigger locks so the hand does not squeeze for long; a power grip rather than a fingertip grip where possible; powered tools to cut hand force; no sharp edges pressing into the hand.
  • Planning: set the table height, patient position and monitors before the case; an "ergonomics buddy" reminds the team; seated or robotic surgery may suit long cases.
  • Exercise: deep neck flexor nods (hold 10 s, 10 times) and scapular retraction during the day; upper trapezius, levator scapulae and pectoral stretches between cases (hold 30 s, twice each side); back and neck strengthening and general fitness outside theatre.

5Manual handling and labourers

Low back pain in industry (W22 and W25, 7 marks); labourer loading 50 kg bags (W22 long case); automobile worker who stoops and squats (S19).

  • Factors for low back pain in industry: heavy, frequent or awkward lifting (bending and twisting), lifting far from the body or from the floor, whole-body vibration, prolonged sitting or standing, slips and falls; low fitness, smoking and obesity; job dissatisfaction and stress.
  • Revised NIOSH lifting equation: under ideal conditions a load of 23 kg is the recommended limit, and it is reduced for distance from the body, height, travel, twisting, frequency and poor grip. A 50 kg bag lifted to 3 feet is well beyond it, so the task itself must change.
  • Prevention: redesign first (lighter bags, trolleys, hoists, conveyors, loads at waist height, team lifts), then training and fitness. Training in lifting technique alone did not prevent back pain (Verbeek et al., 2011; see also safe lifting in Neuro).
  • Squatting and stooping work (automobile workers, farm labour): knee and ankle pain, back strain; low stools or kneeling pads, rotate tasks, strengthen quadriceps and hips, stretch calves and hamstrings.

The housemaid, the physiotherapist and the air hostess have been asked on their own, so each has a fuller sub-section below.

Housemaid with low back ache (7 marks in S16)

Paid domestic work in India is done mostly at floor level, with simple tools, in several houses a day. In a Bengaluru survey of 408 women domestic workers, the average working day was about 6 h, and most worked all 7 days of the week (Jebaraj et al., 2022).

  • Pain in the past 12 months in the same survey: knees 38.2%, upper back 31.9%, low back 27.5%.
  • Risk factors for her low back pain: more than 10 years in the job and psychological distress each about doubled the odds (Jebaraj et al., 2022). Low fitness, obesity and earlier injury add to them.
  • Knees: prolonged kneeling or squatting at work nearly doubled the odds of knee osteoarthritis (odds ratio 1.9), and obesity raised the risk much further (Coggon et al., 2000).
  • Washing work: in women who wash utensils and clothes, symptoms were linked with the working posture, the height of the washing platform and the height of the seat (Tondre et al., 2025).

Long handles, knee pads, wheeled buckets and task rotation follow the OSHA housekeeping guidance; keeping the load close to the waist and not twisting follow the UK Health and Safety Executive's handling advice.

Physiotherapy for the housemaid

  • Assess: pain, back and hip mobility, core endurance, hamstring length and the knees; ask her to show each chore, and score the worst posture with REBA.
  • Educate: back pain is common and rarely serious; stay active and keep working with the changes above.
  • Strengthen (8–12 reps, 2–3 days a week, as in ACSM guidance): abdominal bracing, bridging, four-point arm and leg lifts, and sit-to-stand or wall squats for the quadriceps and hips.
  • Stretch the hamstrings, hip flexors and calves, each held 10–30 s; practise bending from the hips with the back straight.
  • Walk regularly and control weight, which also protects the knees; relaxation for stress.

Classic answer vs current practice: low back pain

Classic

Rest, a lumbar belt, and electrotherapy such as TENS, ultrasound or interferential therapy before exercise.

Current

NICE (NG59) advises information to stay active and an exercise programme, with manual therapy only as part of a package that includes exercise. It says not to offer belts or corsets, TENS, ultrasound or interferential therapy.

Write in the exam

"Education to stay active, exercise and change of the tasks; belts and electrotherapy are not recommended for low back pain (NICE)."

Ergonomic care for physiotherapists (7 marks in S19)

Physiotherapists get the same work-related disorders they treat. A review found a lifetime prevalence of 55–91%. The low back is the commonest site, followed by the neck, upper back and shoulders (Milhem et al., 2016).

  • USA: 45% had low back symptoms in the past year, followed by the wrist and hand, upper back and neck. Lifting or transferring dependent patients was the factor rated most likely to cause them (Bork et al., 1996).
  • Australia: lifetime prevalence was 91%, and about 1 in 6 changed jobs within physiotherapy or left it. Mobilisation and manipulation were linked with thumb symptoms (Cromie et al., 2000).
  • India: about 9 in 10 physiotherapists in a Delhi survey had pain that began after they started work. Specialty, gender, clinic furniture and the hours of patient contact were related to it (Iqbal and Alghadir, 2015).
  • Risk factors: lifting and transferring patients, repetitive movements, awkward and static postures, treating many patients in a day, and working while injured. Low back pain is more common in women, younger therapists and rehabilitation settings (Milhem et al., 2016).

Work heights for light and heavy work are from the Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety guidance on standing work.

  • Equipment first: knowing correct body mechanics does not by itself prevent injury, so mechanical aids for transfers are needed (Milhem et al., 2016).
  • Body mechanics as a support: keep the patient close to your waist, take a stable wide base, bend the hips and knees a little, do not twist (turn with the feet), and keep the head up.
  • Work organisation: a fair caseload, scheduled rest breaks, regular ergonomics training and yearly health checks (Mahmood et al., 2025); rotate tasks; report pain early and do not work through an injury.
  • Own fitness: daily physical activity; back, core, neck, shoulder and grip strengthening; stretching between patients.
  • Self-check: a Nordic questionnaire survey of the team, and REBA scores of transfer and treatment postures.

Air hostess (3 marks in W18)

Cabin crew lift, push, pull and reach in a narrow cabin that moves. NIOSH lists lifting, pushing and pulling, reaching, prolonged standing, cramped or twisted postures and repetition. Turbulence adds the risk of falls. In one airline study most injuries were to the back, neck or shoulders. Back injuries were linked most often with turbulence, overhead bins and service trolleys (Logie et al., 1997).

  • Bags: avoid lifting heavy bags overhead; passengers lift their own bags or check them in; heavy bags go under the seat.
  • Trolleys: push with both hands, close to the body, using body weight; keep trolleys maintained and report broken wheels.
  • Turbulence: stop service, secure the trolleys and sit with the belt fastened; use handholds when moving.
  • Galley: store often-used items where they need no overhead lifting or deep bending.
  • Standing and long duty days: supportive shoes without high heels, calf raises, compression stockings if the legs swell; a sleep plan for irregular schedules and time-zone changes.
  • Fitness: back, core and upper-body strengthening.

7Psychological hazards, job stress and shift work

Asked for 7 marks in W17, S18 and W18 (stress and psychological hazards) and in S20, W21 and W24 (the physiotherapist's role in stress management), and 3 marks in W21 (shift work).

  • Causes: high demands with little control over the work (the demand–control model); great effort with little reward (effort–reward imbalance); monotony and job dissatisfaction; anxiety about finishing work on time and to quality; long hours and shift work; poor relationships, harassment, insecurity.
  • Effects: muscle tension and pain, headache, fatigue, poor sleep, raised blood pressure, anxiety, depression, more errors and accidents, absence.
  • Prevention at work: reasonable workload and deadlines, more control and variety, breaks, support from supervisors, clear roles, safe workplaces.

The physiotherapist's role in stress management

  • Relaxation: progressive muscle relaxation (Jacobson: tense then release each muscle group), physiological relaxation (Laura Mitchell: move into the opposite of the stress posture), diaphragmatic breathing.
  • Exercise: regular aerobic exercise and stretching programmes at work; yoga.
  • Ergonomic and task changes that remove physical strain, and education on posture, sleep and pacing.
  • Referral to a counsellor or doctor for anxiety or depression.

Effects of shift work (W21)

  • Disturbed body clock: poor sleep, fatigue, more accidents.
  • Digestive problems, weight gain and metabolic and heart risk; night shift work is classified as "probably carcinogenic" (IARC, 2019).
  • Strain on family and social life.
  • Advice: forward-rotating shifts, protected sleep in a dark quiet room, regular meals, exercise, avoiding long runs of night shifts.

Management

  • Pain relief (first 2 weeks): heat, TENS, gentle mobilisation, as a bridge to exercise.
  • Exercise: deep neck flexor training (chin nods), scapular retraction and lower trapezius strengthening with bands, neck and thoracic mobility, pectoral stretching; progressing to resisted shoulder and neck strengthening and general fitness.
  • Ergonomics: the workstation set-up in section 2, a headset, micro-breaks with 2–3 workstation exercises.
  • Childcare: lift the child close to the body with knees bent, feed with the back supported and the baby on a pillow, use a carrier that spreads the load.
  • Health promotion: sleep, stress management (breathing, relaxation), walking or yoga, weight and posture awareness.

3 mMechanical stresses faced by truck drivers. (S24)

Prolonged sitting in a fixed posture; whole-body vibration transmitted through the seat; poor seat and reach causing twisting and neck strain; loading and unloading heavy goods and climbing in and out of the cab; long hours and night driving causing fatigue.

3 mErgonomic advice to an air hostess. (W18)

Stresses: lifting, pushing and pulling trolleys, reaching overhead, prolonged standing, cramped postures, turbulence and long irregular duty days. Advice: avoid lifting heavy bags overhead (passengers lift their own, heavy bags under the seat); push trolleys with both hands close to the body and report broken wheels; stop service and sit belted in turbulence; store galley items without overhead reach; supportive shoes, calf raises, stockings if legs swell; sleep plan; back, core and upper-body strengthening.

3 mEffects of shift work on health. (W21)

Disturbed circadian rhythm with poor sleep and fatigue; more errors and accidents; digestive problems, weight gain and metabolic and heart risk; night shift work is probably carcinogenic (IARC 2019); strain on family and social life.

7 mDiscuss the role of physiotherapy in stress management in an industrial set-up. (S20, W21, W24)

Define work stress and list its causes (demand–control, effort–reward, monotony, deadlines, shift work) and effects (muscle tension, pain, fatigue, sleep problems, anxiety). PT role: screening and education; relaxation (Jacobson's progressive relaxation, Mitchell's physiological relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing); workplace exercise and stretching programmes and aerobic fitness; yoga; ergonomic redesign to remove physical strain; pacing, breaks and sleep advice; referral for psychological help; evaluation of the programme (pain, absenteeism, stress questionnaires).

LAQA 27-year-old labourer loads 50 kg bags into a truck at about 3 feet, has back pain radiating to the right leg for 5 months, no neurological deficit, pain 3/10 on bending and loading, and smokes bidis. Functional diagnosis with ICF; goals and plan. (7 + 8, W22)

ICF (7): health condition: mechanical low back pain with referred leg pain; body functions: pain on flexion and loading, possible reduced hamstring flexibility and core endurance; activities: bending, lifting, loading; participation: daily wage work; environment: 50 kg bags with no lifting aids (barrier), co-workers (possible team lifting); personal: young smoker. Goals and plan (8): short term: pain-free bending, correct lifting habits; long term: full work without recurrence. Plan: education and reassurance, graded activity, core and hip strengthening, hamstring flexibility, aerobic fitness, smoking cessation advice, and above all task change: 25 kg bags or two-person lifts, trolleys or a ramp, loading from waist height, rotating tasks.

MCQThe top of a computer screen should be:A. Well above eye levelB. At or slightly below eye levelC. At chest levelD. At desk level

B.

MCQFor prolonged standing, the most effective preventive measure was found to be:A. Standing still on a hard floorB. Regular movement and changes of positionC. Tighter shoesD. Longer shifts with fewer breaks

B. Mats, sit–stand options, footwear and stockings also help.

MCQFor laparoscopic surgery, the operating table is best set at about:A. 1.2 × elbow heightB. Shoulder heightC. 0.7–0.8 × elbow heightD. Knee height

C. Lower than for open surgery, so the instrument handles are near elbow level.

MCQThe load constant of the revised NIOSH lifting equation is:A. 10 kgB. 23 kgC. 40 kgD. 50 kg

B.
