1(c)Obesity in adults and children: body composition, BMI, grades, causes and weight management

Obesity was asked in 14 of 20 papers, with 10 long cases about a housewife, banker, lecturer, schoolboy or bride-to-be who needs to lose weight. Every answer has the same three parts: measure (BMI, waist, body fat, fitness), explain (why she gained weight and what it does to health), and prescribe (exercise with FITT, diet with a dietitian, and habits she can keep).

1Definition and types

Define obesity and list its types: 3 marks in W16 and S25.

  • Obesity is abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that may impair health (WHO). It is screened with the body mass index and waist, and confirmed, where possible, by body fat.
  • BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². Example: 68 kg and 1.60 m gives 68 ÷ 2.56 = 26.6 kg/m².
BasisTypes
Fat distributionAndroid (apple, central, upper-body; commoner in men; higher risk of diabetes and heart disease) and gynoid (pear, hips and thighs; commoner in women)
Fat cellsHyperplastic (more fat cells; starts in childhood; harder to reverse) and hypertrophic (bigger fat cells; adult onset)
CausePrimary (simple, exogenous: energy intake more than use) and secondary (endogenous: hypothyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, PCOS, drugs such as steroids, genetic syndromes such as Prader–Willi)
SeverityBy BMI grade (section 2)

2Grades of obesity and their significance

Grades of obesity and their clinical significance: 3 marks in W17, 7 marks in S24; BMI and its uses: 3 marks in W22 and S23.

Normal18.5–24.918.5–22.9

The revised Indian definition (2025)

  • Any BMI over 23 kg/m² counts as obesity in Asian Indians; there is no separate "overweight" step.
  • Stage 1: extra fat without effects on organ function or daily activities.
  • Stage 2: BMI over 23 plus a large waist (90 cm or more in men, 80 cm or more in women) or waist-to-height ratio over 0.5, plus limits in daily activities (for example knee pain, breathlessness) or an obesity-related disease (for example type 2 diabetes).
  • BMI grades in the same paper: I 23–24.9, II 25–27.5, III 27.6–32.4, IV 32.5 or more. Body fat over 25.5% in men and over 38% in women is obesity.

Clinical significance

  • Risk of diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and death rises with each grade; at the same BMI, Asian Indians carry more body fat and abdominal fat, so the cut-offs are lower.
  • Grade guides treatment: lifestyle for all; drugs and closer medical care in higher grades or with disease; bariatric surgery for severe obesity with disease.
  • Waist adds what BMI misses: a person with normal BMI and a large waist is still at risk.
  • Uses of BMI: simple, cheap screening of individuals and populations; tracking change; deciding treatment. Limits: does not separate fat from muscle (a muscular athlete scores high), says nothing about fat distribution, needs age charts in children.

3Body composition and anthropometry

Methods of measuring body composition (S16, W16, 7 marks); anthropometric assessment in obesity (S26, 7 marks); fat mass and fat-free mass (S24, 3 marks).

  • Fat mass: all the body's fat, both essential fat (in nerves, bone marrow, organs; a little needed for health, more in women) and storage fat under the skin and around the organs.
  • Fat-free mass: everything else: muscle, bone, organs and water (about three-quarters water). It sets the resting metabolic rate, so weight loss should remove fat and keep fat-free mass (resistance training, enough protein, no crash diets).
  • Body fat percentage = fat mass ÷ body weight × 100.

Anthropometric assessment in obesity (S26)

  1. Weight (light clothes, no shoes, calibrated scale) and height (stadiometer); BMI.
  2. Waist circumference (site in the figure); hip circumference; waist-to-hip and waist-to-height ratios.
  3. Skinfolds at standard sites; mid-upper-arm and neck circumference (neck for sleep apnoea risk).
  4. In children: BMI-for-age on IAP charts (section 5).
  5. Record the same time of day, the same side and the mean of two readings; repeat monthly.

4Causes, set-point theory and effects on health

Set-point theory (S20, 7 marks); obesity and its effects on adult health (W20, 7 marks).

  • Causes: energy intake more than energy use: high-calorie food, sugary drinks, eating out; sedentary work and screen time; poor sleep and stress; genetics and family habits; pregnancy and caesarean recovery without return to activity; menopause; drugs (steroids, some antidepressants); hormonal disease (hypothyroidism, PCOS); stopping sport or smoking; ageing.
  • Set-point theory: the brain (hypothalamus, with signals such as leptin from fat) defends a "set" body weight like a thermostat. When a person diets, hunger rises and metabolism falls, so weight returns towards the old level; this explains regain after crash diets. Slow weight loss with regular exercise and lasting habits is thought to help the body settle at a lower weight.

5Childhood obesity

Causes of childhood obesity (S20, W21, W24, 7 marks); causes, ill effects, school fitness screening and health promotion (W20, long answer); 14-year-old boy who eats fast food (W21, long case).

  • Measure: BMI-for-age on the IAP 2015 charts for 5–18 years: overweight from the line equal to an adult BMI of 23, obesity from the line equal to 27. Under 5 years, use WHO standards.
  • Causes: fast food, sugary drinks and snacks; large portions; too little play and sport; screen time; short sleep; parents with obesity and family eating habits; urban living with no safe play space; academic pressure; emotional eating; rarely endocrine or genetic disease; low or high birth weight, mother's diabetes in pregnancy, formula feeding.
  • Ill effects: early type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and cholesterol, fatty liver, sleep apnoea, asthma; knee pain, flat feet, knock knees, slipped capital femoral epiphysis; early puberty and PCOS in girls; teasing, low self-esteem, depression; obesity continues into adult life.

Plan: consent from parents, screening at the start of the year by trained teachers and physiotherapists, a report card for each child, referral of children with obesity, high blood pressure or very low fitness, and repeat testing at the end of the year.

Health promotion for overweight children

  • Activity: at least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity, mostly aerobic, with vigorous and muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on 3 days a week (WHO); daily physical education and sport periods; active travel to school.
  • Food: no junk food or sugary drinks in canteens; fruit and home food in tiffin; water bottles; family meals.
  • Habits: limit recreational screen time; regular sleep.
  • Whole family changes habits together; no blame or teasing; praise effort, not weight.
  • Teacher and parent education; posters and competitions; fun, non-competitive games for children who dislike sport.

6Weight management: exercise and lifestyle

Exercise prescription and lifestyle change appear in almost every obesity long case (S19, S21, S20, W24, W22, S25).

Exercise prescription (FITT) for weight loss
FITTAerobicResistance
FrequencyAt least 5 days a week2–3 days a week, not on consecutive days
IntensityModerate (40–59% of heart rate reserve; can talk, not sing), progress to vigorousModerate load, 8–12 repetitions, 2–3 sets
Time30 minutes a day building to 60 (150 minutes a week, then more than 250 for real weight loss and to keep it off)8–10 exercises for the major muscle groups
TypeBrisk walking, cycling, swimming, water exercise, dancing; low impact if joints hurtBody weight, bands, weights; keeps fat-free mass
  • Heart rate target (Karvonen): target = resting HR + (maximum HR − resting HR) × intensity. For a 27-year-old with resting HR 80: maximum 220 − 27 = 193; reserve 113; 40–59% gives about 125–147 beats a minute.
  • Evidence: 150–250 minutes a week prevents weight gain and gives modest loss; more than 250 minutes a week gives clinically important loss, especially with a diet (ACSM, 2009).
  • Diet (with a dietitian): a deficit of about 500 kcal a day loses about 0.5 kg a week; fewer refined carbohydrates, sugar and fried food; more vegetables, pulses and protein; regular meals.
  • Target: 5–10% of body weight over 3–6 months; then maintenance.
  • Lifestyle: more daily steps (stairs, walking during calls), less sitting, 7–8 hours of sleep, stress management, a food and activity diary, family support.
  • Precautions: screen for heart disease, diabetes and high blood pressure; low-impact work for painful knees (cycling, water exercise, quadriceps strengthening; see section 8); avoid exercising in heat; good footwear; monitor blood sugar in diabetes.

Why crash dieting fails

  • Rapid loss is mostly water and muscle, not fat; metabolism slows (set point); hunger rises.
  • Fatigue, dizziness, poor concentration, hair loss, constipation, irregular periods, gallstones, nutrient deficiency; risk of disordered eating.
  • Weight returns, often with more fat ("yo-yo" dieting).

7Worked long case: weight loss before marriage

From real papers (W22, 3 + 5 + 7; S25, 5 + 10)

"A 27-year-old girl has been referred to your physiotherapy clinic for weight reduction. She is 5 ft 3 inches and weighs 68 kgs and has a waist circumference of 99 cms. Her body fat percentage is 50%, and YMCA sit and reach test values are -3 cms. She can do 12 modified push-ups and 34 squats in a minute. She is getting married in 3 months and has started crash dieting for her weight loss. Comment on her fitness status and write about her fitness management program." (S25)

Spot the clues before you write

5 ft 3 in, 68 kg: BMI 26.6. waist 99 cm, body fat 50%: abdominal obesity with very high fat. sit and reach −3 cm: poor flexibility. 12 modified push-ups: fair. marriage in 3 months: a deadline, realistic goals. crash dieting: stop it and explain why.

Fitness management programme

  • Warm-up 5–10 minutes of easy walking and mobility.
  • Aerobic 5 days a week: brisk walking, cycling, dance or swimming; 30 minutes rising to 50–60; 40–59% of heart rate reserve (about 125–147 beats a minute), progressing to intervals of faster walking.
  • Resistance 2–3 days a week: squats, lunges, bridges, modified push-ups, rows with a band, planks; 8–12 repetitions, 2–3 sets.
  • Flexibility daily: hamstring, calf, hip flexor and back stretches, 30 seconds × 2–3.
  • Cool-down and stretching.
  • Lifestyle: a daily step target that rises each week, stairs, sleep, water, food diary; explain why crash diets fail; a plan to continue after the wedding.
  • Review every 2–4 weeks: weight, waist, body fat, fitness tests.

8Obesity with knee pain

S16 (50-year-old man, class I obesity, both knees) and W16 (54-year-old homemaker, 150 cm, 75 kg, knee pain worse on stairs): ICF and rehabilitation.

  • Why the knees hurt: extra load on the joint with every step and stair, weak quadriceps, low activity, and inflammation from fat tissue; early knee osteoarthritis.
  • Exercise: quadriceps (static quads, straight-leg raise, terminal knee extension, mini-squats within pain), hip abductors and extensors, hamstring and calf stretches; non-weight-bearing aerobic exercise (static cycling, water walking) progressing to walking.
  • Weight loss with diet and exercise; losing more than 5% of body weight within about 20 weeks improved disability in knee osteoarthritis (Christensen et al., 2007).
  • Joint protection: avoid squatting and sitting cross-legged on the floor during flares, use a higher chair and western toilet, handrail on stairs (up with the good leg, down with the painful one), a stick in the opposite hand, cushioned footwear.
  • Pain relief: heat or ice, TENS, as a bridge to exercise.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mFat mass and fat-free mass. (S24)

Body weight = fat mass + fat-free mass. Fat mass: essential and storage fat. Fat-free mass: muscle, bone, organs and water (about three-quarters water); it determines resting metabolic rate. Measured by skinfolds, BIA, DEXA, underwater weighing. Weight loss should reduce fat mass and preserve fat-free mass (resistance training, protein, no crash diets).

3 mBody mass index and its uses. (W22, S23)

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². WHO: normal 18.5–24.9, overweight 25–29.9, obese 30 or more; Asian Indians: normal up to 22.9, overweight 23–24.9, obese 25 or more. Uses: screening, grading, tracking, treatment decisions, population surveys. Limits: does not separate fat from muscle or show fat distribution.

7 mDiscuss five causes of childhood obesity. (W21)

Define (BMI-for-age, IAP adult-equivalent 23 and 27 lines). Causes: fast food and sugary drinks; inactivity and screen time; short sleep; family obesity and habits; urban environment without play space; academic stress and emotional eating; rarely endocrine or genetic disease; early-life factors (maternal diabetes, birth weight). Add one line on prevention for each.

LAQA 35-year-old banker, 155 cm, 96 kg, gained weight after a caesarean six years ago; long working hours. Exercise prescription, lifestyle modification and ergonomic advice. (5 + 5 + 5, S21)

BMI 96 ÷ 1.55² = 40.0 (class III). Screen BP, sugar, thyroid, joints. Exercise: low-impact aerobic 5 days a week, 20–30 minutes building to 60, 40–59% HRR; resistance 2–3 days, 8–12 reps; stretches; progress to more than 250 minutes a week; check abdominal wall (diastasis) and back. Lifestyle: dietitian-led 500 kcal deficit, food diary, sleep, stress, walking breaks and stairs, home-cooked lunch. Ergonomics: workstation set-up, chair with lumbar support, screen at eye level, feet supported, micro-breaks every 30–60 minutes with desk exercises.
