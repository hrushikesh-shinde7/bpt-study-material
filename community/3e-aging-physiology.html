3(e)Physiology of ageing: musculoskeletal, neurological, sensory, cardiorespiratory and metabolic changes, and the effects of exercise

Ageing changes were asked in 14 of 20 papers, usually one system at a time: "age-related changes in the nervous system", "six cardiopulmonary changes", "musculoskeletal changes". Learn each system as a short list of changes, add what each change does to function, and finish with what exercise can still improve. That last part turns a 7-mark list into a physiotherapy answer.

My revision

1Ageing in general

  • Ageing is the gradual, progressive decline in the body's reserve and its ability to adapt to stress, which raises the risk of disease and death with time. Theories of why it happens are on theories of ageing.
  • Primary ageing is the change that happens to everyone; secondary ageing is the change added by disease, inactivity, smoking and poor nutrition. Much of what looks like ageing is secondary, which is why exercise helps.
  • Reserve: at rest an older person may look normal; the loss shows when the body is stressed (illness, surgery, heat, exercise, a fall).
  • People age at different rates, and different systems in the same person age at different rates.
LevelWhat the person can do
FunTakes part in work, home and leisure without restriction
FunctionStill does most work and home tasks, but changes how they are done; leisure is limited by falling capacity
FrailtyBasic daily activities use up most of the reserve; community activities are limited; needs help with home or outside tasks
FailureNeeds help with basic and instrumental daily activities; may be bedbound

3Neurological and sensorimotor changes

Nervous system: 7 marks in S17, W25 and S26; sensorimotor system: 7 marks in S23; visual changes: 3 marks in S16.

Central nervous system

  • Brain volume falls (more in the frontal lobes and hippocampus); loss of neurons in some areas and of synapses and dendrites; white matter changes.
  • Fall in neurotransmitters, especially dopamine (slower movement) and acetylcholine (memory).
  • Cognition: slower processing speed and reaction time; weaker short-term and working memory and divided attention; knowledge, vocabulary and judgement are kept.
  • Motor control: slower, less smooth movements, poorer fine coordination, slower anticipatory postural adjustments.
  • Sleep: lighter, broken sleep; earlier waking.

Peripheral and autonomic nervous system

  • Loss of motor and sensory nerve fibres; slower nerve conduction; ankle jerks may be reduced or absent.
  • Autonomic: weaker baroreflex (dizziness on standing, postural hypotension); poor temperature control; bladder changes (urgency, nocturia).

Sensory systems

SenseChangeWhat it means
VisionPresbyopia (cannot focus near); smaller pupil and yellower lens, so less light reaches the retina; poor dark adaptation and glare tolerance; lower contrast sensitivity and depth perception; narrower visual field; cataract, glaucoma and macular degeneration more commonMisjudges steps and kerbs, falls at night, reading difficulty: good lighting, contrast edges on steps, no glare, regular eye checks

Sensorimotor summary (S23): slower sensing (fewer receptors and fibres), slower processing (central changes) and slower, weaker responses (fewer motor units, sarcopenia) together make balance reactions late and weak, which is why falls rise with age (see falls).

4Cardiovascular and respiratory changes

Cardiorespiratory changes: 3 marks in S19, S20 and W24, and part of the W20 long answer; respiratory changes: 7 marks in S22, 3 marks in S26.

Heart and vessels

  • Arteries stiffen: systolic blood pressure and pulse pressure rise.
  • The left ventricle wall thickens and relaxes slowly, so filling depends more on the atrial contraction (atrial fibrillation is poorly tolerated).
  • Maximum heart rate falls (roughly 220 minus age); the heart responds less to adrenaline; resting heart rate changes little.
  • Maximum cardiac output and VO₂max fall: about 3–6% per decade in the twenties and thirties, more than 20% per decade from the seventies (Fleg et al., 2005).
  • Weaker baroreflex: postural hypotension; veins less efficient: varicose veins, swelling.

Respiratory system (7 marks in S22)

An older person usually breathes normally at rest. The losses show as reduced reserve in exertion, pneumonia, surgery or bed rest. Smoking, inactivity and lung disease add secondary loss on top of these primary changes.

Physiotherapy implications

  • Surgery and illness: age is an independent risk factor for lung complications after surgery: odds ratio about 2.1 at 60–69 and 3.0 at 70–79 (Smetana et al., 2006). Teach deep breathing and coughing before surgery and mobilise early.
  • Position: sit or stand upright rather than lying flat. In people aged 67–88, PaO₂ was about 5 mmHg higher sitting than lying (Hardie et al., 2002).
  • Inspiratory muscle training: in healthy adults over 60 it raised maximal inspiratory pressure by about 26 cmH₂O, against about 4 in controls, but did not clearly improve walking distance (Manifield et al., 2021).
  • Aerobic training: raised VO₂max by about 16% in older adults (Huang et al., 2005). It improves exercise tolerance even though it does not stop the decline in resting lung function.
  • Posture and chest mobility: thoracic extension, rib cage mobility and breathing exercises against kyphosis.
  • Airway clearance and prevention: active cycle of breathing and huffing in chest infection; avoid long bed rest; stop smoking; advise vaccination; watch for silent aspiration in people with swallowing problems.
  • Monitoring: do not rely on breathlessness alone; check oxygen saturation and the Borg scale.

5Metabolic and other changes

Metabolic changes were part of the W20 long answer.

  • Body composition: less muscle and body water, more fat (especially around the abdomen).
  • Basal metabolic rate falls, mostly because muscle mass falls; weight gain is easy if food intake stays the same.
  • Glucose: insulin resistance and glucose intolerance; type 2 diabetes more common.
  • Hormones: fall in growth hormone, sex hormones (menopause, lower testosterone) and vitamin D made in the skin.
  • Temperature control: less sweating and shivering, weaker thirst: risk of heat stroke, cold injury and dehydration.
  • Kidneys: lower filtration; drugs are cleared more slowly.
  • Skin: thin, dry, fragile, slow to heal: pressure sores, tears, care with electrotherapy and heat.
  • Immune system: weaker, so infections are more common and vaccines work less well.

6Effects of exercise in older people

Effects of exercise on the neuromuscular system (W21, long answer; W25, 7 marks) and on the musculoskeletal and nervous systems (W22, 7 marks).

SystemEffect of training
MuscleProgressive resistance training increases strength and power at any age: frail nursing-home residents aged about 90 gained 174% in strength and 9% in thigh muscle area, and tandem gait speed improved 48%, after 8 weeks (Fiatarone et al., 1990). Early gains are neural (better recruitment and firing), later gains add muscle size
Nervous systemFaster reaction time, better coordination and balance reactions; aerobic exercise improves blood flow to the brain, memory and mood, and lowers the risk of cognitive decline
Balance and fallsExercise reduced the rate of falls in older people living in the community by 23%; programmes with balance and functional exercise worked best (Sherrington et al., 2019)
Bone and jointsWeight-bearing and resistance exercise slow bone loss; exercise reduces osteoarthritis pain and stiffness; range of motion improves with stretching
Heart and lungsHigher VO₂max, lower resting heart rate and blood pressure, better exercise tolerance
MetabolismBetter insulin sensitivity and blood sugar, less abdominal fat, better sleep
Function and mindFaster walking, easier stairs and sit-to-stand, independence; less depression and anxiety, more social contact

WHO recommendations for adults aged 65 and over (2020)

  • 150–300 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity.
  • Muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week.
  • Multicomponent activity with balance and strength training on 3 or more days a week, to improve function and prevent falls.
  • Do as much as abilities allow; some activity is better than none; reduce sitting.

Precautions

  • Screen for heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, diabetes, osteoporosis and falls risk; check drugs (beta-blockers blunt heart rate, so use the Borg scale).
  • Longer warm-up and cool-down; avoid breath-holding; rise slowly from lying (postural hypotension); keep hydrated and avoid heat.
  • Start low, progress slowly; supervise balance work; good footwear and a safe floor.

7Model long answer: ageing changes and exercise

From a real paper (W22, 9 + 7 marks as printed)

"Explain the various changes occurring in the Musculoskeletal & Neurological systems due to aging (9 Marks). Also write effects of exercises in the same systems in this population. (7 Marks)"

  1. [Introduction, 1] Define ageing; primary and secondary ageing; loss of reserve.
  2. [Musculoskeletal, 4] Muscle, bone, cartilage, tendons, discs, posture and gait, each with its effect on function (section 2).
  3. [Neurological, 4] Brain, cognition, motor control, peripheral nerves, autonomic, senses (section 3).
  4. [Exercise, 7] Resistance, balance, aerobic and flexibility training with their effects on muscle, bone, nerves, balance and falls, with two evidence lines (Fiatarone; Sherrington), WHO doses and precautions (section 6).

The W20 long answer pairs cardiorespiratory and metabolic changes (sections 4 and 5) with national policies for senior citizens (see elderly in India) and the fun-to-failure model (section 1). No patient case has been set on this topic alone; use these changes to explain impairments in any elderly long case (see geriatric evaluation).

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mVisual changes due to ageing. (S16)

Presbyopia; smaller pupil and yellow lens (less light, colour change); poor dark adaptation and glare tolerance; reduced contrast sensitivity and depth perception; narrower field; cataract, glaucoma, macular degeneration. Effect: falls on steps and at night; advice on lighting, contrast edges and eye checks.

3 mEnumerate six cardiopulmonary changes in the elderly. (S20, W24)

Stiffer arteries with raised systolic BP; lower maximum heart rate and VO₂max; weaker baroreflex (postural hypotension); stiffer chest wall and weaker respiratory muscles; loss of lung recoil with lower FEV₁ and VC and higher RV; weaker cough and mucociliary clearance.

7 mAge-related changes in the respiratory system. (S22)

Normal at rest, less reserve. Chest wall stiffer (rib cage compliance about 30% lower); lung recoil lost; diaphragm strength about 25% lower; TLC same, RV up about 50%, VC down, FEV₁ down 20–30 ml a year; airways close in normal breathing (about 44 lying, 65 sitting); alveolar area 75 to 60 m²; PaO₂ ≈ 109 − 0.43 × age, PaCO₂ unchanged; hypoxic drive down about 50%; weaker cough, slower clearance, silent aspiration. PT: pre-operative breathing and early mobilisation (age raises lung complications), upright positioning, inspiratory muscle training, aerobic training, posture work, airway clearance, monitor saturation.

7 mAge-related changes in the nervous system. (S17, S26)

Central: brain volume loss (frontal, hippocampus), fewer synapses, white matter change, less dopamine and acetylcholine; slower processing and reaction time, weaker working memory, kept knowledge; slower, less coordinated movement; lighter sleep. Peripheral: fewer fibres, slower conduction, reduced ankle jerks, reduced vibration and position sense in the feet. Autonomic: postural hypotension, poor temperature control, bladder urgency. Senses: vision, hearing, vestibular loss. Result: slower balance reactions and falls; PT: balance, strength, dual-task and aerobic training.

LAQWrite physiological effects of ageing. Describe effects of exercise in detail on the neuromuscular system for the aged population. (W21)

Define ageing, primary and secondary. Changes by system: musculoskeletal, neurological, sensory, cardiovascular, respiratory, metabolic (sections 2–5). Effects of exercise on the neuromuscular system: strength and power gains with resistance training (neural then muscle size; Fiatarone 1990), faster reaction time and better coordination, balance training cutting falls by 23% (Sherrington 2019), aerobic exercise for cognition and mood; WHO doses; precautions.
