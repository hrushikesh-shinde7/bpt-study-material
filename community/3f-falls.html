3(f)Falls in the elderly: risk factors, pathomechanics of balance, fear of falling and fall prevention

Falls were asked in 8 of 20 papers, with 5 long answers, and they appear inside most elderly cases too. Every answer has the same skeleton: why older people fall (intrinsic and extrinsic factors, failing balance systems), how to find who will fall (history and tests), and what prevents it (balance and strength exercise first, then the home, drugs, eyes, feet and fear).

My revision

1Falls: definition and burden

  • Fall (WHO): an event which results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground or floor or other lower level.
  • About 28–35% of people aged 65 and over fall each year, rising to 32–42% over 70 (WHO, 2007); a previous fall is the strongest predictor of another.
  • Consequences: fractures (hip, wrist, spine, pelvis), head injury, long lie on the floor (pressure sores, dehydration, pneumonia), fear of falling, activity restriction, loss of independence, admission to care, death.

3Pathomechanics of balance problems in the elderly

Pathomechanics of balance issues in the elderly (S20, W24, 7 marks of the long answer).

  • Sensory: less reliable information from eyes, inner ear and feet; older people depend more on vision, so dim light and busy patterns cause falls.
  • Central: slower reaction time and integration; walking while talking or carrying (dual-tasking) worsens gait.
  • Motor: loss of fast-twitch fibres and power, so the corrective step is too slow or too short; reduced ankle range and strength shift control from the ankle strategy to the hip strategy, and stepping becomes late.
  • Posture and gait: flexed posture moves the centre of mass forward; smaller limits of stability; slower, shorter, wider steps; more sway.
  • Anticipatory control: late postural adjustments before reaching or lifting.
  • Other: postural hypotension (brain under-perfused on standing), fear of falling (stiff, cautious gait), drugs (sedation, low BP).

4Assessing falls risk

  • Screening questions: Have you fallen in the past year? Do you feel unsteady? Do you worry about falling?
  • History of each fall: place, time, activity, dizziness or blackout, injury, could they get up, how long on the floor.
  • Tests: Timed Up and Go (13.5 seconds or more means higher risk), Berg Balance Scale, gait speed, 30-second chair stand, single-leg stance, postural BP, vision, feet and sensation, cognition, FES-I for fear (see assessment scales).
  • Medication review and home visit.
  • Risk level decides the response: low risk gets education and exercise; high risk (a fall with injury, repeated falls, inability to get up, or poor gait and balance) gets a full multifactorial assessment and tailored plan (World Falls Guidelines, 2022).

5Fall prevention

Fall-prevention programme (S20, W24, 8 marks); environmental modifications (W22); how can falls be prevented (W17, 4 marks).

Exercise: the most effective single measure

  • Exercise reduced the rate of falls in community-dwelling older people by 23%; balance and functional exercise reduced it by 24%, and programmes adding resistance training by about a third (Sherrington et al., 2019).
  • Larger effects come from programmes that challenge balance and give more than 3 hours of exercise a week in total (Sherrington et al., 2017).
  • Otago Exercise Programme: home-based progressive leg strengthening (ankle weights) and balance exercises about 3 times a week plus walking, taught by a physiotherapist over several visits; it reduced falls by about a third (Thomas et al., 2010).
  • Balance training content: standing with a narrower base (feet together, tandem, single leg), reducing hand support, moving the centre of mass (reaching, weight shifts), stepping in all directions, walking with turns, heel and toe walking, sit-to-stand, stair practice; tai chi; progress by reducing support and adding dual tasks.
  • Strength: sit-to-stand, mini-squats, heel and toe raises, hip abduction and extension, knee extension with weights, 2–3 sets of 8–12, 2–3 times a week.

Environmental modifications

AreaModification
LightingBright, even light; switches at room entrances and by the bed; night lights to the toilet
FloorsRemove loose rugs, cords and clutter; non-slip mats; wipe spills; mark step edges with contrasting colour
StairsRails on both sides, good light at top and bottom, no objects on steps
Bathroom and toiletGrab bars; western toilet or a raised commode over the Indian toilet; non-slip floor; bathing stool; hand shower

Kitchen and storageCommon items between waist and shoulder height; no climbing on stools
OutdoorsEven paths, rails at entrances, avoid walking on wet or dark roads

Home safety assessment and modification reduced the rate of falls (rate ratio 0.81), with more benefit in people at high risk and when done by an occupational therapist (Gillespie et al., 2012).

Other measures

  • Medication review: stop or reduce sedatives and other risky drugs.
  • Vision: cataract surgery, updated glasses; care with bifocals on stairs.
  • Feet: podiatry, proper footwear.
  • Postural hypotension: rise in stages, fluids, drug review.
  • Bone health: calcium, vitamin D if deficient, osteoporosis treatment; hip protectors in care homes.
  • Education: what causes falls, what to do after a fall, using a call bell or phone; keep active.

Getting up after a fall (teach it)

  1. Stay calm and check for injury; if hurt, call for help and keep warm.
  2. Roll onto the side, then onto hands and knees.
  3. Crawl to a firm chair or bed.
  4. Put hands on the seat, bring the stronger leg forward with the foot flat (half-kneeling).
  5. Push up with arms and leg, turn and sit; rest before standing.

6Worked long case: a man with two falls and fear of falling

From a real paper (S23, 5 + 10)

"Enumerate different causes of falls in elderly people. Discuss rehabilitation of a 72 years old male patient with a history of two falls in last one year and a positive fear of fall."

Spot the clues before you write

two falls in a year: recurrent faller, high risk: needs a full multifactorial assessment. positive fear of falling: he restricts activity, loses strength and confidence, and falls more: break this cycle. 72, male: check BP drugs, prostate symptoms with night toilet trips, vision.

Causes (5 marks): intrinsic and extrinsic factors from section 2, grouped under musculoskeletal, balance and gait, sensory, medical, psychological, environment, medicines, footwear.

3 mEnumerate the extrinsic risk factors for falls in the elderly. (W25)

Poor lighting; slippery or wet floors; loose rugs, cords, clutter; stairs without rails; low beds and chairs; Indian toilets and bathrooms without grab bars; uneven outdoor surfaces; poor footwear; faulty or wrong walking aids; medicines (sedatives, antidepressants, BP drugs, four or more drugs).

7 mEnumerate the causes of falls in the elderly and explain environmental modifications for prevention. (W22)

Causes: intrinsic (weakness, balance and gait, vision and sensation, postural hypotension, neurological and other diseases, cognition and mood, fear) and extrinsic (environment, drugs, footwear, aids). Modifications by area: lighting, floors, stairs, bathroom and toilet, bedroom, kitchen, outdoors; home safety checks reduce falls, more in high-risk people.

LAQDescribe the pathomechanics of balance issues in the elderly and a physiotherapy programme for fall prevention. (7 + 8, S20, W24)

Pathomechanics: sensory decline (vision, vestibular, somatosensory), slower central processing and dual-task interference, motor decline (power, type II fibres, ankle range, strategy shift, slow stepping), flexed posture and smaller limits of stability, anticipatory control, postural hypotension, fear, drugs. Programme: screening and tests, progressive balance and strength (Otago; more than 3 hours a week), gait and dual-task training, home modification, drug review, vision and footwear, education and getting up from the floor, review.
