3(a–c)Senior citizens in India: social issues, national policies and schemes, NGOs, institutional vs community living, and communication barriers

This part was asked in 6 of 20 papers: national policies for the elderly, NGOs, communication barriers three times, and two long cases asking whether an older person should go to an institution or stay at home. There is no single right answer to that question; the examiner wants the advantages and disadvantages of each, weighed against this person's needs, wishes and supports.

My revision

1Senior citizens in India

  • Senior citizen: a person aged 60 or over (Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007).
  • Numbers: 104 million people aged 60 and over in the 2011 Census (8.6%); projected to reach about 227 million (14.9%) by 2036, with more elderly than children under 15 by about 2046 (India Ageing Report, 2023).
  • Features of ageing in India: most elderly live in villages; more widows than widowers (feminisation of ageing); fast growth of the "oldest old" (80 and over); many in the poorest groups; most worked in the informal sector without pensions.

Social issues

  • Break-up of joint families and migration of children to cities or abroad; elders living alone or as couples.
  • Financial insecurity and dependence on children; few pensions.
  • Multiple chronic diseases, disability, poor access to health care and rehabilitation.
  • Loneliness, depression, loss of role after retirement or widowhood.
  • Elder abuse and neglect: physical, emotional, financial.
  • Unsafe housing (stairs, Indian toilets) and few elder-friendly public spaces.

2National policies, laws and schemes

National policies that facilitate the elderly (S18, 7 marks; W18, 3 marks); also part of the W19 and W20 long answers.

Policy or schemeMain points
National Policy on Older Persons (1999)State support for financial and food security, health care and nutrition, shelter, education, welfare, and protection of life and property against abuse and exploitation
Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act (2007)Children and heirs must maintain parents and senior citizens; claims through maintenance tribunals (allowance up to ₹10,000 a month); old age homes for indigent elders in districts; medical care; protection of life and property; abandoning a senior citizen is punishable
National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (2010)Geriatric clinics and wards at district hospitals, rehabilitation units and physiotherapy at CHCs, home-based care through PHCs and sub-centres, regional geriatric centres and national centres of ageing
Ayushman Arogya MandirsElderly and palliative care in the package of primary health care (see health care delivery)
AB PM-JAY for 70 and over (2024)₹5 lakh a year hospital cover for every person aged 70 and over, whatever their income (Ayushman Vay Vandana card)
Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension SchemeMonthly pension for elderly persons below the poverty line, topped up by states
Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana (2017)Free assistive devices (walking sticks, crutches, walkers, wheelchairs, hearing aids, spectacles, dentures) for poor senior citizens with age-related disability, through camps
Atal Vayo Abhyuday YojanaGrants to NGOs for old age homes, day care centres, mobile medical units; Elderline 14567 helpline
Other benefitsHigher income-tax exemption, savings schemes with higher interest, priority counters, reserved seats in buses

3NGOs in elderly care

Role of NGOs for elderly care in India (W19, 3 marks).

  • Examples: HelpAge India, Dignity Foundation, Agewell Foundation, Nightingales Medical Trust, Silver Innings, local religious and community trusts.
  • Roles: mobile medical units and health camps (eye, hearing, physiotherapy); old age homes, day care centres and senior citizens' clubs; helplines and protection from abuse; livelihood and pension help; sponsorship of destitute elders; awareness and advocacy for policy; training caregivers; disaster relief for elders; linking elders to government schemes.

4Institutionalised and community-dwelling elders

Should she be institutionalised or stay at home (S18, 4 marks); hospitalisation versus home management (S20, 5 marks); old age home versus own home with an attendant (W17, 4 marks).

OptionAdvantagesDisadvantages
Living at home (community-dwelling)Familiar surroundings, family and neighbours, independence and dignity, own routine, cheaper, keeps roles and social contactUnsafe home (stairs, toilet), loneliness if alone, no help in emergencies, carer burden, harder access to therapy
Old age home or care institutionSupervision and help with daily care, safer environment, regular meals and medicines, company of peers, quicker help in emergenciesLoss of independence and privacy, separation from family, routine set by the institution, cost, risk of depression and inactivity, variable quality
HospitalNeeded for acute illness, surgery and intensive therapyDeconditioning, delirium, infections, cost; not a place to live

How to decide: the person's wishes; medical stability; function (ADL, cognition, falls risk); safety and accessibility of the home; availability of family or a paid attendant; money; and community services (home physiotherapy, day care, helplines). The usual aim is home with support where safe, an institution when needs cannot be met safely at home.

5Communication barriers in the elderly

Communication barriers in elderly people: 7 marks in S20, W21 and W24.

BarrierExamplesWhat to do
HearingPresbycusis (high tones lost first), difficulty in noiseFace the person, quiet room, speak slowly and clearly in a lower pitch, do not shout, hearing aid in and working
VisionPoor near vision, cataract, glareGood light without glare, glasses on, large-print, high-contrast handouts
CognitionSlower processing, poor memory, dementia, deliriumShort sentences, one instruction at a time, repeat, demonstrate, written or picture reminders, involve family
Speech and languageAphasia or dysarthria after stroke, loose denturesYes/no questions, gestures, picture boards, patience
EmotionalDepression, anxiety, fear, embarrassment, low self-esteemBuild trust, listen, respect, privacy

Cultural and educationalDifferent language, low literacy, beliefs about illnessLocal language, simple words, culturally fitting examples

6Worked long case: alone on a fourth floor after hip surgery

From a real paper (S18, 5 + 6 + 4)

"A 65 years old, female, living alone on 4th floor in a chawl, underwent girdle stone arthoplasty of left hip joint. i) Explain the functional diagnosis ii) Discuss the long term goals with its relevant management (6) iii) Discuss giving reason if she should be institutionalised or continue to stay at home. (4)"

Spot the clues before you write

Girdlestone arthroplasty: the femoral head and neck are removed without a replacement, so the leg is shorter and the hip unstable; she will need a walking aid, a shoe raise and strong hip muscles. living alone: no help at home. 4th floor of a chawl: many stairs, often a shared toilet: major barriers.

One-line functional diagnosis

A 65-year-old woman living alone on a fourth floor after left Girdlestone arthroplasty, with limb shortening, hip weakness and instability, unable to manage stairs and outdoor walking safely without help.

Long-term goals and management (6 marks)

  • Goals (3–6 months): walk independently indoors and outdoors with a stick or walker and shoe raise; manage stairs with rail and aid; independent in self-care and toileting; no falls.
  • Strength: hip abductors, extensors and quadriceps, progressive, plus the other leg and arms for aids.
  • Gait: shoe raise for the shortening; walker progressing to a stick in the right hand; gait training to reduce the lurch.
  • Stairs: up with the good leg, down with the operated one, rail and stick; practise many times before discharge.
  • Balance and falls prevention; home advice: raised toilet seat or commode, grab bars, clear floors, phone within reach.
  • Endurance: walking programme; rest stops on landings.

Institution or home (4 marks)

  • For home: her familiar place, independence, neighbours in the chawl, lower cost; possible if she can manage stairs and toilet safely, with a helper, a meal service or neighbours checking daily, and home physiotherapy.
  • For an institution: four floors of stairs without a lift, a shared toilet, and living alone make falls and isolation likely in the early months; an institution or a family member's ground-floor home gives supervision.
  • Suggested answer: a short stay with family or in a care facility (or a ground-floor arrangement) during rehabilitation, then return home once stairs, toileting and outdoor walking are safe, with support and follow-up. Say that the final choice is hers.

Practice case (S20)

A 60-year-old retired executive with metabolic and heart disease after an Austin Moore prosthesis for a fractured neck of femur, in a joint family whose members cannot give time, with a domestic help and frequent admissions: functional diagnosis, short- and long-term goals, and hospitalisation versus home management (section 4; home management with a trained helper, home physiotherapy and early detection of deterioration usually wins unless he is medically unstable).

3 mRole of NGOs for elderly care in India. (W19)

Examples: HelpAge India, Dignity Foundation, Agewell. Roles: mobile medical units and camps, old age homes and day care, helplines and protection from abuse, livelihood and pension help, sponsorship of destitute elders, awareness and advocacy, caregiver training, linking to government schemes.

7 mNational policies that facilitate the elderly. (S18)

National Policy on Older Persons 1999 (financial and food security, health, shelter, welfare, protection); Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007 (maintenance tribunals, old age homes, medical care, penalty for abandonment); NPHCE 2010; PM-JAY for all aged 70 and over (2024); IGNOAPS pension; Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana; NGO grants and Elderline; tax and savings benefits. Add the physiotherapist's role in NPHCE units.

7 mCommunication barriers in the geriatric population. (W21, W24)

Barriers: hearing, vision, cognition, speech and language, emotional, physical, cultural and literacy, professional (jargon, haste, elderspeak), environmental. Strategies: face-to-face, quiet and well-lit room, slow clear speech, aids in place, short instructions, demonstration, written large-print handouts, teach-back, family involvement, respect and patience.
