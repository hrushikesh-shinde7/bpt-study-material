2(e)Labour and postnatal care: physiotherapy in labour, the puerperium, caesarean section and postnatal complications

Labour and postnatal care were asked in 14 of 20 papers, with 9 long answers. The physiotherapist's job is the same throughout: help the woman cope with labour without fear, then rebuild the pelvic floor, abdomen and back after birth while she learns to feed, lift and carry her baby without strain.

1Labour: stages and changes

Physical and physiological changes of labour and the puerperium: 10 marks in the S18 long answer.

Changes in labour

  • Uterus and cervix: contractions become stronger, longer and more frequent; the cervix thins (effacement) and opens (dilatation); the baby descends and rotates through the pelvis.
  • Heart and circulation: each contraction pushes blood back into the circulation, so cardiac output and blood pressure rise; pain and anxiety add to this.
  • Breathing: oxygen use rises; pain causes fast breathing and over-breathing (tingling, dizziness).
  • Other: stomach empties slowly; sweating, fluid loss and fatigue; the bladder is pressed and may not empty; the pelvic floor and perineum stretch greatly in the second stage.

2Physiotherapy in labour

Pain relief in labour (S17), breathing in labour (S19), PT during labour (S23), each 7 marks; positions in labour (W19, 3 marks); the second stage: positions, breathing and electrotherapy (S24, long answer).

  • Aims: reduce fear and pain, help her stay in control and mobile, help labour progress, save energy, protect the perineum, and keep the baby well oxygenated.
  • Antenatal preparation is where most of this is taught: what happens in labour, positions, breathing and relaxation practice, partner's role (see antenatal classes).

Positions

  • First stage: walking, standing and leaning forward on the partner or bed, sitting on a birthing ball, kneeling forward over a pillow or ball, all-fours (eases backache when the baby faces forward), side-lying for rest or with an epidural. Upright and mobile positions shortened the first stage by about 1 hour 22 minutes and reduced caesarean and epidural use (Lawrence et al., 2013).
  • Second stage: the position she chooses, including upright ones such as kneeling, squatting with support, all-fours and side-lying; avoid lying flat on the back, which presses on the great vessels and narrows the pelvic outlet (WHO, 2018).

Breathing

  • Early labour: slow, relaxed breathing through the contraction: a sigh out at the start, slow breaths in through the nose and out through the mouth, a sigh at the end.
  • Strong contractions: lighter, slightly faster breathing at the peak, returning to slow breathing as it fades.
  • Transition (urge to push before full dilatation): "pant-pant-blow" to avoid pushing too early.
  • Second stage: push with the natural urge, breathing out or with short breath-holds, rather than long forced breath-holding; pant as the head crowns so the perineum stretches slowly.
  • Avoid over-breathing: if she feels tingling or dizziness, slow down and breathe into cupped hands.
  • Why breathing helps: focuses attention, aids relaxation, keeps oxygen supply to mother and baby, and prevents exhausting breath-holding.

3The puerperium and the goals of postnatal care

Changes of the puerperium (S18); three goals of postnatal care (W25, 3 marks).

  • Puerperium: the 6 weeks after delivery while the body returns towards its non-pregnant state.
  • Uterus: involution: the fundus is at the umbilicus after delivery, shrinks by about a finger's breadth a day, is no longer felt above the pubis by about 2 weeks and is near normal size by 6 weeks; after-pains, stronger while feeding.
  • Lochia: red (first few days), then pink-brown, then yellow-white, lasting a few weeks.
  • Pelvic floor and perineum: stretched, bruised or torn; episiotomy heals in 1–2 weeks; leakage is common at first.
  • Abdomen: lax, weak muscles; diastasis recti in many women (see diastasis recti).
  • Joints: ligaments stay lax for months; back and pelvic girdle pain are common.
  • Circulation: blood volume and cardiac output fall back over weeks; blood clots more easily, so deep vein thrombosis risk stays raised for 6 weeks.
  • Breasts: colostrum, then milk "comes in" on about day 2–4; engorgement may follow.
  • Bladder and bowel: passing more urine in the first days; constipation and haemorrhoids are common.
  • Mind: joy, tiredness, sleep loss; baby blues in many.

Goals of postnatal physiotherapy

  1. Prevent complications: chest problems after anaesthesia, deep vein thrombosis, urinary retention, constipation.
  2. Relieve pain: perineum, back, pelvis, wound, breasts.
  3. Restore the pelvic floor (continence, support) and the abdominal wall.
  4. Teach posture and body mechanics for feeding, lifting and carrying the baby.
  5. Support emotional well-being, rest and family help.
  6. Return gradually to fitness and to work, and keep a lifelong pelvic floor habit.

4Postnatal exercise programme

Postnatal care after a normal delivery with episiotomy (S16) and for an executive who wants her fitness back after her second baby (W20).

WhenExercises and advice
Day 1 to the end of week 1Deep breathing; ankle pumps and leg exercises (clots); gentle pelvic floor contractions from the first day, which also ease perineal swelling; transversus abdominis drawing-in and pelvic tilts; getting out of bed by rolling to the side; walking; check for diastasis recti; feeding and lifting posture
Weeks 2–6Progress pelvic floor holds and fast contractions; bridging, side-lying leg lifts, four-point kneeling with arm and leg lifts; head lifts only if the abdominal gap does not dome; daily walks
6–12 weeksAfter the 6-week check: low-impact aerobic exercise (brisk walking, cycling, swimming once bleeding has stopped), resistance training for legs, back and arms; progress abdominal work
After 12 weeksRunning and high-impact exercise only when she passes load and impact tests without leaking, heaviness or pain (Goom et al., 2019); 150 minutes a week of activity

Care of the perineum and episiotomy

  • Ice pack wrapped in cloth for 10–15 minutes in the first 24–48 hours for swelling and pain.
  • Gentle pelvic floor contractions to pump away swelling.
  • Lie on the side to rest and feed in the first days; sit on a soft cushion and squeeze the pelvic floor before sitting down.
  • Hold a clean pad on the stitches when opening the bowels; avoid straining; fibre and fluids.
  • Keep the area clean and dry; report increasing pain, smell or discharge (infection).

5Caesarean section

Complications of caesarean section (S17, 3 marks); elective caesarean for cephalopelvic disproportion: management and home programme (S17, long answer).

  • Cephalopelvic disproportion: the baby's head is too big for the mother's pelvis, so a planned (elective) caesarean is done.
  • Complications: bleeding; infection of the wound or uterus; deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism; chest infection and atelectasis after anaesthesia; urinary retention or bladder injury; paralytic ileus and wind pain; wound breakdown, later incisional hernia, adhesions and chronic pain; spinal headache; delayed mobility and breastfeeding; in later pregnancies, scar rupture and abnormal placenta. Baby: fast breathing for a short time after birth.

Physiotherapy after caesarean section

  • Before surgery (elective): teach breathing exercises, supported huffing and coughing, bed mobility and leg exercises.
  • Day 0–1: deep breathing; huff or cough with a pillow pressed over the wound; ankle pumps and static quadriceps; roll to the side and push up to sit; walk early with good upright posture; gentle transversus and pelvic floor contractions (pregnancy itself loaded the pelvic floor).
  • Days 2–7: progress walking; posture; feeding positions that keep the baby off the wound (side-lying, football hold, pillow on the lap); relief of wind pain by walking, pelvic tilts and abdominal massage.
  • Weeks 2–6: progress abdominal and pelvic floor exercises; scar care once healed (gentle massage around the scar); no lifting heavier than the baby or straining for about 6 weeks; drive when she can do an emergency stop without pain.
  • Home programme: breathing and leg exercises daily in the first week, pelvic floor and transversus 3 times a day, walking a little more each day, posture and lifting advice, warning signs (calf pain, fever, wound discharge, breathlessness).

Low back pain after delivery (W18)

  • Differential diagnosis: mechanical (postural) low back pain from feeding, lifting and weak core; pelvic girdle pain (sacroiliac joint or symphysis pubis); lumbar disc prolapse with nerve root pain; coccydynia (pain on sitting, after a difficult birth); dragging back pain from pelvic floor weakness or prolapse; rarely infection or haematoma at the epidural site, or osteoporosis of pregnancy with vertebral fracture.
  • Red flags: fever, night pain, leg weakness or numbness, bladder or bowel change, saddle numbness: refer urgently.
  • Assessment: pain site and behaviour, delivery details (instruments, epidural, baby weight), feeding and lifting habits, posture, lumbar and SIJ tests (active straight leg raise, posterior pelvic pain provocation), neurological screen, diastasis check, pelvic floor assessment, function and mood.
  • Other impairments in this population: pelvic floor weakness (leakage, prolapse), diastasis recti, neck and upper back pain from feeding, wrist and thumb pain, fatigue and deconditioning, weight retention, low mood.

Breast engorgement (S24)

  • Definition: painful, swollen, hard breasts, usually 2–5 days after birth as milk comes in, from milk stasis plus swelling of the breast tissue.
  • Management: frequent, effective feeding (on demand, 8–12 times a day) with a correct latch; hand-express a little to soften the areola before latching; cold packs between feeds for swelling; gentle lymphatic drainage strokes towards the armpit, avoiding deep or forceful massage; a supportive, non-tight bra; pain relief as prescribed; refer if fever or a red, hot area appears (ABM protocol 36, 2022).

Postpartum blues (S22)

  • Short-lived tearfulness, mood swings, irritability, anxiety and poor sleep in about half or more of new mothers, starting 2–3 days after birth and settling within 2 weeks; due to the sudden fall in hormones, sleep loss and adjustment.
  • Management: reassurance that it is common, rest and sleep, family help with the baby, talking about feelings, gentle walks and relaxation.
  • If symptoms last beyond 2 weeks or are severe, think of postnatal depression: screen (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale) and refer; confusion or hallucinations mean postpartum psychosis, an emergency.

7Breastfeeding posture: ergonomics for a lactating mother

Ergonomic advice for a lactating mother (S21, 3 marks); poor feeding technique in the S26 long case.

  • Bring the baby to the breast, not the breast to the baby: a pillow on the lap raises the baby to breast height.
  • Sit in a chair with back support (a small cushion behind the low back), feet flat or on a low stool, shoulders relaxed, arm supported on a pillow or armrest.
  • Avoid long neck bending: look up and around between sucks; stretch the neck and upper back after feeds.
  • Change positions: cradle, cross-cradle, football (rugby) hold (good after caesarean), and side-lying (good at night and with a sore perineum).
  • Keep the wrists straight when holding the baby's head; support with the forearm.
  • Change nappies at waist height; lift the baby close with knees bent; carry in a carrier that holds the baby high and close; do not carry on one hip for long.
  • Sleep and rest when the baby sleeps; drink water during feeds.

3 mBreast engorgement and its management. (S24)

Painful swelling of both breasts 2–5 days after birth from milk stasis and tissue swelling. Management: frequent effective feeds with correct latch, hand expression to soften the areola, cold packs between feeds, gentle lymphatic strokes towards the axilla (no deep massage), supportive bra, analgesics; refer if fever or a red hot area (mastitis).

3 mPositions in labour. (W19)

First stage: walking, standing leaning forward, sitting on a ball, kneeling forward, all-fours, side-lying; upright positions shorten the first stage (about 1 hour 22 minutes) and reduce caesarean and epidural use. Second stage: her choice, including kneeling, supported squatting, all-fours, side-lying; avoid lying flat on the back.

7 mPhysiotherapy management of pain during labour. (S17)

Antenatal education to reduce fear; positions and mobility; breathing levels (slow, lighter at the peak, pant-pant-blow in transition, avoid over-breathing); relaxation (Mitchell, progressive); sacral massage and warm packs; TENS with electrodes at T10–L1 and S2–S4 and a boost button; warm water; continuous support; coordination with medical analgesia (positions with epidural).

LAQDiscuss the various complications that can be seen in the postnatal period and their management. (7 + 8, S23)

List with management: perineal pain (ice, pelvic floor, positioning), urinary retention and incontinence (mobility, PFMT), constipation and haemorrhoids (diet, posture), diastasis recti (TrA, avoid sit-ups while doming), back and pelvic girdle pain (posture, core, belt), DVT (prevention, urgent referral), chest complications after anaesthesia (breathing, huffing), wound problems after caesarean, breast engorgement and mastitis, de Quervain's, baby blues and depression (support, EPDS, referral). End with the home programme and 6-week review.
