6(b i)Ability management: the worker care spectrum, job analysis, ergonomic evaluation, REBA, injury prevention and employee fitness

Ability management was asked in 10 of 20 papers, always as short answers: task analysis, job demand analysis, ergonomic evaluation, REBA, cognitive ergonomics, workstation exercises, employee fitness. It is the "before injury" half of the worker care spectrum: understand the job, fit it to the worker, and keep the worker fit for it.

My revision

1The worker care spectrum

  • Pre-placement screening: after a job offer, the worker's health, fitness and functional abilities are matched with the job demands; it records a baseline, identifies needs for training or adaptations, and must not be used to exclude persons with disability unfairly (RPWD Act, 2016).
  • Ability management: keeping healthy workers healthy and productive by fitting the job to the worker and the worker to the job.

2Job analysis

Job analysis (S26) and job demand analysis (S21), 3 marks; importance of task analysis (S19, 7 marks).

PartWhat it records
Job descriptionJob title and purpose, main duties, tools and machines, materials, hours and shifts, breaks, workplace, supervision, pay system (piece rate or salary)
Job demand analysisThe physical, mental and environmental demands of the job and how often each occurs (below)
Task analysisEach task broken into steps, with posture, force, repetition, duration, tools and environment for each step, and the risk factors found

Job demand analysis

  • Physical: lifting, carrying, pushing and pulling (weights and distances); sitting, standing and walking time; bending, kneeling, squatting, crawling, climbing, reaching above shoulder; gripping, fine finger work, repetition; vibration.
  • Frequency (Dictionary of Occupational Titles): occasionally up to a third of the day, frequently a third to two-thirds, constantly more than two-thirds.
  • Mental and sensory: attention, memory, decisions, reading, vision, hearing, speed and deadlines.
  • Environment: heat, cold, noise, light, dust, fumes, heights, wet floors, shift work.
Physical demand levels
LevelLifting
SedentaryUp to 10 lb (about 4.5 kg) occasionally; mostly sitting
LightUp to 20 lb (9 kg) occasionally, 10 lb frequently
MediumUp to 50 lb (23 kg) occasionally, 25 lb (11 kg) frequently
HeavyUp to 100 lb (45 kg) occasionally, 50 lb frequently
Very heavyOver 100 lb occasionally, over 50 lb frequently

Importance of task analysis (S19)

  • Finds the exact steps and postures that cause pain or injury, so the fix is specific.
  • Basis for ergonomic redesign of tools, workstation and methods.
  • Matches the job to the worker in pre-placement and return to work (compare with the functional capacity evaluation).
  • Designs job-specific work conditioning and hardening (job simulation).
  • Shows what light duties are possible during recovery; guides job rotation.
  • Basis for training safe methods and setting fair production rates; supports compensation decisions.

3Ergonomics

Purpose and principles of ergonomics (S22), cognitive ergonomics (S21), define ergonomics (S23), 7 marks each.

  • Definition (International Ergonomics Association, 2000): "the scientific discipline concerned with the understanding of interactions among humans and other elements of a system", and the profession that applies theory and methods to design in order to optimise human well-being and overall system performance. Simply: fitting the job to the person.
DomainConcerned with
PhysicalAnatomy, anthropometry, physiology and biomechanics: postures, manual handling, repetition, musculoskeletal disorders, workplace layout
CognitiveMental processes (perception, memory, reasoning, motor response): mental workload, decision-making, skilled performance, human–computer interaction, human error, work stress, training
OrganisationalWork systems: shift design, teamwork, communication, work pace, participation

Purpose

  • Prevent work-related musculoskeletal disorders and accidents.
  • Reduce fatigue and errors; improve comfort, productivity and quality.
  • Include older workers and persons with disability by adapting work.

Principles

  1. Work in neutral postures (spine in its curves, elbows by the side, wrists straight, neck upright).
  2. Reduce excessive force (lighter loads, mechanical aids, good grips).
  3. Keep everything within easy reach; work at the right height (around elbow height; lower for heavy work, higher for fine work).
  4. Reduce excessive repetition and static loading; vary tasks.
  5. Minimise fatigue with rest breaks and job rotation.
  6. Minimise contact stress and vibration.
  7. Provide clearance and space to move; allow change of posture (sit–stand).
  8. Keep a comfortable environment (light, temperature, noise).
  9. Make displays and controls easy to understand (cognitive); involve workers in changes (participatory ergonomics).

Cognitive ergonomics (S21)

  • Mental workload: too much (overload: errors, stress) or too little (boredom, lapses); balance tasks and breaks.
  • Attention and alertness: monotonous monitoring, night shifts and fatigue reduce vigilance.
  • Display and control design: clear labels, logical layout, warnings that stand out, controls that move the expected way.
  • Human error: design so mistakes are hard to make and easy to correct (checklists, forcing functions).
  • Computer work: simple screens and software, readable fonts, fewer interruptions.
  • Training and decision support for complex tasks; stress management.
  • Examples: control rooms, drivers and pilots, surgeons, call centres, health-care workers.

4Ergonomic evaluation and REBA

Importance of ergonomic evaluation at the workplace (S16, 7 marks); ergonomic physiotherapy evaluation (S19, 3 marks); REBA (W19, 7 marks); one ergonomic scale (S23, 7 marks).

Ergonomic physiotherapy evaluation: steps

  1. Meet management and workers; review injury and absence records; symptom survey (Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire).
  2. Walk-through observation and video of the job; job and task analysis.
  3. Measure the workstation (heights, reaches, seat, screen), loads, forces, frequency and environment (light, noise, temperature).
  4. Score risk with a tool: RULA, REBA, NIOSH lifting equation, Strain Index (see risk assessment tools).
  5. Recommend changes from the hierarchy of controls: redesign, tools and aids, layout, breaks and rotation, training, exercise.
  6. Re-evaluate after changes.

Importance of ergonomic evaluation

  • Identifies risk factors before injury occurs; prevents musculoskeletal disorders, accidents and absence.
  • Improves comfort, productivity and quality; reduces compensation costs.
  • Gives objective scores to prioritise and justify changes, and to show improvement.
  • Helps return injured workers and include workers with disability.
  • Meets legal duties for a safe workplace.

Rapid Entire Body Assessment (REBA)

  • Developed by Hignett and McAtamney (2000) to score whole-body postures in tasks that are unpredictable or varied (health care, service, industry).
  • Group A: trunk, neck and legs, plus a load or force score → score A.
  • Group B: upper arm, lower arm and wrist, plus a coupling (grip) score → score B.
  • Scores A and B give score C from a table; an activity score is added (static posture over a minute, repeated small actions, or rapid large posture changes) → REBA score 1–15.
REBA scoreRiskAction
1NegligibleNone needed
2–3LowMay be needed
4–7MediumNeeded
8–10HighNeeded soon
11–15Very highNeeded now

RULA is the upper-limb version for seated and standing work with arms (computer, assembly), scored 1–7.

5Injury prevention, employee fitness and workplace health promotion

Employee fitness programme (W19), workplace health promotion (S18), workstation exercises (S20), 3 marks each; workstation exercises for a sedentary worker (S25, 7 marks).

Injury prevention

  • Ergonomic redesign first; then safe work methods, training in handling and tool use, PPE.
  • Early reporting of symptoms and early physiotherapy (stage 1 of a cumulative trauma disorder is reversible).
  • Warm-up before heavy shifts, micro-breaks, job rotation.

Employee fitness programme

  1. Needs assessment and management support; a committee with workers.
  2. Health risk appraisal and fitness tests (BP, BMI, waist, aerobic fitness, strength, flexibility).
  3. Programme: on-site exercise classes or gym, walking groups, stretching breaks, sports; education on back care, nutrition, weight, smoking, stress and sleep.
  4. Incentives and convenient timing; family involvement.
  5. Evaluate: participation, fitness changes, injuries, absence, productivity.

Benefits: fewer musculoskeletal injuries and sick days, better fitness, morale and productivity, lower health costs.

Workplace health promotion (S18)

  • Definition (Luxembourg Declaration, 1997): "the combined efforts of employers, employees and society to improve the health and well-being of people at work".
  • Works through better work organisation and environment, active participation of workers, and personal development.
  • Examples: ergonomic workplaces, fitness and health checks, healthy canteens, tobacco-free workplace, stress management, flexible hours.

Workstation exercises for a sedentary worker

3 mJob demand analysis. (S21)

A systematic record of the physical (lifting, carrying, postures, hand use), mental and sensory, and environmental demands of a job, with weights, distances and frequency (occasionally up to a third of the day, frequently a third to two-thirds, constantly more than two-thirds). Used for pre-placement, ergonomic change, FCE matching and return to work.

3 mEmployee fitness programme. (W19)

Workplace programme to improve workers' health and fitness: needs assessment, health risk appraisal and fitness testing, exercise classes, walking groups and stretch breaks, education (back care, nutrition, smoking, stress), incentives, evaluation of participation, injuries and absence. Benefits: fewer injuries and sick days, better productivity and morale.

7 mCognitive ergonomics. (S21)

Define ergonomics and its three domains. Cognitive ergonomics deals with perception, memory, reasoning and response: mental workload, attention and vigilance, display and control design, human error, human–computer interaction, decision support, training, work stress. Give examples (control rooms, drivers, surgeons, call centres) and design solutions (clear displays, checklists, breaks, task variety).

7 mImportance of task analysis. (S19)

Define task analysis within job analysis. Importance: identifies harmful steps and postures; basis for ergonomic redesign; matches worker to job in pre-placement and return to work; designs job simulation in work hardening; identifies light duties; guides rotation, training and production rates; supports compensation. Give an example (lifting bags into a truck: bending, twisting, 50 kg, 3 ft height).
