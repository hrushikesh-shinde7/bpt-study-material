2(d)Menopause and osteoporosis: hormonal and physiological changes, types and risk factors of osteoporosis, prevention and physiotherapy

Menopause and osteoporosis were asked in 8 of 20 papers, with 3 long answers. Every question follows from one fact: oestrogen falls. That speeds bone loss, weakens the pelvic floor and vaginal tissues, adds abdominal fat and raises heart risk, and the physiotherapist answers each with exercise, education and fall prevention.

My revision

1Menopause and its hormonal changes

Hormonal changes and sequelae of menopause (W24, 8 marks); physiological variations with menopause (S23, 7 marks).

  • Menopause: the permanent end of menstruation from loss of ovarian follicle activity, diagnosed after 12 months without a period. Indian studies report an average age of about 46 years, earlier than the about 51 years usual in Western women.
  • Perimenopause: the years of irregular cycles and symptoms before and the first year after the last period. Postmenopause: the years after. Premature menopause: before 40; surgical menopause: after removal of both ovaries (sudden symptoms).
  • Hormonal changes: oestrogen (oestradiol) and progesterone fall; inhibin falls, so FSH rises markedly and LH rises; androgens become relatively higher.
SystemEffect of low oestrogen
VasomotorHot flushes, night sweats, palpitations, poor sleep
MindMood swings, irritability, anxiety, low mood, poor concentration
BoneRapid bone loss in the first years after menopause, osteoporosis and fractures (wrist, spine, hip)
Muscles and jointsLoss of muscle mass and strength, joint aches and stiffness
GenitourinaryVaginal dryness and painful intercourse, urgency and frequency, urinary infections, stress incontinence, prolapse (weaker pelvic floor and tissues)
Heart and metabolismWorse cholesterol profile, more abdominal fat, insulin resistance, higher blood pressure and heart disease risk
Skin and othersThin dry skin, hair changes, weight gain

Medical treatment: hormone therapy for troublesome symptoms in suitable women (doctor's decision), vaginal oestrogen for genitourinary symptoms, and bone-protecting drugs when fracture risk is high.

3Osteoporosis: definition and types

Types of osteoporosis (S23, 3 marks).

  • Definition (WHO, 1994): a systemic skeletal disease characterised by low bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration of bone tissue, with a consequent increase in bone fragility and susceptibility to fracture.
  • Diagnosis: bone mineral density by DEXA at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score (standard deviations from the young adult mean).
TypeFeatures
Primary type I (postmenopausal)Women about 50–70; oestrogen loss; mainly trabecular bone; vertebral crush and distal radius (Colles') fractures
Primary type II (senile, age-related)Men and women over about 70; trabecular and cortical bone; hip and vertebral fractures
Idiopathic (juvenile)Children and young adults without a known cause; rare
SecondarySteroids, thyroid and parathyroid excess, rheumatoid arthritis, malabsorption, chronic kidney or liver disease, alcohol, long immobilisation, some drugs (anticonvulsants, heparin)
RegionalDisuse after paralysis or a plaster; complex regional pain syndrome

4Risk factors and prevention

Predisposing factors (W22) and risk factors (S26) for osteoporosis; prevention of osteoporosis with ageing (W18). All 3 marks.

Risk factorsExamples
Cannot changeFemale sex, older age, early menopause, family history of hip fracture, small thin build, previous fragility fracture, Asian or white ethnicity
Can changePhysical inactivity, low body weight, low calcium and vitamin D (little sunlight), smoking, alcohol, excess caffeine and salt
Diseases and drugsLong-term steroids, rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid or parathyroid excess, diabetes, malabsorption, kidney disease, immobility

Prevention

  • Build peak bone mass in childhood and youth: sport, jumping and running, enough calcium and protein.
  • Exercise throughout life: weight-bearing and impact activity, progressive resistance training, balance training.
  • Nutrition: calcium from milk, curd, ragi, sesame, green leafy vegetables; vitamin D from sunlight and supplements if deficient; enough protein.
  • Lifestyle: stop smoking, limit alcohol and caffeine, keep a healthy weight.
  • Prevent falls: balance training, home safety, vision and drug review.
  • Screen and treat: DEXA for women at risk; medicines (bisphosphonates and others) when fracture risk is high.

5Exercise and physiotherapy for osteoporosis

Exercise programme with rationale for a 75-year-old with osteoporosis and knee pain (S25, 10 marks).

  • A multicomponent programme with progressive resistance training and balance training is recommended; aerobic exercise alone is not enough (Too Fit to Fracture, 2014).
  • After a vertebral fracture, exercise should be planned with a physiotherapist.
ComponentExerciseRationale
ResistanceSquats or sit-to-stand, step-ups, hip and knee strengthening, rows, wall push-ups, back extensors in prone or sitting; 2–3 days a week, 8–12 repetitions, progressing loadMuscle pull on bone stimulates bone formation; strength for function and falls
Impact (if no fracture and joints allow)Heel drops, stamping, marching, stair climbing, brisk walking; low impact firstLoading through the skeleton maintains bone
BalanceTandem and single-leg stance, stepping, tai chi; most daysPrevents the falls that cause fractures
PostureChin tucks, scapular retraction, thoracic extension, back extensor strengtheningReduces kyphosis and vertebral load
AerobicWalking, cycling (alongside, not instead of, the above)Fitness and heart health

Spine-sparing advice

  • Avoid repeated or loaded forward bending and twisting of the spine (toe touches, sit-ups, lifting with a rounded back).
  • Bend at the hips and knees (hip hinge) to pick things up; carry loads close; roll to the side to get out of bed.
  • Avoid high-risk activities for falls; use rails and good lighting.

6Worked long case: menopausal symptoms with rib and back pain

From a real paper (W18)

"A 45 years old, female, 56 kg, 5'6 ht, complains of hot flushes, night sweats, urinary incontinence and mood swings since 3 months. She also complains of rib pain and low back pain while getting up from the bed. Explain the functional diagnosis, physiotherapy assessment and management for the same."

Spot the clues before you write

hot flushes, night sweats, mood swings at 45: perimenopause. urinary incontinence: weak pelvic floor and tissues. rib and low back pain on getting up from bed: possible osteoporotic vertebral or rib fracture; needs DEXA and an X-ray. 56 kg, 5'6": BMI about 20, a thin build, a risk factor for osteoporosis.

One-line functional diagnosis

A 45-year-old perimenopausal woman with vasomotor symptoms, stress or mixed incontinence and mechanical back and rib pain with suspected osteoporosis, limiting getting out of bed, lifting and sleep.

Assessment

  • History: menstrual history, symptoms and their timing, falls, fractures, steroid use, diet and calcium, sunlight, smoking, activity, family history; type of leakage.
  • Red flags: sudden severe back pain, height loss, night pain, weight loss (refer for X-ray, DEXA, blood tests).
  • Examination: posture (kyphosis, height), spinal tenderness and range, rib springing gently (avoid forceful tests), strength of back extensors and hips, balance (single-leg stance, TUG), pelvic floor (PERFECT), BMI.
  • Questionnaires: menopause symptom scale, ICIQ for leakage, mood screen.

Management

ProblemTechnique and doseWhy
Rib and back painHeat, TENS, gentle mobility; log-roll to get out of bed; avoid bending and twisting under loadRelieves pain and protects a possibly fragile spine
Bone lossProgressive resistance (legs, hips, back extensors) 2–3 times a week; weight-bearing walking daily; impact only after fracture is excludedLoading maintains bone
PostureThoracic extension, chin tucks, scapular retraction, 10 × 2 dailyLess kyphosis and vertebral load
IncontinencePelvic floor training, at least 8 contractions 3 times a day; the knack; bladder training if urgencyFirst-line treatment
Hot flushes, sleep, moodAerobic exercise 150 minutes a week, relaxation and slow breathing, yoga; cool bedroom, light clothingBetter sleep, mood and well-being
Risk factorsCalcium-rich diet, vitamin D and sunlight, no smoking, little alcohol; balance training and home safetyPrevents fractures

Practice case (S25)

A 75-year-old woman with osteoporosis on calcium, bilateral knee pain and difficulty on stairs (evaluation 5, exercise programme with rationale 10): evaluate knee OA and falls risk (TUG, chair stand, balance), posture and fracture history; programme from section 5 with knee-friendly loading (sit-to-stand from a higher chair, step-ups on a low step, quadriceps work), balance training, back extensors, walking, stair training with a rail, and spine-sparing advice.

3 mTypes of osteoporosis. (S23)

Primary type I (postmenopausal, about 50–70, trabecular bone, vertebral and wrist fractures); primary type II (senile, over 70, trabecular and cortical, hip and vertebral fractures); idiopathic juvenile; secondary (steroids, endocrine, rheumatoid arthritis, malabsorption, alcohol, immobilisation, drugs); regional (disuse, CRPS).

3 mRisk factors for osteoporosis. (S26)

Non-modifiable: female, age, early menopause, family history of hip fracture, small build, previous fragility fracture. Modifiable: inactivity, low weight, low calcium and vitamin D, smoking, alcohol, caffeine. Disease and drugs: steroids, RA, thyroid or parathyroid excess, malabsorption, immobility.

7 mPhysiological variations associated with menopause. (S23)

Definition and stages; hormonal changes (oestrogen and progesterone down, FSH and LH up); effects: vasomotor, mood and sleep, bone loss, muscle and joints, genitourinary and pelvic floor, heart and metabolism, skin and weight. Add one line on PT for each.
