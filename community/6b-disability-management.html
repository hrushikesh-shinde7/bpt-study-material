6(b ii)Disability management: acute care, functional capacity evaluation, work conditioning, work hardening and job simulation

Disability management was asked in 10 of 20 papers: functional capacity evaluation five times, work conditioning and work hardening six times, and two long cases asking for both. Remember the order: treat the injury, measure what the worker can do (FCE), compare it with what the job needs (job analysis), and close the gap with work conditioning and then work hardening.

My revision

1Disability management and acute care

  • Disability management: the "after injury" half of the worker care spectrum (see the diagram on ability management): a planned process by the employer, worker and health team to limit the effect of an injury or illness on work and bring the worker back safely and early.
  • Steps: early reporting → acute care → case management and communication with the employer → functional capacity evaluation → work conditioning → work hardening → modified or transitional duties → full return to work → prevention of recurrence.

Acute care

  • Diagnosis and medical treatment; pain and swelling control (rest from aggravating tasks, ice, elevation, medication, electrotherapy).
  • Early, graded movement and exercise; avoid long bed rest and deconditioning.
  • Education and reassurance; set an expected return-to-work date early.
  • Contact with the employer about light or modified duties; keep the worker connected to the workplace.
  • Screen for "yellow flags" (fear of movement, low mood, job dissatisfaction) that delay recovery.

2Functional capacity evaluation

Functional capacity evaluation or assessment: 7 marks in W16, S17 and S19; define it for 3 marks in W22 and W25; the W20 long case.

  • Definition: a standardised set of tests that measures a person's safe maximum ability to perform work-related physical tasks, and compares it with the demands of a job or a physical demand level.
  • Uses: return-to-work decisions and job matching; planning work conditioning and hardening; deciding on modified duties; disability rating and compensation; pre-placement screening; measuring progress.

Components

PartWhat is tested
IntakeHistory, job description, symptoms, self-rated abilities, medical clearance, consent
Musculoskeletal screenPosture, range of motion, strength, sensation, heart rate and BP
Material handlingLifting floor to waist, waist to shoulder and overhead; carrying; pushing and pulling, with weights increased until a safe maximum
Positional tolerancesSitting, standing, walking, stooping, kneeling, crouching, reaching, climbing stairs and ladders, balance
Hand functionGrip (dynamometer), pinch, dexterity tests
Aerobic capacityStep or treadmill test
Effort and consistencyRepeated trials, heart rate response, observed body mechanics, consistency between tests
ReportSafe abilities, physical demand level, comparison with the job, projection to an 8-hour day, recommendations
  • Approaches: the kinesiophysical approach (Isernhagen Work System, 28 work-related tests) watches body mechanics to decide the safe maximum; psychophysical approaches use the worker's own judgement of an acceptable load.
  • Duration: usually several hours, often over 1–2 days.
  • Limits: affected by pain, fear and motivation; predicts return to work only modestly; needs a trained evaluator and standard protocol.

3Work conditioning and work hardening

Work conditioning (S17, S22), definitions of both (W21, W22), 3 marks; work hardening with two examples (W18, 7 marks).

FeatureWork conditioningWork hardening
DefinitionAn intensive, work-related, goal-oriented conditioning programme to restore systemic, neuromusculoskeletal and cardiopulmonary function (strength, endurance, mobility, motor control, aerobic capacity) so the person can return to workA highly structured, goal-oriented, individualised, interdisciplinary programme using real or simulated work tasks to restore physical, behavioural and vocational functions for return to a specific job
TeamUsually one discipline (physiotherapist)Interdisciplinary: physiotherapist, occupational therapist, psychologist, vocational counsellor, case manager
FocusPhysical capacity: general strength, flexibility, endurance, aerobic fitnessJob-specific tasks, productivity, safety, work tolerance, work behaviours (punctuality, pacing, attitude), pain coping
HoursPart of a day, several days a weekBuilds up towards a full working day, several days a week
EntryAfter acute care, when there is a physical deficitWhen physical capacity is nearly enough but job tolerance, confidence or behaviour limit return

Work conditioning: content

  • Aerobic training (treadmill, cycle, walking) 20–40 minutes.
  • Progressive strengthening of the injured area and the whole body (trunk, legs, grip).
  • Flexibility and mobility; motor control and core stability.
  • Basic body mechanics and lifting practice; education.

Work hardening: content

  • Job simulation: real or simulated tasks from the job analysis, graded in weight, height, distance, repetitions and time.
  • Physical conditioning continued alongside.
  • Body mechanics and pacing during the tasks; ergonomic changes for the real job.
  • Pain management, stress and fear-of-movement counselling; work habits (timekeeping, following instructions).
  • Regular re-measurement against the job demands; plan for graded return.

Two examples of work hardening (W18)

  • Warehouse loader after low back strain: lifting crates from floor to waist to shoulder with gradually heavier loads (for example 10 kg to 25 kg), carrying 20 m, pushing a loaded trolley, loading a mock truck bed, building up to the full shift's number of lifts with correct technique.
  • Data-entry clerk after carpal tunnel release or wrist fracture: graded typing and mouse sessions from 15 to 60 minutes, filing and lifting files, dexterity tasks, workstation set-up and micro-breaks, building up to a full working day.

Job simulation (S16, 3 marks)

  • Reproducing the real tasks of a job (same tools, weights, heights, postures and pace) in the clinic or at a mock workstation.
  • Used to train (work hardening) and to test whether the worker can meet the job demands (FCE).
  • Examples: work simulators with interchangeable tool handles, work sample batteries (such as Valpar work samples), mock construction, assembly or office stations.

4Return to work

  • Options: same job full duty; same job with changes (tools, layout, hours); modified or light duty for a period (transitional work); a different job with the same employer; retraining (see vocational rehabilitation).
  • Graded return: start with part days and lighter tasks, increase weekly as tolerance improves.
  • Prevention of recurrence: ergonomic changes, home exercise, fitness, early reporting (see ability management).

5Worked long case: a sugarcane worker with neck and arm pain

From a real paper (W18)

"A 32 years old, male, 65 kg, 5'8 ft, working in sugar cane industry, complains of neck arm pain since 6 months. Write the job task analysis. Describe the work hardening and work conditioning program for him."

Spot the clues before you write

sugarcane industry: cutting with a sickle, carrying bundles on the head or shoulder, loading, or feeding cane and lifting sugar bags in the mill. neck and arm pain for 6 months: cervical overload with possible nerve root irritation; persistent, so deconditioning and fear are likely. 32, 65 kg: young, light build carrying heavy loads.

Job task analysis

TaskDemandsRisk to neck and arm
Cutting caneStooping or squatting for hours, repetitive forceful sickle strokes, heatSustained neck flexion, repetitive shoulder and elbow loading
Bundling and carryingHeavy bundles on the head or shoulder, carried over uneven fieldsAxial compression of the cervical spine, one-sided shoulder load
Loading trucks or cartsLifting bundles above shoulder height, twisting, climbingOverhead lifting with neck extension
Mill work (if in the factory)Feeding cane into crushers, lifting sugar bags, vibration, noise, heat, shiftsRepetitive lifting, awkward reach
Work organisationPiece rate, long days in the harvesting season, few breaksFatigue, no recovery

Functional diagnosis in one line: a 32-year-old sugarcane worker with 6 months of mechanical neck pain with arm referral (rule out cervical radiculopathy) from head and shoulder loading, stooped cutting and overhead loading, with reduced neck mobility, weak deep neck and scapular muscles and low work tolerance, unable to manage full harvesting or loading work.

Assessment before the programme

  • Neck range, neurological screen (myotomes, reflexes, sensation, Spurling's and upper limb tension tests), strength of deep neck flexors, scapular and grip muscles; pain scale and Neck Disability Index.
  • Functional capacity evaluation: lifting floor to waist and waist to shoulder, carrying, overhead work tolerance, repetitive arm work, stooping tolerance; compare with the job demands above (very heavy work).

Work conditioning (weeks 1–3)

ImpairmentExercise and doseWhy
Neck stiffness and painNeck and thoracic mobility, nerve gliding if arm symptoms, 10 repetitions twice a day; heat or TENS before exercise if neededRestores movement, calms nerve symptoms
Weak neck and scapular musclesDeep neck flexor training, isometrics in all directions, scapular retraction and lower trapezius with bands, 3 × 10, progressing to resisted shoulder workSupports the neck under load
Low general capacityAerobic exercise 20–30 minutes; trunk, hip and leg strengthening; grip strengtheningBuilds endurance for long harvest days
Poor lifting mechanicsHip-hinge and squat lifting practice with light loadsMoves load from neck and back to the legs

Work hardening (weeks 3–8)

  • Job simulation: stooped cutting motions with a weighted handle; lifting and carrying bundles of graded weight (10 kg rising towards his job load) with correct technique; loading onto a raised platform; repeated for longer periods towards a full shift.
  • Load carriage advice: avoid head-loading; carry on both shoulders with a padded yoke or use a cart or wheelbarrow; share loads; keep loads close.
  • Pacing: micro-breaks every 30–60 minutes with neck and shoulder stretches; drink water in the heat.
  • Behaviour and confidence: pain education, graded exposure to feared tasks, punctuality and full-day attendance.
  • Return to work: lighter tasks first (cutting without carrying), then full work; discuss with the contractor or employer about load size and carts.

Practice case (W20)

A 54-year-old production supervisor in a garment factory, a month after surgery for multiple fractures from a road accident: plan the functional capacity assessment (section 2: intake, musculoskeletal screen, lifting and carrying within fracture healing limits, walking and stairs, standing and sitting tolerance, hand function, aerobic test) and then work conditioning (strength, mobility, endurance for the operated limbs and whole body) and work hardening (supervising rounds on the factory floor, standing and walking for hours, climbing stairs, carrying files and samples, computer work), building to full days.

Test yourself

Answer on paper first, then open the answer.

3 mDefine work hardening and work conditioning. (W21, W22)

Work conditioning: an intensive, work-related, goal-oriented programme, usually by one discipline, to restore strength, endurance, mobility, motor control and aerobic capacity for return to work. Work hardening: a highly structured, individualised, interdisciplinary programme using real or simulated work tasks to restore physical, behavioural and vocational functions for a specific job.

3 mJob simulation. (S16)

Reproducing the actual tasks of a job (tools, weights, heights, postures, pace) in the clinic or a mock workstation, to train job-specific tolerance in work hardening and to test job readiness in an FCE. Examples: work simulators, work sample batteries, mock loading or assembly stations.

7 mFunctional capacity evaluation. (W16, S17, S19)

Definition; uses (return to work, job matching, programme planning, compensation, pre-placement); components (intake, musculoskeletal screen, lifting floor-waist-shoulder and carrying, pushing and pulling, positional tolerances, hand function, aerobic test, effort and consistency, report with physical demand level and 8-hour projection); approaches (kinesiophysical, Isernhagen; psychophysical); limits.

LAQ54-year-old production supervisor, a month after fracture fixation from a road accident: functional capacity assessment, work conditioning and work hardening. (W20)

Job analysis of a supervisor (standing and walking rounds, stairs, carrying files, computer work). FCA: intake and medical clearance, fracture healing limits, ROM and strength, walking and stair tolerance, standing and sitting tolerance, carrying and lifting within limits, hand function, aerobic test, report. Work conditioning: progressive strengthening, mobility, gait and balance, aerobic training. Work hardening: simulated floor rounds, stair climbing with files, desk work periods, building to full days; graded return with modified duties first.

MCQWork hardening differs from work conditioning mainly because it:A. Uses only aerobic exerciseB. Is interdisciplinary and uses real or simulated job tasksC. Is done in the acute stageD. Does not involve the employer

MCQThe Isernhagen Work System is a method of:A. Ergonomic posture scoringB. Functional capacity evaluationC. Noise measurementD. Vocational counselling
