My revision

1What rehabilitation is

Define rehabilitation: 3 marks in S20 and the first 2 marks of the S23 long answer.

Definition (WHO)

Rehabilitation is "a set of interventions designed to optimize functioning and reduce disability in individuals with health conditions in interaction with their environment".

  • Older WHO wording (still quoted in Indian textbooks): all measures that reduce the impact of disabling and handicapping conditions and enable disabled people to achieve social integration.
  • Aim: the highest possible independence in daily life, school, work and community, with the least restriction.
  • Kinds of rehabilitation: medical (treatment, therapy, aids), vocational (work), social (family and community life), educational, and psychological.

Types by where it is given (syllabus 4b)

TypeWhat it means
Institution-based (IBR)Hospitals, rehabilitation centres and special schools with specialist staff and equipment; the person comes to the centre
Outreach (reach-out)Staff from the institution go out to the community: camps, mobile vans, home visits, satellite clinics
Community-based (CBR)Rehabilitation within the person's own community, run with the person, the family and local workers, using local resources, with referral to institutions when needed (see CBR)

2Institution-based vs community-based rehabilitation

Asked in W16, W21, S23 (10 marks), S24, W24 and S25. Write the table, then two lines on why they complement each other.

CBR definition (ILO, UNESCO and WHO, 2004)

"CBR is a strategy within general community development for the rehabilitation, equalization of opportunities, poverty reduction and social inclusion of all people with disabilities." It is carried out "through the combined efforts of people with disabilities themselves, their families, organizations and communities, and the relevant governmental and non-governmental health, education, vocational, social and other services".

FeatureIBRCBR
PlaceHospital, rehabilitation centre, special school, mostly in citiesHome, village, school, workplace
Who deliversSpecialists: doctors, therapists, prosthetists, psychologistsCBR workers, family, ASHA and anganwadi workers, teachers, volunteers, supported by professionals
ApproachMedical model: treats the impairmentSocial and rights model: health, education, livelihood, social life and empowerment
DecisionsMade by professionals; the person is a patientMade with the person and family, who are partners
CostHigh (buildings, equipment, staff, travel and stay for the family)Low; uses local materials and existing services
CoverageFew people, mainly urban and those who can travelMany people, including rural and poor families
TechnologyAdvanced equipment, investigations and surgerySimple, local, appropriate (aids made from bamboo or wood, home adaptations)
Skills learntIn an artificial setting; may not carry over to homeIn the real home and village, so they carry over
ContinuityEnds at discharge; follow-up is hardOngoing, sustained by the community
Social inclusionMay separate the person from family and communityKeeps the person in the family; changes community attitudes
Best forAcute and complex care: surgery, intensive therapy, fitting of prostheses and orthoses, training of staffLong-term needs, common disabilities, prevention, school and work inclusion

Conclusion line for the exam: they are not rivals. CBR handles most needs locally and refers complex cases to institutions; institutions train CBR workers and provide specialist care and back-up. Outreach programmes link the two.

3The rehabilitation team

Role of the occupational therapist (S26), the social worker (W20, S21, S26) and team effort in CBR (S23).

MemberMain role
Doctor (physiatrist)Diagnosis, medical and surgical care, prognosis, disability certification, leads the team in a hospital
PhysiotherapistMovement and function: assessment, exercise, mobility and gait training, pain relief, chest care, aids and their training, home programmes, prevention
Occupational therapistSee below
Speech therapist and audiologistSpeech, language and swallowing therapy; hearing tests and hearing aids; alternative communication
Prosthetist and orthotistMeasures, makes, fits and repairs artificial limbs, callipers, splints and spinal braces
Clinical psychologistCognitive and IQ testing, counselling for adjustment, depression and anxiety, behaviour therapy, family counselling
Vocational counsellorAssesses work ability and interests, guides to training and suitable jobs, helps placement (section 5)
Social workerSee below

The occupational therapist (S26)

  • Assesses and trains activities of daily living: feeding, dressing, bathing, toileting, cooking, using money and transport.
  • Hand function: fine motor training, hand splints, sensory re-education.
  • Adaptive devices (built-up spoons, button hooks, reachers, sock aids) and home, school and workplace modification.
  • Cognitive and perceptual retraining (memory, attention, neglect) after brain injury and stroke.
  • Pre-vocational and work skills, wheelchair seating, energy conservation, play and sensory integration in children.

The medical social worker (W20, S21; in CBR, S26)

  • Psychosocial assessment: family, income, housing, education, work, community support.
  • Counselling the person and family to accept the disability and take part in rehabilitation.
  • Linking to benefits: disability certificate and UDID card, pensions, travel concessions, free aids and appliances (ADIP), scholarships, loans for self-employment (see policies).
  • Arranging financial help through hospital funds, government schemes and NGOs.
  • Discharge planning, home and community resources, return to school or work, follow-up.
  • Advocacy against discrimination and abuse.
  • In CBR: community survey to identify persons with disability, awareness meetings, forming self-help groups and CBR committees, linking families to schemes, livelihood and education, mobilising local resources.

How a team works

  • Multidisciplinary: each professional assesses and treats separately with their own goals.
  • Interdisciplinary: professionals meet, share findings and set common goals with the person.
  • Transdisciplinary: one key worker (often the CBR worker or family) delivers the programme, trained and supported by the others; the usual model in CBR.

Why team effort matters in CBR (S23)

  • Disability affects health, education, work and social life, so no single worker can meet all needs.
  • Shared goals avoid duplication and conflicting advice; scarce professionals train and support many local workers.
  • Referral in both directions between village and institution; continuity of care.
  • The community owns the programme, which makes it cheaper and sustainable; the person and family are empowered.

4Vocational rehabilitation, guidance and counselling

Vocational rehabilitation (W19, S21, 3 marks); significance of vocational guidance and counselling (W17, S23, 7 marks).

Purpose (ILO Convention 159, 1983)

"To enable a disabled person to secure, retain and advance in suitable employment and thereby to further such person's integration or reintegration into society."

  1. Vocational evaluation: medical and physical capacity (functional capacity evaluation), education, skills, interests, aptitude, work history, local job market.
  2. Vocational guidance and counselling: choose a realistic goal that matches ability and interest.
  3. Vocational training: skill training in industrial training institutes, Vocational Rehabilitation Centres for Handicapped (Ministry of Labour and Employment), NGOs; work conditioning and work hardening (see disability management).
  4. Placement: return to the old job with changes, open employment (4% of government posts reserved under the RPWD Act, 2016), sheltered workshop, supported employment, or self-employment with concessional loans (National Divyangjan Finance and Development Corporation).
  5. Follow-up to solve problems at work and keep the job.

Significance of vocational guidance and counselling

  • Matches the job to the person's remaining abilities, so training is not wasted and failure is avoided.
  • Builds motivation, self-esteem and acceptance of disability; reduces depression.
  • Leads to earning and economic independence; reduces dependence on family and state.
  • Gives a social role and dignity, the final aim of rehabilitation (participation).
  • Guides the family and employer; identifies adaptations, aids and job modifications.
  • Informs about schemes, reservation, loans and training centres.

5ADL training and the disabled hand

Training for activities of daily living with examples, and management of the disabled hand (both W17, 7 marks).

ADL training

  • Basic ADL: feeding, grooming, bathing, dressing, toileting, transfers, walking or wheelchair mobility. Instrumental ADL: cooking, shopping, housework, money, phone, transport.
  • Steps: assess (Barthel Index, FIM); set goals with the person; analyse the task and break it into steps; teach step by step (forward or backward chaining), in the real setting and at the usual time; use the easiest method first and fade help; add aids and change the environment; train the family; review.
  • Examples: hemiplegia: dress the affected arm first and undress it last, sit to dress, elastic laces; paraplegia: sliding-board transfer to the toilet, long-handled mirror for skin checks; rheumatoid hands: built-up handles, lever taps; low vision: tactile marks on the stove.
  • Principles of energy conservation and joint protection for long-term conditions.

The disabled hand

  • Assess: cause (nerve injury, burns, stroke, rheumatoid arthritis, leprosy, amputation), range, strength (grip and pinch with a dynamometer), sensation, deformity, prehension patterns and function in tasks.
  • Treat: pain and oedema control (elevation, retrograde massage), mobilisation and stretching, strengthening, splints (resting, functional and corrective; for example cock-up splint in radial nerve palsy, knuckle-bender in ulnar claw hand), sensory re-education and protection of anaesthetic hands, prehension and dexterity training (pegboards, picking up objects of different sizes).
  • Adapt: built-up and lever handles, universal cuff, one-handed techniques, tools for work; vocational adjustment.

6NGOs, the Spastics Society of India and the Red Cross

Role of NGOs: S16, W17, S23 and S25 (3 marks) and S19 (7 marks); Spastics Society of India (S18); Red Cross (S20, W21).

  • NGO: a voluntary, non-profit organisation run independently of the government, registered as a society or trust. Examples: ADAPT (formerly the Spastics Society of India), Indian Red Cross Society, HelpAge India, Bhagwan Mahaveer Viklang Sahayata Samiti (Jaipur foot), Amar Seva Sangam, Mobility India, CBM.
  • Role in rehabilitation and community welfare:
    • Surveys and early identification; awareness to reduce stigma.
    • Services where government services do not reach: therapy, camps, aids and appliances, special and inclusive schools.
    • Training CBR workers, parents and volunteers.
    • Vocational training, self-help groups and livelihood.
    • Advocacy for rights, accessibility and policy; linking people to government schemes.
    • Raising funds; research; disaster relief.
    • Flexible and close to the community, so they often pilot new programmes that government later adopts.

Spastics Society of India (now ADAPT)

  • Started on 2 October 1972 in Mumbai by Dr Mithu Alur to provide education and treatment for children with cerebral palsy; now called ADAPT (Able Disabled All People Together).
  • Functions: special and inclusive education; therapy (physiotherapy, occupational and speech therapy); training courses for teachers and therapists; vocational training and placement of young adults; early intervention; advocacy for inclusive education; outreach in the slums and villages; research and publications.

Red Cross Society

  • The Red Cross movement began with Henry Dunant after the battle of Solferino; the International Committee was founded in Geneva in 1863. The Indian Red Cross Society was set up by an Act of 1920; the President of India is its president.
  • Seven principles: humanity, impartiality, neutrality, independence, voluntary service, unity, universality.
  • Functions: disaster preparedness and relief; blood donation and blood banks; first aid training; ambulance and hospital services; health education and maternal and child welfare; help for war victims and tracing of missing family members; care of the sick and disabled; Junior and Youth Red Cross in schools and colleges.
